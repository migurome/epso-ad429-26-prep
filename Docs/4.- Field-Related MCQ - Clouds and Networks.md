# 4. Succeeding in the Clouds & Networks Field-Related MCQ Test

The Field-Related Multiple Choice Test is the second hurdle in the EPSO/AD/429/26 (4) competition, and the first one that is specific to the Clouds & Networks field rather than to general cognitive aptitude. Where the reasoning tests of Phase 1 measure how you think, this test measures what you know: your professional command of the concepts, trade-offs and vocabulary a cloud and network engineer works with day to day, at the level expected of an AD7 administrator designing, securing and operating the cloud infrastructure and networks that support the European institutions' IT systems.

Format, in brief: **30 questions, 40 minutes, scored 0–30, pass mark 15/30**. Passing the mark is necessary but not sufficient — because this stage is classifying rather than purely eliminatory, EPSO only carries forward the best-scoring candidates in the Clouds & Networks field, up to a multiple of the number of posts available (166 posts in this field, per the competition notice). In practice this means 15/30 is a floor to clear comfortably, not the actual target; your real goal is a score that holds up well against your competitor pool. The test is taken in your **Language 2** (typically English, French or German), not your strongest language, which adds a layer of terminology pressure on top of the technical content itself.

Unlike the verbal reasoning test discussed in the previous chapter, there is **no passage of text** to analyse here: each item is a self-contained question drawing on your own subject-matter knowledge of cloud computing and networking. This changes the shape of your preparation substantially — it is less about technique and more about making sure your working knowledge of a defined set of topic areas is accurate, current, and precise enough to withstand deliberately similar-sounding wrong answers.

In the following, we will cover:

- How field-related MCQ items are typically designed, and the shapes wrong answers tend to take

- A short list of free resources for further study

## How These Questions Are Designed

Well-constructed technical MCQ items rarely rely on obscure trivia or vendor-specific configuration syntax — that would be unfair across such a broad field, and easy to get wrong even for an expert. Instead, they test whether you can tell apart two concepts that are genuinely easy to confuse under time pressure. Recognising the typical shapes wrong answers take will save you time and help you avoid the traps set for the unwary:

- **The reversed pair.** Two related concepts (IDS/IPS, overlay/underlay, RBAC/ABAC) are swapped, so the distractor describes concept B correctly but attaches concept A's name to it.
- **The half-truth.** A statement that starts out correct and then quietly introduces a false or overreaching claim (for example, "a VPN encrypts traffic in transit, so no other network security controls are needed").
- **The outdated or oversimplified best practice.** A claim that used to be, or sounds like it should be, good practice, but that mainstream consensus has since qualified or superseded (for example, treating a hardened network perimeter as sufficient protection on its own, without a zero trust layer for internal traffic).
- **The category error.** An answer that correctly describes a real concept, just not the one the question actually asked about (for example, describing NFV when asked about SDN).
- **The common misconception.** A statement that "feels" right because it is a widely repeated but technically incorrect interpretation (the classic conflation of "the cloud provider secures everything" with the actual, narrower scope of the shared responsibility model).

When you are unsure between two options, ask yourself: which one names the actual mechanism or root cause, and which one only sounds plausible? The correct answer in a well-designed item is nearly always the one that is both technically precise and addresses the real underlying issue, not just a symptom of it.

## 1. Cloud Service and Deployment Models

This area starts from the vocabulary every cloud professional is expected to use precisely: the **NIST definition of cloud computing** (SP 800-145), which characterises cloud computing by five essential characteristics — on-demand self-service, broad network access, resource pooling, rapid elasticity, and measured service — and formalises the now-standard **three service models**: Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS), Platform as a Service (PaaS) and Software as a Service (SaaS). The most tested distinction here is exactly how much of the technology stack the customer versus the provider manages under each model: in IaaS the customer manages the operating system upward while the provider handles the physical infrastructure; in PaaS the provider additionally manages the OS and runtime, leaving the customer only the application and its data; in SaaS the provider manages the entire stack and the customer simply configures and uses the finished application.

This gradient of responsibility is formalised as the **shared responsibility model**: the cloud provider is responsible for the security "of" the cloud (physical infrastructure, virtualization layer, and — depending on the service model — more of the stack above it), while the customer remains responsible for security "in" the cloud — critically, the customer is always responsible for its own data, identity configuration and access management, no matter which service model is chosen. A common misconception is treating "the provider secures everything" as a blanket statement rather than a division that shifts, but never disappears, with the service model.

The four **deployment models** — private, public, community and hybrid — describe who owns and who can use the infrastructure, and are often confused with the separate concept of **multi-cloud** (using two or more public cloud providers, whether or not they are integrated) versus **hybrid cloud** (deliberately integrating private/on-premises infrastructure with public cloud resources, often for regulatory reasons or to support demand-driven "cloud bursting"). Private cloud trades the elasticity and pooled economics of public cloud for exclusive control and customisation — a trade-off institutions handling sensitive data weigh constantly.

## 2. Networking Fundamentals

Two competing reference models organise how networking concepts are discussed, and confusing them is a classic trap. The **OSI model** divides network communication into seven layers — Physical, Data Link, Network, Transport, Session, Presentation and Application — each with a distinct responsibility; a switch operates primarily at Layer 2 (forwarding frames by MAC address), while a router operates primarily at Layer 3 (forwarding packets by IP address). The **TCP/IP model**, which actually underlies the real-world Internet, is coarser: it collapses OSI's Physical and Data Link layers into a single Network Interface (or Link) layer, and its Session, Presentation and Application layers into a single Application layer, leaving four layers in total (Network Interface, Internet, Transport, Application). The OSI model remains a valuable teaching and troubleshooting tool even though no production network implements it literally.

**Subnetting and CIDR notation** are the practical mechanics of IP addressing: a CIDR suffix such as /24 states how many leading bits of an address are fixed as the network portion, leaving the remaining bits for host addresses — a /24 network has 256 total addresses (254 usable once the network and broadcast addresses are excluded), while a smaller suffix like /16 yields a much larger address space. Getting comfortable converting between prefix length, subnet mask and usable host count is a recurring practical skill.

**Routing versus switching** is the other foundational pair: switching moves traffic within a single network segment based on physical (MAC) addressing, while routing moves traffic between different networks or subnets based on logical (IP) addressing and a routing table. Finally, **DNS resolution** is the process — root servers, then top-level-domain servers, then the domain's authoritative name server — by which a human-readable hostname is translated into the IP address a client actually needs to open a connection, with results cached along the way to avoid repeating the full lookup chain.

## 3. Network Security and Perimeter Defence

This area probes whether you understand not just what a security control does, but where its protection actually stops. A **stateful firewall** tracks the state of active connections — recognising, for instance, that inbound traffic is a legitimate reply to an outbound request it already permitted — and filters primarily on IP address, port and protocol. A **next-generation firewall (NGFW)** builds on this with application-layer awareness: inspecting payload content, identifying the actual application or user behind a connection, and often integrating intrusion prevention and TLS inspection, typically reaching up to Layer 7 rather than stopping at Layer 4.

**VPNs** split into two use cases that are easy to conflate: a **site-to-site VPN** creates a persistent encrypted tunnel between two networks — for example, an on-premises data centre and a cloud VPC — so that entire networks can communicate as if local; a **remote-access VPN** instead connects an individual device or user to a network. Neither, by itself, inspects payloads or enforces least-privilege access once traffic arrives — a VPN protects confidentiality and integrity in transit, not what happens afterwards, which is why it is normally paired with other controls rather than relied on alone.

The most consequential distinction in this area is **zero trust versus perimeter-based security**. A traditional perimeter ("castle-and-moat") model concentrates defences at the network edge and trusts traffic relatively freely once inside; zero trust instead assumes no implicit trust based on network location alone, so every request — internal or external — is authenticated, authorised and continuously verified. Finally, **IDS versus IPS** hinges on placement and action: an IDS typically sits out-of-band, monitoring a copy of traffic and alerting on suspicious activity without altering it, while an IPS sits inline and can actively block or drop malicious traffic in real time.

## 4. Cloud-Native Architecture and Containers

Cloud-native design starts with **containers versus virtual machines**. A VM virtualises hardware through a hypervisor and runs a complete, independent guest operating system on top of it, giving strong isolation at the cost of overhead and slower startup; a container instead packages an application with its dependencies while sharing the host operating system's kernel, making it far lighter and faster to start, though with a thinner isolation boundary than a full VM.

**Kubernetes** is the de facto orchestration layer for containers, and three of its objects are tested particularly often. A **Pod** is the smallest deployable unit — one or more containers that share a network namespace and storage and are always scheduled together onto the same node. Because Pods are ephemeral (created, destroyed and rescheduled with new IPs at any time), a **Service** provides a stable virtual IP address or DNS name that load-balances traffic across the current, healthy set of matching Pods, decoupling clients from any individual Pod's lifecycle. A **Deployment** sits a level above both: it declaratively manages a set of replica Pods, handling rolling updates and rollbacks so the running state converges on the desired one.

At the application-architecture level, **microservices versus monolithic** design is a genuine trade-off, not a one-directional improvement: microservices allow independent deployability and per-service technology choices, but introduce distributed-systems complexity a monolith never has to face — network latency and partial failure between services, harder end-to-end testing and observability, and the need to manage data consistency across separately owned services.

Finally, a **service mesh** (such as Istio or Linkerd) addresses a problem specific to microservices at scale: rather than each service reimplementing retries, timeouts, mutual TLS and observability, a mesh provides this consistently via sidecar proxies deployed alongside every service, handling service-to-service ("east–west") traffic — a distinct concern from an API gateway, which manages traffic entering the cluster from external ("north–south") clients.

## 5. Software-Defined Networking and Network Virtualization

**Software-Defined Networking (SDN)** is defined by a single architectural move: separating the **control plane** (which decides how traffic should be forwarded) from the **data plane** (which actually forwards it). Traditionally, each switch and router runs both planes locally and independently; SDN centralises control-plane logic in a software controller that can programmatically manage forwarding behaviour across many devices at once — typically via a protocol such as OpenFlow — enabling centralised, consistent, and far more agile network management than reconfiguring each device by hand.

A closely related but distinct pair is **overlay versus underlay networks**. The underlay is the physical infrastructure — the actual routers, switches and cabling — that provides basic IP connectivity. An overlay is a logical network built on top of that physical infrastructure, typically using encapsulation, allowing virtual topologies to be created independently of how the physical network is actually wired. **VXLAN (Virtual Extensible LAN)** is the most commonly tested overlay technology: it encapsulates Layer 2 Ethernet frames inside Layer 3 UDP packets so that Layer 2 segments can be stretched across an IP network, and — critically — it uses a 24-bit identifier (the VNI), supporting millions of isolated virtual segments where traditional 802.1Q VLAN tagging is capped at roughly 4,000.

**Network Functions Virtualization (NFV)** is frequently, and wrongly, treated as a synonym for SDN — a classic category error. NFV is about replacing dedicated hardware network appliances (physical firewalls, load balancers, routers) with software-based virtual network functions running on standard servers; SDN is about separating and centralising control of how traffic flows. The two are complementary and often deployed together in modern network architectures, but they solve different problems: NFV virtualises *what* performs a network function, while SDN changes *who decides* how traffic is forwarded.

## 6. Cloud Migration, Cost Management and FinOps

Migration questions test whether you can match a strategy to its actual constraints rather than reciting labels. The **"6 R's" of cloud migration** are: **Rehost** ("lift and shift" — moving an application to the cloud largely unchanged, usually the fastest path but the one that captures the fewest cloud-native benefits); **Replatform** ("lift, tinker and shift" — making a handful of targeted optimisations, such as swapping a self-managed database for a managed one, without redesigning the core architecture); **Refactor / re-architect** (redesigning the application to be genuinely cloud-native, for example decomposing a monolith into microservices); **Repurchase** ("drop and shop" — replacing the existing application with a different commercial product, often a SaaS offering, rather than migrating the codebase at all); **Retire** (decommissioning an application that is no longer needed); and **Retain** (deliberately keeping an application where it currently runs, often for compliance reasons or because it was too recently invested in to justify migrating yet).

Once workloads are in the cloud, **pricing model** choice becomes a cost-management lever in its own right: **on-demand** pricing charges the highest unit rate but requires no commitment, suiting unpredictable or short-lived workloads; **reserved** pricing trades a one- to three-year commitment for a substantial discount on predictable, steady-state workloads; **spot** pricing offers the deepest discounts precisely because the provider can reclaim that capacity on short notice, making it suitable only for fault-tolerant, interruption-tolerant workloads.

**FinOps** formalises cost management as a cross-functional, iterative practice — commonly framed as Inform, Optimize and Operate phases — that makes cloud spend a shared responsibility across finance, engineering and business stakeholders, rather than a topic engineering is kept walled off from. **Cost-allocation tagging** (by cost centre, project, environment, and so on) is what makes this practice operationally possible: it is what lets spend actually be attributed to the right owner for chargeback, budget accountability and follow-up.

## 7. High Availability, Load Balancing and Resilience in the Cloud

Load balancing algorithms decide how incoming requests are distributed across a pool of servers, and two are tested particularly often. **Round robin** cycles through servers in sequence, sending each new request to the next server in the list regardless of how busy it currently is — simple, but blind to uneven load. **Least connections** instead routes each new request to whichever server currently has the fewest active connections, adapting to real-time load and handling unevenly-sized or long-running requests far better than a fixed rotation.

**Auto-scaling** automates capacity to track demand: horizontal auto-scaling adjusts the number of running instances in response to a live metric (CPU utilisation, request count, queue depth), so capacity grows and shrinks with load instead of sitting permanently over- or under-provisioned.

The most consequential resilience distinction is **multi-AZ versus multi-region deployment**. Availability Zones within a single region are physically separate data centres connected by low-latency links, so deploying across multiple AZs protects against the failure of any single data centre while still allowing practical synchronous replication. Deploying across multiple *regions* goes further, protecting against a region-wide event (a natural disaster, a large-scale regional outage) and potentially reducing latency for users spread across different geographies — but at the cost of materially higher inter-region latency and the added complexity of managing data replication and consistency across regions. Neither replaces the other; many resilient architectures use both.

Finally, **chaos engineering** — deliberately injecting controlled failures into a system, such as terminating instances or introducing artificial network latency — exists to surface hidden dependencies, missing fallbacks and incorrect assumptions about failure handling proactively, under conditions the team controls, rather than waiting for those same weaknesses to surface as an uncontrolled incident in production. It is a complement to monitoring and testing, not a replacement for either.

## 8. Cloud Governance, Compliance and Identity & Access Management

Access control in the cloud starts from the **principle of least privilege**: users, roles and automated workloads alike should be granted only the minimum permissions required for their specific function, which limits the blast radius of a compromised credential or a simple mistake — the opposite of granting broad standing access "to avoid delays."

The mechanism for enforcing this at scale is where **RBAC versus ABAC** becomes the key tested distinction. Role-based access control (RBAC) assigns permissions to predefined roles and maps users to one or more of those roles — simple to reason about, but coarse-grained, since every meaningful combination of conditions needs its own role. Attribute-based access control (ABAC) instead evaluates a broader set of attributes — of the user, the resource, and the context, such as time of day, location or data sensitivity — against policies at request time, enabling more fine-grained, dynamic access decisions without an explosion of roles.

**Compliance frameworks** provide the external structure this internal control operates within. **ISO/IEC 27001** is not a technical product certification; it is a risk-based management-system standard specifying how to establish, implement, maintain and continually improve an Information Security Management System (ISMS) — a process and governance standard, not a checklist of specific technologies. For an EU institution, **GDPR-aligned data residency** is a recurring practical concern in cloud architecture: where personal data is physically stored and processed determines which legal safeguards apply, and moving personal data outside the EEA generally requires an adequate legal basis, which is why cloud region selection is a compliance decision as much as a latency or cost one.

Finally, **policy-as-code / guardrails** operationalise governance preventively: encoding rules (such as "no publicly readable storage buckets" or "resources must be tagged") as automated, version-controlled, testable policies enforced at resource-creation time, so non-compliant configurations are blocked before they exist rather than caught later by manual review or audit.
