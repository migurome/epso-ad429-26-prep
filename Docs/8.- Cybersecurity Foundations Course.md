# 8. Cybersecurity Foundations — A Course

This is the study track, not the exam track.

The rest of the Cybersecurity section prepares you for one specific test: 30 questions in 40 minutes on the duties listed in Annex II of EPSO/AD/430/26. It assumes you already know the field and only need to sharpen the distinctions an examiner is likely to probe. This course sits underneath that. It builds the subject from first principles, in the order a textbook would, so that the exam material rests on something rather than floating.

**Where it comes from.** The syllabus follows *Computer Security: Principles and Practice* (Stallings & Brown, Pearson, 4th edition), the standard university text for this subject, and each module says which chapters it corresponds to so you can go to the source when you want the full treatment. The text here is written from scratch: it explains the same material, selects what matters for an EU institution's security work, and connects it to the legal and operational layer the book predates — NIS2 and the Cyber Resilience Act, the institutions' own regime under Regulation 2023/2841 and CERT-EU, zero trust, threat-informed defence with MITRE ATT&CK, risk-based vulnerability management, DevSecOps, attacks on machine-learning systems, and the post-quantum migration. The book itself is a commercial publication and is not part of this repository.

**How the two fit together.** The book's strength is the technical foundation: how cryptography actually works, why a buffer overflow happens, what an intrusion detector is really measuring. Its blind spot, being from 2018, is everything the last few years added. The exam's field-related MCQ tests both, so the honest preparation is to learn the foundation properly and layer the current practice on top.

**How to use it.** Work a module at a time, in order for the first five and in any order after that — the later modules depend on the vocabulary of the earlier ones, not the reverse. Read the module, then answer its ten questions without looking back. When you miss one, the useful question is not "what was the answer" but "which distinction did I not have". Each module ends with the handful of distinctions that generate most of the questions in that area; if you can state them in your own words, the module is done.

**A warning about depth.** An AD 8 post is a senior technical role, and the test is written for people who have done the work. Reading about buffer overflows does not give you the intuition of someone who has debugged one. Where a module marks something as worth practising rather than reading, that is a real recommendation.

# 9. Course Modules

## Module 1 — Security concepts, threats and design principles

*Corresponds to chapter 1 of the reference book.*

### The three properties, and why there are five

Computer security is conventionally defined by the **CIA triad**: **confidentiality** (information is not disclosed to unauthorised parties), **integrity** (information and systems are modified only in authorised ways), and **availability** (systems work promptly and service is not denied to authorised users). NIST's formal definition adds a decomposition worth knowing: confidentiality covers both *data confidentiality* and *privacy*, the individual's control over what is collected about them and to whom it is disclosed; integrity covers both *data integrity* and *system integrity*, the assurance that a system performs its function unimpaired.

Two further properties are usually added because the triad does not capture them. **Authenticity** is the property of being genuine and verifiable — that a message really came from the source it claims. **Accountability** is the requirement that actions be traceable to the party responsible, which is what makes after-the-fact investigation, intrusion detection and legal recourse possible. Note that accountability is a design requirement, not an outcome: a system that does not log cannot be made accountable retrospectively.

The three levels of impact defined in FIPS 199 — **low**, **moderate** and **high** — describe the effect of a breach on organisational operations, assets or individuals, and they are the reason that "how bad would this be" is a question with an official vocabulary rather than an improvised one.

### Threat, attack, vulnerability, asset

The words are not interchangeable and the exam knows it. A **vulnerability** is a weakness in a system: it may be *corrupted* (loss of integrity), *leaky* (loss of confidentiality), or *unavailable/slow* (loss of availability). A **threat** is a potential for a security violation — a circumstance or capability that could breach security and cause harm. An **attack** is a threat carried out: an intelligent act that deliberately attempts to evade security services. A **countermeasure** reduces the risk, by preventing the attack, detecting it, or enabling recovery — and countermeasures may themselves introduce new vulnerabilities, which is why adding controls is not monotonically safe.

Attacks divide by the attacker's relationship to the flow of information. A **passive attack** attempts to learn or make use of information without affecting system resources: eavesdropping on message contents, or traffic analysis, which extracts meaning from the pattern of communication even when the contents are encrypted. Passive attacks are hard to detect and are therefore countered by prevention (encryption) rather than detection. An **active attack** attempts to alter resources or affect operation: replay, masquerade, modification of messages, denial of service. Active attacks are hard to prevent absolutely and are therefore countered by detection and recovery. That asymmetry — prevent the passive, detect the active — is a genuinely useful design heuristic.

Attacks also divide by origin. An **inside attack** is initiated by an entity inside the security perimeter, authorised to access resources but using them in a way not approved. An **outside attack** comes from outside that perimeter. The insider is the harder problem precisely because authorisation is not the issue.

### The design principles

The book lists thirteen fundamental design principles, the first eight of which come from Saltzer and Schroeder's 1975 paper and have survived fifty years of practice:

- **Economy of mechanism** — keep the design as simple and small as possible; complexity is where exploitable flaws hide, and simple mechanisms are easier to verify.
- **Fail-safe defaults** — base access decisions on permission rather than exclusion. The default is no access; the scheme names the conditions under which access is granted. A failure then denies rather than permits.
- **Complete mediation** — check every access against the access-control mechanism, every time. Caching a permission decision and reusing it is where this principle is usually broken.
- **Open design** — the design should be open rather than secret. Security must rest on the key, not on the obscurity of the algorithm.
- **Separation of privilege** — require more than one condition to grant access. Multi-factor authentication and two-person control are instances.
- **Least privilege** — every process and user operates with the minimum set of privileges necessary, for the shortest time necessary.
- **Least common mechanism** — minimise functions shared by different users, because shared mechanisms are paths for unintended information flow.
- **Psychological acceptability** — security mechanisms must not unreasonably obstruct the work. Controls people route around are worse than controls that are slightly weaker but used.
- **Isolation** — keep public access systems separate from critical resources; keep users' processes and files separate from one another; separate security mechanisms from the rest.
- **Encapsulation** — a specific form of isolation based on hiding internal structures behind a defined interface.
- **Modularity** — build security functions as separate, protected modules, and use common modules rather than reimplementing.
- **Layering** — use multiple, overlapping protections so that no single failure is fatal. This is defence in depth stated as a design rule.
- **Least astonishment** — the mechanism should behave as the user expects. A control whose behaviour surprises people will be misused.

These are not historical curiosities. "Secure by design" and "secure by default", now legal requirements under the Cyber Resilience Act, are fail-safe defaults and economy of mechanism given statutory force.

### Attack surfaces and attack trees

An **attack surface** is the set of reachable and exploitable vulnerabilities in a system: open ports and the code listening on them, services reachable inside the firewall, code that parses incoming data, interfaces and web forms, and staff susceptible to social engineering. It divides into a **network** attack surface, a **software** attack surface, and a **human** attack surface. The point of naming it is that a surface can be measured and then reduced — closing a port, removing a parser, dropping a feature — and that reduction is a security gain even when no specific vulnerability is known.

An **attack tree** is a hierarchical decomposition of the ways to reach an attack goal. The root is the goal; each branch is a way to achieve the node above it; leaves are the concrete actions available to an attacker. Its value is that it forces exhaustiveness — it makes you enumerate the paths you had not considered — and that it supports reasoning about which defence closes the most branches.

### Machine-learning systems as an attack surface

A trained model is an asset with an attack surface the book does not describe, and it belongs here because these attacks are best understood as the ordinary threat categories above applied to a new kind of asset. What makes them distinctive is that they target the *learning*, not the code around it, so the application-security controls of Module 8 do not address them.

- **Poisoning** corrupts the training data or the training process so that the deployed model is wrong in a way the attacker chose — a backdoor that fires on a specific trigger, or a general degradation. It is an integrity attack, and it happens *before* deployment, which is why the defence is provenance and validation of training data rather than anything done at inference time.
- **Evasion** crafts an input that the model misclassifies at inference time: the adversarial example. Neither the model nor its data is touched; only the input is manipulated.
- **Model inversion** reconstructs characteristics of the training data from the model's outputs. **Membership inference** answers the narrower and often more damaging question of whether one specific record was in the training set. Both are confidentiality attacks against the *data*, and both matter under the GDPR, because a model trained on personal data can leak it without anyone ever copying the file.
- **Model extraction** rebuilds an equivalent model by querying the original enough times, defeating the confidentiality of the model itself as intellectual property.
- **Prompt injection** affects systems built on large language models: instructions hidden in content the model reads are executed as though they came from the operator. It is the injection class of Module 8 in a setting where data and instructions travel the same channel by design, which is why filtering is a mitigation and not a fix.

The defender's equivalent catalogue is **MITRE ATLAS**, the ATT&CK-style knowledge base for attacks on machine-learning systems, and the legal layer is the **AI Act**, which imposes accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity requirements on high-risk systems.

### The distinctions that generate questions

- Vulnerability vs. threat vs. attack vs. risk: a weakness, a potential, an act, an expected loss.
- Passive vs. active attack, and the prevention/detection asymmetry that follows.
- Fail-safe defaults vs. complete mediation: the first is what happens when nothing says yes; the second is that the question is asked every time.
- Least privilege vs. separation of privilege: minimum rights vs. more than one condition.
- Attack surface vs. attack tree: what is reachable vs. how it could be reached.
- Poisoning vs. evasion: training-time integrity vs. inference-time misclassification.
- Model inversion vs. membership inference: reconstructing the data vs. proving one record was used.

## Module 2 — Cryptographic tools

*Corresponds to chapter 2 of the reference book; chapters 20 and 21 for the algorithms, covered in Module 3.*

### Symmetric encryption and what it does not give you

Symmetric encryption has five ingredients: plaintext, an encryption algorithm, a secret key shared by both parties, ciphertext, and a decryption algorithm. Its security rests on two requirements: the algorithm must be strong enough that an opponent with ciphertext cannot recover the key or plaintext, and the key must be kept secret. Notably, it does *not* rest on the algorithm being secret — that is the open-design principle, and it is why publicly analysed algorithms are trusted over proprietary ones.

Attacks divide into **cryptanalysis**, which exploits the structure of the algorithm or characteristics of the plaintext, and **brute force**, which tries every key. Brute force is what key length defends against, and key length alone tells you nothing about resistance to cryptanalysis.

The critical limitation: symmetric encryption gives **confidentiality only**. It does not, on its own, tell you who sent the message or whether it was altered. A recipient who decrypts successfully learns that the sender knew the key, which is weaker than it sounds when the key is shared by many parties.

### Message authentication and hash functions

**Message authentication** verifies that a message has not been altered and that its source is genuine. Three mechanisms, and the exam confuses them deliberately:

A **message authentication code (MAC)** is a fixed-size tag computed from the message and a shared secret key. The recipient recomputes it and compares. A MAC gives integrity and origin authentication between parties who share the key — but because both parties can produce it, it gives no **non-repudiation**: neither can prove to a third party which of them created the message.

A **hash function** maps a message of any length to a fixed-length digest, with no key. On its own it gives no security at all against an active attacker, who can alter the message and recompute the hash. It becomes useful when combined with a secret (HMAC) or with a private key (a digital signature). The properties required of a cryptographic hash are **preimage resistance** (given a digest, you cannot find a message that produces it), **second preimage resistance** (given a message, you cannot find a different one with the same digest), and **collision resistance** (you cannot find any two messages with the same digest). Collision resistance is the strongest and the first to fall: MD5 and SHA-1 are broken for collisions and must not be used where an adversary controls any part of the input.

A **digital signature** is produced with the signer's private key and verified with the corresponding public key. It gives integrity, authentication of origin, and non-repudiation, because only the holder of the private key could have produced it. Note the direction carefully: signing uses the *private* key, verification uses the *public* one. Encryption for confidentiality goes the other way — encrypt with the recipient's public key, decrypt with their private key. Reversing either is the single most common distractor in this whole area.

### Public-key cryptography

A public-key scheme has six ingredients: plaintext, algorithm, a public key and a private key forming a matched pair, ciphertext, and the decryption algorithm. The essential property is that it is computationally infeasible to derive the private key from the public key, so the public key can be published.

Public-key cryptography solves the problem symmetric cryptography cannot solve alone — establishing a shared secret between parties who have never met — but it is orders of magnitude slower. Real protocols are therefore **hybrid**: public-key operations establish a session key, and symmetric encryption carries the bulk data. TLS works exactly this way, and understanding that split explains most of what a TLS handshake is doing.

### Key management, and where cryptography actually fails

In practice, cryptographic systems rarely fail because the mathematics was broken. They fail at key management: keys hard-coded in source, shared by email, never rotated, never revoked, or generated from a weak source of randomness.

**Random and pseudorandom numbers** deserve specific attention because they are invisible when wrong. Keys, nonces and initialisation vectors must be unpredictable. A pseudorandom generator is deterministic given its seed, so its output is only as unpredictable as the seed; a **true random number generator** draws on a physical source. Systems that seeded from the clock, or from a process ID, have produced predictable keys at scale.

Key management covers generation, distribution, storage, rotation, escrow, revocation and destruction. Keys belong in a hardware security module or a managed key service, never in a repository. **Perfect forward secrecy** — using ephemeral keys so that compromise of a long-term key does not expose past sessions — is why TLS 1.3 removed static RSA key exchange entirely.

### Encryption of stored data

Encrypting data at rest protects against theft of the medium: a stolen disk, a discarded backup, a seized server. It does not protect against an attacker who has compromised the running application, because the application must be able to decrypt in order to work. Stating what a control protects against, rather than that it "protects the data", is the difference between an answer and a slogan.

### The distinctions that generate questions

- Confidentiality vs. integrity vs. authentication vs. non-repudiation, and which mechanism provides which.
- MAC vs. hash vs. digital signature — key/no key, and repudiable/non-repudiable.
- Which key signs and which key verifies; which key encrypts and which decrypts.
- Symmetric vs. asymmetric: speed, key distribution, and why real systems are hybrid.
- Preimage vs. collision resistance, and why a broken hash is broken for signatures before it is broken for anything else.
- Encryption at rest vs. encryption in transit vs. encryption in use: three different threat models.

## Module 3 — Cryptographic algorithms

*Corresponds to chapters 20 and 21 of the reference book.*

### Symmetric algorithms

A **block cipher** processes fixed-size blocks; a **stream cipher** produces a keystream combined with the plaintext bit by bit. **DES** used a 56-bit key and is now trivially brute-forced; **triple DES** extended its life at a heavy performance cost; **AES** replaced both, with a 128-bit block and keys of 128, 192 or 256 bits, and is the current standard. RC4, the best-known stream cipher, is broken and prohibited in TLS.

**Modes of operation** determine how a block cipher handles messages longer than one block, and the choice matters more than candidates expect. **ECB** encrypts each block independently and therefore leaks structure — identical plaintext blocks produce identical ciphertext blocks, which is why the famous encrypted-penguin image is still recognisable. ECB is essentially never correct. **CBC** chains blocks so that identical plaintext produces different ciphertext, at the cost of requiring a unique, unpredictable initialisation vector and being vulnerable to padding-oracle attacks when integrity is not checked. **CTR** turns a block cipher into a stream cipher and parallelises well, but reusing a counter with the same key is catastrophic. **GCM** adds authentication to counter mode, producing **authenticated encryption**: confidentiality and integrity in one construction. Modern practice is to use an AEAD mode — AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305 — and never to hand-roll encrypt-then-MAC.

**Key distribution** is the problem symmetric cryptography cannot solve internally: for *n* parties who must all communicate pairwise, the number of keys grows as *n(n−1)/2*. That combinatorial explosion is the practical reason public-key cryptography exists.

### Hash functions and MACs

**SHA-2** (SHA-256, SHA-384, SHA-512) is the current workhorse; **SHA-3**, based on a different construction (a sponge rather than Merkle–Damgård), exists as a hedge in case SHA-2 falls. **HMAC** is the standard construction for turning a hash function into a MAC, and it is not simply hashing the key concatenated with the message: the nested construction exists specifically to resist length-extension attacks that the naive approach permits.

**Authenticated encryption** combines confidentiality and integrity. The ordering matters: *encrypt-then-MAC* is the construction with the strongest security proof, and designs that MAC the plaintext and then encrypt have produced real vulnerabilities.

### Public-key algorithms

**RSA** rests on the difficulty of factoring the product of two large primes. Its keys must be much longer than symmetric keys for comparable strength — 2048 bits or more against AES's 128 — and it is slow, which is why it is used for key transport and signatures rather than bulk data.

**Diffie–Hellman** is a key *agreement* protocol, not an encryption algorithm: two parties derive a shared secret over a public channel without ever transmitting it. Plain Diffie–Hellman is vulnerable to a man-in-the-middle attack because neither party authenticates the other — which is precisely why it is always used with authentication, and why "DH provides authentication" is a distractor.

**Elliptic-curve cryptography** achieves equivalent strength with much shorter keys, which matters for constrained devices and for handshake size.

### Post-quantum, which the book predates

**Shor's algorithm** on a sufficiently large quantum computer breaks factoring and discrete logarithms, which removes the basis of RSA, Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography. **Grover's algorithm** gives only a quadratic speedup against symmetric primitives, so the response there is larger keys and digests — AES-256, SHA-384 — rather than replacement. The urgency comes from **harvest now, decrypt later**: traffic captured today is decrypted when the capability arrives, so data with a long confidentiality horizon is already exposed. The standardised replacements are **ML-KEM** for key encapsulation and **ML-DSA** and **SLH-DSA** for signatures, deployed in hybrid mode alongside a classical algorithm. The first practical step is an inventory of where cryptography is used, with what algorithms, key sizes and lifetimes — **crypto-agility** is the actual objective.

### The distinctions that generate questions

- ECB vs. CBC vs. CTR vs. GCM, and why ECB is never the answer.
- Key agreement vs. key transport; Diffie–Hellman gives neither confidentiality nor authentication by itself.
- Why RSA keys are so much longer than AES keys for the same strength.
- HMAC vs. hash-of-key-plus-message.
- Shor vs. Grover: what breaks and what merely weakens.

## Module 4 — User authentication

*Corresponds to chapter 3 of the reference book.*

### Identification is not authentication

**Identification** is the user presenting a claimed identity; **authentication** is the system establishing that the claim is valid. The user ID is not secret — it has to be usable for addressing and for assigning permissions — and the authenticator is. NIST SP 800-63 frames digital authentication as *establishing confidence* in an identity presented electronically, which is the right framing: authentication produces a confidence level, not a binary certainty, and that is what allows risk-based and step-up authentication to make sense.

Note also that **user authentication** and **message authentication** are different problems. The first establishes who is at the keyboard; the second establishes that a message is unaltered and from the claimed source.

### The four means

Authentication factors are conventionally grouped as:

- **Something you know** — password, PIN, answers to prearranged questions.
- **Something you possess** — a smart card, a hardware token, a phone. Often called a *token*.
- **Something you are** (static biometrics) — fingerprint, retina, face.
- **Something you do** (dynamic biometrics) — voice pattern, handwriting, typing rhythm.

**Multifactor authentication** requires factors from *different* groups. A password plus a security question is two things you know, and is not multifactor — a distinction the exam tests directly.

### Passwords, and what actually goes wrong

Passwords remain the most common and the most attacked mechanism. The standard defence is to store not the password but a **salted hash**: the salt is a random value stored alongside, unique per user, which means identical passwords produce different hashes, defeats precomputed rainbow tables, and forces an attacker to attack each account separately. The hash must additionally be **slow** — bcrypt, scrypt, Argon2 — because a fast hash like SHA-256 lets an attacker with a stolen database try billions of candidates per second.

Attack strategies worth being able to name: **offline dictionary attack** against a stolen hash file; **specific-account attack** against one user, defeated by lockout; **popular-password attack** trying common passwords across many accounts, which lockout does *not* defeat and which is countered by blocking known-breached passwords; **workstation hijacking**; **exploiting user mistakes** such as written-down or reused passwords; and **electronic monitoring**, against which a simple hashed challenge does not help if the attacker can replay it.

Current guidance has reversed on two long-standing practices. Forced periodic rotation is now discouraged, because it drives predictable increments; and composition rules that mandate character classes produce weaker, more memorable-to-machines passwords than simple length. Screening against known-breached password lists is now the recommended control.

### Tokens and biometrics

**Memory cards** store but do not process data; **smart cards** contain a processor and can perform cryptographic operations, which lets them prove possession of a key without disclosing it. A smart card or FIDO2 authenticator that signs a challenge bound to the origin is **phishing-resistant**, because a proxy site cannot replay the signature — this is why passkeys are qualitatively different from one-time codes, not merely more convenient.

**Biometrics** are compared against an enrolled template. The engineering trade-off is between the **false match rate** (an impostor accepted) and the **false non-match rate** (a legitimate user rejected); adjusting the decision threshold moves one up and the other down, and the right point depends on whether the cost of wrongful admission or wrongful exclusion is higher. The property that distinguishes biometrics from other factors: a compromised password can be changed, and a fingerprint cannot. That is the argument for treating biometrics as a local unlock for a device-bound key rather than as a credential sent to a server.

### Remote authentication and its threats

Over a network, the authenticator must never be sent in a way that can be captured and replayed, so remote authentication uses **challenge–response**: the host sends a random challenge, and the user's device returns a function of the challenge and the secret. The randomness of the challenge is what prevents replay.

The threats to enumerate: **eavesdropping**, **host attacks** on the stored password file, **replay**, **trojan horse** capture of the authenticator, and **denial of service** through mass lockout.

### The distinctions that generate questions

- Identification vs. authentication vs. authorisation.
- What makes authentication multifactor, and why two passwords are not.
- Salt vs. pepper vs. slow hashing: what each defeats.
- Which password attacks account lockout stops, and which it does not.
- False match rate vs. false non-match rate, and the threshold trade-off.
- Why FIDO2/passkeys are phishing-resistant and SMS codes are not.

## Module 5 — Access control

*Corresponds to chapter 4 of the reference book.*

### The three A's, and what sits between them

**Authentication** establishes who you are; **authorisation** — access control — determines what you may do; **audit** (accounting) records what you did. RFC 4949 defines access control as the process of granting or denying specific requests to obtain and use information and related processing services. The model has three elements: a **subject** (an entity capable of accessing objects — typically a process acting on behalf of a user), an **object** (the resource being protected), and an **access right** (read, write, execute, delete, create, search).

Subjects are conventionally classed as **owner**, **group** and **world**. The distinction that matters is that a subject is normally a *process*, not a person: privileges attach to the process, which is why a compromised process runs with the rights it was given rather than the rights the user would have exercised deliberately.

### The four models

**Discretionary access control (DAC)** lets the owner of a resource decide who may access it. It is flexible and it is how UNIX file permissions and most file shares work. Its weakness is structural: an authorised subject can pass its access on to another, so the owner's decision does not bind transitively, and DAC cannot prevent information flowing to somewhere the original owner would not have allowed.

**Mandatory access control (MAC)** compares security labels (clearances on subjects, classifications on objects) against system-wide rules that ordinary users cannot override. It is the model of classified environments, and its point is exactly the flow control DAC cannot give.

**Role-based access control (RBAC)** assigns permissions to roles and users to roles. It matches how organisations actually think — a job function has a set of needs — and it makes joiner–mover–leaver tractable, because a change of role is a change of assignment rather than an audit of individual grants.

**Attribute-based access control (ABAC)** decides from attributes of the subject, the object, the action and the environment, evaluated by policy. It expresses things RBAC cannot — "a case handler in the same unit as the file, during working hours, from a managed device" — and it scales to fine-grained and context-dependent policy at the cost of much harder analysis: with RBAC you can enumerate who has access, and with ABAC you generally cannot.

A classic representation is the **access matrix**: subjects as rows, objects as columns. Stored by column it becomes an **access control list** (per object, who may do what); stored by row it becomes a **capability list** (per subject, what it may do). The two are equivalent in expressive power and very different operationally: revoking one subject's access is easy with ACLs and hard with capabilities, while answering "what can this subject reach" is the reverse.

### Identity, credential and access management

**ICAM** is the umbrella discipline: managing digital identities, the credentials bound to them, and the access those credentials confer, across their whole lifecycle. Its practical core is the **joiner–mover–leaver** process, and the step organisations get wrong is almost always **mover**: rights are granted well and withdrawn poorly, so people accumulate the union of every role they have held. Periodic access reviews find this, but a review is a detective control — the fix is to make a role change trigger revocation automatically, driven by authoritative HR data.

**Federation** lets one organisation trust identities asserted by another. **SAML 2.0** is the long-established XML-based standard for enterprise single sign-on. **OAuth 2.0** is an *authorisation* framework for delegated access: it issues access tokens and says nothing about who the user is. **OpenID Connect** is the identity layer on top of OAuth 2.0, and the ID token is what actually authenticates. Using bare OAuth as an authentication protocol is a classic and consequential design error.

### Privileged access

Privileged accounts are the ones that matter, and they need their own regime: vaulting of credentials, session recording, approval workflows, and above all **just-in-time elevation** so that standing administrative privilege is eliminated rather than merely monitored. The measure of a PAM programme is how few permanently privileged accounts remain, not how many sessions were recorded.

**Privileged access management (PAM)** governs what a privileged session may do; **privileged identity management (PIM)** governs the lifecycle of who holds those roles at all — assignment, periodic recertification, expiry. Vendors blur the two; exams do not. Together, **PAM/PIM** answer “who may become an administrator” and “what happens while they are one”.

### The distinctions that generate questions

- DAC vs. MAC: who decides, and whether the decision binds transitively.
- RBAC vs. ABAC: enumerable roles vs. context-dependent policy, and what each buys and costs.
- ACL vs. capability list: the same matrix, sliced two ways, with opposite revocation properties.
- OAuth vs. OIDC vs. SAML: authorisation, identity on top of it, and the enterprise SSO standard.
- Why "mover" is the weak step of joiner–mover–leaver.

## Module 6 — Malicious software

*Corresponds to chapter 6 of the reference book.*

### Classifying malware by propagation and payload

The useful taxonomy separates **how it spreads** from **what it does** — the book organises the whole chapter this way, and it is a better mental model than a flat list of names, because the two dimensions are independent.

**Propagation mechanisms:**

- **Infected content — viruses.** A virus attaches itself to a host file or document and replicates when that host is executed or opened. Its defining feature is that it needs a host and needs the user to run it. A **macro virus** rides in a document's scripting code.
- **Vulnerability exploit — worms.** A worm propagates by itself across a network by exploiting a vulnerability, with no user action. That autonomy is the whole difference from a virus, and it is why worm outbreaks are measured in minutes.
- **Social engineering — spam, trojans.** A **trojan** presents a useful function and hides a malicious one; it does not self-replicate, it relies on the user choosing to run it.

**Payloads:**

- **System corruption** — destruction of data or of the system itself. Ransomware belongs here: it corrupts availability by encryption and monetises restoration.
- **Attack agent — bots and zombies.** The compromised machine is enrolled in a botnet under a command-and-control channel, then used for DDoS, spam or mining.
- **Information theft** — keyloggers, phishing, spyware, credential harvesting.
- **Stealthing** — backdoors and rootkits, which do not do damage themselves but preserve the attacker's access and hide it. A **rootkit** subverts the system's own reporting so that the compromised state is invisible to normal tools; the deeper it sits — kernel, hypervisor, firmware — the less any in-system check can be trusted.

Terms worth separating: a **logic bomb** is dormant code triggered by a condition; a **downloader** is a small first-stage payload whose only job is to fetch a bigger one; an **exploit kit** automates delivery; a **drive-by download** exploits the browser on a compromised site with no click at all.

### Advanced persistent threat

An **APT** is not a kind of malware but a kind of adversary: well-resourced, typically state-sponsored, pursuing specific targets persistently over long periods, and willing to use bespoke tooling and to remain quiet. The security implication is that signature-based defence is structurally inadequate against an adversary who can build something you have never seen, which is the argument for behavioural detection, for hunting, and for assuming compromise rather than assuming a perimeter.

### Countermeasures

Defence layers in four stages: **prevention** (patching, least privilege, application allow-listing, disabling macros, blocking exploitable content at the gateway), **detection** (signature, behavioural, and integrity checking), **identification** of what specifically is present, and **removal** — with the honest caveat that for a kernel-level rootkit or a compromise of unknown depth, the only trustworthy removal is a rebuild from known-good media.

Signature detection generalises poorly, which is why modern endpoint tools watch behaviour: a process that enumerates files and encrypts them at speed is ransomware regardless of whether its hash has been seen. **Sandboxing** runs suspicious content in isolation to observe it, and mature malware detects sandboxes and stays dormant.

Anti-virus detection generations, worth knowing as a progression: simple scanners matching signatures; heuristic scanners looking for suspicious structure; activity traps watching behaviour at run time; and full-featured protection combining all of them. Modern **EDR** and **XDR** are the same progression continued — telemetry from endpoints, correlated across network, identity and cloud.

### The distinctions that generate questions

- Virus vs. worm vs. trojan: needs a host and a user, spreads by itself, or is chosen and run.
- Propagation vs. payload as independent axes.
- Backdoor vs. rootkit vs. logic bomb.
- Why an APT is an adversary, not a malware family.
- Why signature detection cannot be the primary control, and what replaces it.

## Module 7 — Denial of service, intrusion detection and firewalls

*Corresponds to chapters 7, 8 and 9 of the reference book.*

### Denial of service

A **denial-of-service attack** attempts to prevent authorised use of a resource. It targets availability, and it works by exhausting something: network bandwidth, system resources such as connection table entries, or application capacity.

A **SYN flood** is the classic resource-exhaustion attack: the attacker sends connection requests with spoofed sources, and the target allocates state for half-open connections that never complete. The defence, **SYN cookies**, avoids allocating state until the handshake finishes — an elegant illustration of the general principle that you should not commit resources on an unauthenticated request.

A **distributed** denial-of-service attack uses many compromised machines, which defeats source-based blocking and multiplies volume. **Reflection** attacks send requests with the victim's spoofed address to third-party servers, which reply to the victim; **amplification** chooses a protocol where the reply is much larger than the request — DNS, NTP, memcached — multiplying the attacker's bandwidth many times over. The countermeasure that actually addresses the root cause is **source address filtering** at the network edge (BCP 38), because reflection depends entirely on spoofing being possible.

Defences divide into **prevention** (over-provisioning, rate limiting, filtering spoofed sources), **detection and filtering** during the attack, **traceback**, and **recovery**. Note that responding to a DoS attack is largely an exercise in having arranged things in advance — upstream scrubbing, a contact at the provider, a tested runbook — because the attack is not the time to negotiate.

### Intrusion detection

An **intrusion detection system** monitors events and analyses them for signs of intrusion. Three components: **sensors** collecting data, **analysers** deciding whether an intrusion occurred, and a **user interface**. It is a *detective* control — an **IDS** reports, an **IPS** blocks.

Two analysis approaches, and the distinction is fundamental:

- **Anomaly detection** builds a model of normal behaviour and flags deviation. It can detect attacks never seen before, at the cost of false positives and of a training period during which "normal" must actually be normal.
- **Signature or heuristic detection** matches known malicious patterns or attack rules. It is precise about what it knows and blind to everything else.

Deployment divides into **host-based** (HIDS: system calls, file integrity, logs — sees what happened on the machine, including inside encrypted traffic) and **network-based** (NIDS: packet and flow analysis — sees relationships between hosts, but is defeated by encryption and by the need to reassemble what the endpoint will see). Neither subsumes the other, which is why serious deployments have both.

The **base-rate fallacy** is the governing constraint and the single most examinable idea here. If real intrusions are rare compared with legitimate activity, even a very low false-positive rate produces alerts dominated by false alarms. A detector with 99 % true-positive and 1 % false-positive rate, applied where 10 in 100 000 events are genuine, yields about 10 true alerts against about 1 000 false ones — roughly 1 % precision. This is why raw detector output is enriched, correlated and risk-scored before it reaches an analyst, and why "improve the model" is often the wrong answer where "reduce the population you run it over" is the right one.

**Honeypots** are decoy systems with no production purpose, so any interaction with them is by definition suspicious — which inverts the base-rate problem and makes them very high-precision sensors.

### Firewalls

A firewall enforces an access policy between networks. Its design assumptions are that all traffic passes through it, that only authorised traffic is allowed, and that the firewall itself is immune to penetration. It can filter on direction, source and destination address, protocol and port, application content, and user identity.

Types, in increasing order of what they understand:

- **Packet-filtering firewall** — decides per packet on header fields. Stateless, fast, and unable to see that a packet belongs to a session it never authorised.
- **Stateful inspection firewall** — tracks connection state, so return traffic for an established session is permitted without a rule that would also permit an unsolicited inbound connection.
- **Application-level gateway (proxy)** — terminates the connection and relays at application level, so it can inspect and constrain protocol semantics. Most capable, most expensive.
- **Circuit-level gateway** — relays at transport level without inspecting content; SOCKS is the example.

A **bastion host** is a hardened, exposed system running proxy services. A **DMZ** holds systems that must be reachable from outside, between an external and an internal firewall, so that compromising a public server does not put the attacker on the internal network. **Host-based firewalls** and **personal firewalls** enforce policy at the endpoint, which is what still works when the perimeter is not where the users are.

An **intrusion prevention system** is an IDS placed inline with the authority to drop. Its risk is exactly its benefit: a false positive is now an outage.

Two modern qualifications the book predates. **Encryption** has made deep inspection at the network boundary far less effective, pushing detection to the endpoint and to identity. And **zero trust** rejects the perimeter as the primary control — not "no firewall", but no implicit trust from network location, replaced by per-request verification of identity, device posture and context.

### Threat-informed defence: kill chain, ATT&CK and indicators

Detection improves when it is aimed at what adversaries actually do rather than at whatever a sensor happens to emit. Two models organise that, and the exam expects both.

The **cyber kill chain** describes an intrusion as a sequence — reconnaissance, weaponisation, delivery, exploitation, installation, command and control, actions on objectives. Its operational value is that breaking any single link defeats the whole chain, so a defence can be placed where it is cheapest rather than where the damage would occur. Its weakness is that it is linear and perimeter-shaped: it describes an intrusion arriving from outside, not an insider or a compromised identity that is already in.

**MITRE ATT&CK** is the model that supplanted it in practice. It is a knowledge base of observed adversary behaviour organised as **tactics** (the *why* of a step — persistence, lateral movement, exfiltration), **techniques** (the *how*), and sub-techniques, each documented with real procedures, detections and mitigations. Its uses are concrete: describing an intrusion in a vocabulary every team shares; mapping which techniques current detections cover, so that the blind spots become a list rather than a feeling; and driving **purple teaming**, where an offensive team executes named techniques and the defensive team confirms, one by one, whether each was seen. Note what ATT&CK is not: a *behavioural* catalogue says nothing about which vulnerabilities you have, which is Module 9's subject.

An **indicator of compromise (IoC)** is an observable artefact suggesting an intrusion — a file hash, an address, a domain, a registry key. An indicator of attack describes behaviour instead. The difference is what the **pyramid of pain** ranks: hashes are trivial for an adversary to change, addresses and domains are cheap, host and network artefacts cost more, tools cost real work, and **TTPs** — tactics, techniques and procedures — are what an adversary would have to relearn. Detections built at the base of the pyramid expire; detections built at the top survive a campaign.

Intelligence is exchanged in **STIX**, the data format for indicators, actors and the relationships between them, over **TAXII**, the transport protocol that carries it. It is handled under the **traffic light protocol**, whose colours — TLP:RED, TLP:AMBER, TLP:GREEN, TLP:CLEAR — govern how far a recipient may pass it on. Getting TLP wrong is a trust failure that ends the sharing relationship, which is why it is a management control and not a formality.

**Threat hunting** is the deliberate search for an intrusion that produced no alert, starting from a hypothesis drawn from ATT&CK or from intelligence rather than from a detector's output. It exists because of the **false negative**: the base-rate arithmetic above explains why the alert queue fills with noise, but it is the intrusion the detector never scored at all that hunting is for. Tooling follows the same shift — **EDR** analyses endpoint behaviour instead of matching file signatures, **NDR** does the equivalent on network telemetry, and **XDR** correlates across endpoint, network, identity and cloud.

### The distinctions that generate questions

- IDS vs. IPS: detect and report vs. inline and block.
- Anomaly vs. signature detection: unknown attacks with noise vs. known attacks with precision.
- HIDS vs. NIDS: what each can see and what each is blind to.
- The base-rate fallacy, and what it implies about alert pipelines.
- Packet filtering vs. stateful vs. proxy.
- Reflection vs. amplification, and why source-address filtering is the root-cause fix.
- Kill chain vs. ATT&CK: a linear sequence of one intrusion vs. a behavioural matrix of tactics and techniques.
- IoC vs. TTP, and what the pyramid of pain is ranking.
- False positive vs. false negative: which one the alert pipeline is about, and which one threat hunting is for.
- STIX vs. TAXII: the format vs. the transport.

## Module 8 — Software security

*Corresponds to chapters 10 and 11 of the reference book.*

### Buffer overflow, and why it still matters

A **buffer overflow** occurs when a program writes more data into a buffer than it can hold, overwriting adjacent memory. On the stack, the adjacent memory includes the saved return address, so an attacker who controls the overflowing data can control where the function returns — classically, to injected shellcode; in modern practice, to existing code fragments.

It is worth understanding the mechanics rather than the label, because the defences only make sense in those terms:

- **Stack canaries** place a known value before the return address and check it before returning, detecting a contiguous overwrite.
- **Non-executable memory** (NX / DEP) marks the stack and heap non-executable, so injected code cannot run. The attacker's answer is **return-oriented programming**: chaining fragments of existing executable code, which needs no injection at all.
- **Address space layout randomisation** randomises where things sit, so the attacker cannot know the addresses to jump to. Its answer is information leaks that disclose addresses.
- **Memory-safe languages** remove the class rather than mitigating it, which is why national security agencies now recommend them for new development.

Other overflow forms worth naming: **heap overflows**, **integer overflows** (where arithmetic wraps and produces a small allocation for a large copy), and **format string vulnerabilities**.

### Handling program input

Most software vulnerabilities reduce to trusting input. Two questions govern everything: **is the size known and checked**, and **is the interpretation correct**.

**Injection attacks** all share one shape: data supplied by a user is interpreted as code by some downstream interpreter. **SQL injection** reaches the database; **command injection** reaches the shell; **cross-site scripting** reaches the victim's browser. The correct defence is not "filter bad characters" — blocklists always miss something — but to *keep data as data*: parameterised queries, safe APIs that do not invoke a shell, and contextual output encoding.

**Input validation** should be a positive test (what is allowed) rather than a negative one (what is forbidden), and it must happen on the server: client-side validation is a usability feature, not a control, because the client is under the attacker's control. Beware **canonicalisation** problems, where the same input has many representations — encoded, double-encoded, alternate Unicode forms — and a check performed before normalisation can be bypassed by choosing a different representation.

### Writing safe code, and the rest of the lifecycle

The book's guidance still holds: correct algorithm implementation, correct machine-language semantics (know what your language does at the edges), safe handling of numeric values including signedness and overflow, and correct memory management.

Interacting with the operating system introduces its own class: environment variables and search paths under the attacker's influence, **race conditions** between checking a condition and acting on it (TOCTOU), temporary files created insecurely, and privileges retained longer than needed.

Output handling is where injection reappears from the other side: **cross-site scripting** is fundamentally an output-encoding failure, and the encoding required depends on the context the value lands in — HTML body, attribute, URL, JavaScript, CSS are five different encodings.

Modern practice adds a pipeline around this. **Threat modelling** (STRIDE) at design time is the cheapest place to find a flaw. **SAST** analyses source without running it — broad coverage, more false positives; **DAST** tests the running application from outside — fewer false positives, only what it can reach; **SCA** inventories third-party dependencies and their known vulnerabilities; **IAST** instruments the running application. An **SBOM**, now mandatory for products with digital elements under the Cyber Resilience Act, is a machine-readable component inventory whose real value is answering "are we affected" within hours of the next Log4Shell rather than within weeks.

### DevSecOps and the software supply chain

Those tools only pay off if they run where the work happens. **DevSecOps** is the practice of building security into the delivery pipeline instead of inspecting for it at the end: checks run automatically on every change, they fail the build when they must, and the developer sees the result in minutes rather than receiving a report weeks later. "Shift left" names the economics — a flaw caught at design costs a fraction of the same flaw caught in production — but the harder part is organisational. A **security champions network** places a trained developer inside each team, because a central security function cannot review everything and does not want to become the bottleneck that teams learn to route around.

A realistic pipeline gates on **SCA** and secrets scanning at every commit, **SAST** on changed code, **DAST** against a deployed test environment, and infrastructure-as-code and container-image scanning before release. Two rules decide whether it works or merely exists. Findings must be triaged and prioritised, because a pipeline that reports a thousand issues teaches everyone to ignore it. And the build itself must be trustworthy: signed artefacts, reproducible builds, pinned and verified dependencies, and pipeline credentials protected at least as well as production ones — a build system with deployment rights *is* a production system.

That last point is the **software supply chain**, a first-order attack vector precisely because it multiplies: compromising one widely used component, or one build system, reaches everyone downstream. Keep two catalogues apart here. **CWE** classifies weakness *types* — the CWE Top 25 is the reference list — while a **CVE** names one specific vulnerability in one specific product; a CVE is an instance of a CWE. The **OWASP Top 10** plays the catalogue role for web application risks. Under the **Cyber Resilience Act** much of this stops being good practice and becomes a legal obligation on the manufacturer: security by design and by default, an SBOM, vulnerability handling throughout the support period, and reporting of actively exploited vulnerabilities.

### The distinctions that generate questions

- Stack canary vs. NX vs. ASLR: which attack step each one breaks, and how each is bypassed.
- Why injection is one class with many names, and why parameterisation beats filtering.
- Allow-list vs. block-list validation; why client-side validation is not a control.
- Canonicalisation and the check-before-normalise error.
- TOCTOU race conditions.
- SAST vs. DAST vs. SCA vs. IAST: what each sees and what each misses.
- CWE vs. CVE: a class of weakness vs. a named instance in a product.
- Why a pipeline that reports everything is worse than one that reports less.

## Module 9 — Operating systems, virtualisation, cloud and IoT

*Corresponds to chapters 12 and 13 of the reference book.*

### System security planning and hardening

Securing a system starts before installation, with a plan that fixes its purpose, the categories of data it handles, its users and their privilege levels, the software it runs, and how it will be maintained. Everything after that is consequence.

**Hardening** is the disciplined removal of what is not needed and the configuration of what is:

- Install the minimum necessary, and remove or disable unneeded services, applications and protocols. Every component not installed is an attack surface that does not exist.
- Configure users, groups and permissions on the principle of least privilege; remove or rename default accounts and change default credentials.
- Configure resource controls.
- Install and configure additional security controls — host firewall, endpoint protection, integrity checking, logging.
- Test the result against the plan.

**Security maintenance** is the part that decays: monitoring and analysing logs, backing up data *and verifying restoration*, and patching. Patching deserves a note about honesty — an unpatched system is not secure because a compensating control exists; a compensating control buys time, and the time has an end date.

**Application security** adds encryption of data at rest and in transit, and the observation that an application's own configuration is usually a bigger risk than its code.

### Vulnerability and exposure management

Patching is one step of a process, and the exam tests the whole of it, because the process is where organisations actually fail.

A **vulnerability scan** enumerates known weaknesses across an estate. Run **unauthenticated**, it sees what an outsider sees and misses most of what is there; run **authenticated**, with credentials on the host, it sees installed versions and configuration and is far more accurate. Either way it finds only what its plugins know about, and it finds *nothing* on assets it does not know exist — which is why asset inventory is the real prerequisite, and why "the scan is clean" is a statement about the scanner.

The identifiers form a chain worth keeping straight, because questions are built on confusing them:

- **CVE** names a specific vulnerability in a specific product. It is an identifier, not a severity.
- **CVSS** scores technical severity from 0 to 10. The base score is intrinsic to the flaw; the threat and environmental metrics adjust it for exploit availability and for what the affected asset is worth to you. Almost everyone uses the base score alone, which is why CVSS is so often mistaken for a priority.
- **EPSS** estimates the probability that a vulnerability will be exploited in the wild in the near term. It answers a different question from CVSS — likelihood rather than impact — and the two used together are far better than either alone.
- The **KEV** catalogue lists vulnerabilities *known to be exploited*. It is not a prediction but an observation, and it outranks any score.

A **vulnerability** is a weakness; an **exposure** is a weakness someone can actually reach. Prioritising by CVSS alone treats a critical flaw in an internet-facing service and the same flaw on an isolated laboratory machine as identical, which is precisely how limited remediation capacity is spent in the wrong place. Risk-based prioritisation combines severity, evidence of exploitation, exposure and asset value, and it is expressed as remediation deadlines by severity band that a named person is accountable for meeting — with measurement of how much of the estate met them, because an unmeasured deadline is a wish.

Assurance activities find what a scanner cannot, and they are not interchangeable:

- A **penetration test** is a scoped, authorised attempt to exploit and chain weaknesses. It reaches what a scanner cannot: logic flaws, broken authorisation, and chains of individually low-severity issues.
- A **red team exercise** does not aim at coverage but at an objective, and it does not warn the defenders. It therefore tests detection and response, not the state of the software.
- **Purple teaming** drops the adversarial framing: offence executes known techniques and defence verifies detection in real time, which is the fastest way to close a specific, named gap.
- A **bug bounty** buys continuous, uncoordinated attention from outside. It presupposes that the obvious findings are already handled, or it becomes an expensive way to be told about them.
- **Coordinated vulnerability disclosure** — a published policy, a contact point, a safe-harbour commitment and a stated timeline — is what turns an outsider's discovery into a report rather than a surprise, and under the current EU regime it is a duty rather than a courtesy.

### Virtualisation

Virtualisation introduces a new trust boundary: the **hypervisor**. A **type 1** hypervisor runs directly on hardware; a **type 2** runs on a host operating system, inheriting all of that host's vulnerabilities. The hypervisor is the security kernel of the whole arrangement, and its compromise is total — a **VM escape**, where code in a guest reaches the hypervisor or another guest, is the defining threat.

Beyond escape, virtualisation brings **VM sprawl** (unmanaged, unpatched machines nobody remembers creating), dormant images that come back online with months of missing patches, and the fact that migrating a running machine moves its memory across a network. Containers sharpen all of this: they share a kernel, so isolation is weaker than a VM's by construction, and the security of the image supply chain becomes a first-class concern.

### Cloud

NIST defines cloud computing by five **essential characteristics** (on-demand self-service, broad network access, resource pooling, rapid elasticity, measured service), three **service models**, and four **deployment models**.

The service models determine who secures what:

- **IaaS** — the provider secures the physical infrastructure and the virtualisation layer; you secure the operating system upward.
- **PaaS** — the provider additionally secures the OS and runtime; you secure your application and your data.
- **SaaS** — the provider secures nearly everything technical; you secure your data, your identities and your configuration.

This is the **shared responsibility model**, and the exam's favourite point about it is what never shifts: whatever the model, **accountability for the data and for regulatory compliance stays with the customer**. A provider's certification is evidence to be assessed, not a transfer of responsibility, and its scope frequently does not cover the specific service contracted.

Deployment models — **public**, **private**, **community**, **hybrid** — matter for data location, which for EU institutions is not a technicality but a legal constraint.

The threats that are genuinely cloud-specific: **misconfiguration**, which is now the dominant cause of cloud breaches and is a customer-side failure in every service model; insecure interfaces and APIs, since the control plane is reachable from the internet; account and credential compromise, where a stolen key can do in seconds what would take an intruder weeks on premises; multi-tenancy and isolation failure; and **vendor lock-in**, which is a security concern because it constrains your ability to leave a provider whose risk profile has changed. **Cloud detection and response (CDR)** tooling exists because the control plane needs monitoring that endpoint tools cannot provide.

Two control families answer the loss of the perimeter directly. A **CASB** sits between users and cloud services to give visibility of what is actually being used — including services adopted without approval — and to enforce policy on access and on data. **DLP** inspects content in motion, at rest and at the endpoint, to stop regulated or classified data leaving by a channel never intended to carry it. Both are only as good as the classification behind them: a control that cannot tell what a document is cannot decide whether it may leave.

### Internet of Things

IoT devices are constrained in power, computation and memory, are deployed in large numbers and in physically accessible places, and often have lifetimes measured in decades. That combination produces a distinctive security profile: default and unchangeable credentials, no update mechanism or one nobody uses, weak or absent cryptography because the device cannot afford it, and physical access to the hardware.

The consequence for a security architect is that device-level hardening cannot be relied upon, so the controls move outward: network segmentation so that a compromised device reaches nothing that matters, gateway-enforced authentication and encryption, monitoring for anomalous device behaviour, and procurement requirements that make updateability a condition of purchase. The Cyber Resilience Act now makes several of these legal obligations on manufacturers rather than aspirations for buyers.

### The distinctions that generate questions

- IaaS vs. PaaS vs. SaaS, and who secures what in each.
- What the shared responsibility model never shifts.
- Type 1 vs. type 2 hypervisor; VM escape as the defining virtualisation threat.
- Why containers isolate less than VMs.
- Why misconfiguration, not provider breach, dominates cloud incidents.
- Why IoT security is enforced at the network rather than on the device.
- CVE vs. CVSS vs. EPSS vs. KEV: identity, severity, likelihood, observed exploitation.
- Vulnerability vs. exposure, and why prioritising by score alone misallocates effort.
- Authenticated vs. unauthenticated scanning.
- Penetration test vs. red team vs. bug bounty: coverage, objective, continuity.
- CASB vs. DLP: control over the service used vs. control over the data itself.

## Module 10 — Database and data centre security

*Corresponds to chapter 5 of the reference book.*

### Why databases are their own problem

A database concentrates value: it holds structured, indexed, queryable data, often about people, and it is reachable by applications that are themselves reachable by users. Two properties make it distinct from file storage. First, the data has **relationships**, so what is disclosed is more than what is read. Second, access control has to work at a granularity — table, column, row, cell — that file permissions cannot express.

### SQL injection

**SQL injection** remains among the most damaging classes of web vulnerability. The mechanism is the general injection shape: user-supplied data is concatenated into a query and the database interprets part of it as SQL. The consequences run from data disclosure to authentication bypass to, where the database account is over-privileged, command execution on the host.

Injection is classified by how the attacker learns the result: **in-band** (the result comes back in the application's response, including error-based and union-based variants), **inferential or blind** (nothing comes back, so the attacker infers from behaviour or from timing), and **out-of-band** (the database is made to open a channel to the attacker, typically DNS or HTTP).

The defence is layered, and only the first item is a real fix:

- **Parameterised queries / prepared statements**, which send the query structure and the data separately so that data can never become code. Stored procedures help only if they are themselves parameterised.
- Positive input validation, as defence in depth rather than as the control.
- **Least privilege for the database account**: the application should not connect as an administrator, so that a successful injection is bounded.
- Suppressing detailed error messages, which removes the attacker's feedback channel.
- A web application firewall, which buys time and does not fix the flaw.

### Database access control and inference

**Database access control** can be **discretionary** (SQL `GRANT`/`REVOKE`, with the cascading-authorisation problem that a grantor can pass on the right to grant) or **role-based**, which is what scales. **Views** are the classic mechanism for restricting access to a subset of rows or columns without duplicating data.

**Inference** is the subtle attack and a favourite of examiners: a user who is not authorised to see a value derives it from values they *are* authorised to see, by combining queries or by exploiting functional dependencies. Aggregate queries over small populations are the standard illustration — asking for the average salary of a group of one. Countermeasures are **inference detection at design time** (altering the structure or the permitted views) and **query restriction at run time** (refusing queries whose result set is too small, or adding noise). This is the same problem that differential privacy addresses in a statistical setting.

### Database encryption

Encrypting the database protects against theft of the storage and against a database administrator who should not see the contents — and it costs, because encrypted columns cannot be indexed or searched normally. The design question is *where* the encryption boundary sits: at the disk, at the database, or in the application. Encryption inside the database protects against a stolen disk but not against a compromised application, since the application must be able to read. Stating what each placement defends against is the whole of the answer.

### Data centre security

Physical and environmental protection is a genuine part of information security, not an afterthought. The **TIA-942** tier classification describes availability by redundancy: Tier 1 basic, Tier 2 with redundant components, Tier 3 concurrently maintainable (any component can be taken out of service without stopping the load), Tier 4 fault tolerant. The threats are environmental (fire, water, temperature, humidity, power), physical intrusion, and infrastructure failure — and the controls are layered perimeters, controlled and logged access, environmental monitoring, redundant power and cooling, and fire suppression suited to electronics.

### The distinctions that generate questions

- Why parameterised queries fix injection and input filtering does not.
- In-band vs. blind vs. out-of-band injection.
- Inference as a distinct attack, and the two families of countermeasure.
- What database encryption protects against, and what it does not.
- Discretionary vs. role-based database access control, and the cascading-grant problem.

## Module 11 — Network security protocols and PKI

*Corresponds to chapters 22, 23 and 24 of the reference book.*

### Transport Layer Security

**TLS** provides confidentiality, integrity and authentication for a transport connection. Its handshake negotiates parameters, authenticates the server (and optionally the client) with certificates, and establishes a shared secret; the record protocol then carries data under symmetric encryption with integrity protection.

Two points carry most of the exam weight. First, TLS authenticates the **server's identity as certified**, not the server's trustworthiness: a valid certificate means a certification authority attested to the name, and a phishing site can hold a perfectly valid certificate. "The padlock means the site is safe" is a distractor precisely because it is what users believe. Second, **TLS 1.3** is not a minor revision: it removed static RSA key exchange, making **forward secrecy** mandatory, cut the handshake to one round trip, and deleted the legacy algorithms that made earlier versions attackable. SSL in all versions, and TLS 1.0 and 1.1, are deprecated.

**HTTPS** is HTTP over TLS. Note what it does and does not conceal: contents and full URL path are protected, but the destination host is visible through DNS and the TLS handshake unless encrypted variants are in use, and traffic volumes and timing remain observable.

### Public-key infrastructure

A **certificate** binds an identity to a public key, signed by a **certification authority**. **X.509** is the format. A PKI comprises the CAs, the registration authorities that verify identity, the repositories, and the revocation machinery.

**Revocation** is the perennially weak part. **Certificate revocation lists** are lists of revoked serials that clients must fetch and that are always somewhat stale; **OCSP** asks the CA about a single certificate, at the cost of latency and of telling the CA what you are browsing; **OCSP stapling** has the server present a recent signed status, which fixes both. Many clients historically failed open when the check was unavailable, which is a decision worth understanding: failing closed breaks the web when a CA has an outage, and failing open means revocation does not work.

The trust model's structural weakness is that a browser trusts many CAs and **any** of them can issue a certificate for any name. **Certificate Transparency** logs address this by making mis-issuance publicly detectable rather than preventable.

**eIDAS** builds the EU's legal layer on this: qualified trust service providers, qualified certificates, and the three tiers of electronic signature — simple, advanced, and qualified — where only a **qualified electronic signature**, created with a qualified signature creation device and a qualified certificate, has the legal effect equivalent to a handwritten signature across all Member States.

### Kerberos

**Kerberos** provides authentication in a distributed environment using symmetric cryptography and a trusted third party. A client authenticates once to the **authentication server**, receives a **ticket-granting ticket**, and uses it to obtain service tickets from the **ticket-granting server** without re-entering credentials — the origin of practical single sign-on.

Two design points are examinable. **Timestamps** are used to limit replay, which is why Kerberos requires reasonably synchronised clocks across the realm and why clock skew is the classic Kerberos failure. And the **KDC is a single point of catastrophic compromise**: an attacker who obtains the KDC's key can mint tickets for anything, which is what a "golden ticket" attack is.

### Email and network-layer security

**S/MIME** provides end-to-end confidentiality, integrity and signatures for email, and depends on certificates and therefore on key distribution — which is why it has never achieved general adoption. **DKIM** signs outbound mail at the domain level so recipients can verify it came from that domain; combined with **SPF** (which authorises sending hosts) and **DMARC** (which states a policy and requests reports), it addresses domain spoofing but not the content of a message from a genuinely compromised account.

**IPsec** operates at the network layer and therefore protects *all* traffic between endpoints transparently, without application support — the property that distinguishes it from TLS. **AH** provides integrity and authentication; **ESP** provides confidentiality as well and is what is normally used. **Transport mode** protects the payload between hosts; **tunnel mode** encapsulates the whole packet, which is what makes site-to-site VPNs work and what hides the internal addressing.

### Wireless and mobile

Wireless is distinctive because the medium is broadcast: an attacker needs proximity, not a connection. **WEP** is broken beyond repair, **WPA** was a transitional fix, and **WPA2** implements **IEEE 802.11i** with AES-CCMP. **WPA3** adds protection against offline dictionary attacks on the handshake. Enterprise deployments should use 802.1X with EAP rather than a pre-shared key, because a shared key cannot be revoked for one user.

Mobile device security is dominated by the fact that the device is outside your physical control and mixes personal and organisational data. The controls are device management, containerisation of organisational data, full-disk encryption, and remote wipe — and the policy question of what an employer may do to a personal device is a genuine constraint, not a technical detail.

### The distinctions that generate questions

- What a valid TLS certificate does and does not attest.
- Why TLS 1.3 mandates forward secrecy, and what static RSA key exchange lost.
- CRL vs. OCSP vs. stapling, and the fail-open dilemma.
- IPsec vs. TLS: which layer, and what that implies about application support.
- AH vs. ESP; transport vs. tunnel mode.
- Why Kerberos needs synchronised clocks, and why the KDC is the crown jewel.
- SPF vs. DKIM vs. DMARC, and what none of them stops.

## Module 12 — Security management, auditing, law and ethics

*Corresponds to chapters 14 to 19 of the reference book.*

### The management cycle

IT security management is a continuous cycle: determine objectives and requirements, assess risk, select and implement controls, monitor, and review. It is driven by policy, and the book is emphatic on one point that recurs in every framework since: without genuine senior management support, security programmes do not receive resources and are not taken seriously. Overall responsibility should be assigned to a single accountable person — the security officer — whose role spans oversight, liaison with senior management, maintenance of objectives and policy, coordination of incident response, and the awareness programme.

### The frameworks that structure all of this

Nobody builds that cycle from first principles; organisations adopt a framework and adapt it, and the exam expects you to know which one does what.

- **ISO/IEC 27001** specifies an **information security management system**: the requirements for running security as a managed process — scope, leadership, risk assessment and treatment, objectives, competence, monitoring, internal audit, management review, continual improvement. It is the one you can be **certified** against, and it is a management standard, not a control catalogue.
- **ISO/IEC 27002** is the control catalogue that accompanies it — implementation guidance for the controls an organisation selects, grouped in the current edition into organisational, people, physical and technological themes. Nobody is certified against 27002.
- **ISO/IEC 27005** gives guidance on information security **risk management**: the method underneath 27001's risk clauses, and the standard that puts vocabulary on the four approaches below.
- The **NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0** is outcome-based and deliberately not certifiable, organised into six functions: **Govern**, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. Govern is what version 2.0 added, and it is the examinable point — risk-management strategy, roles, policy and supply-chain oversight sit *above* the operational five rather than beside them. **NIST SP 800-53** supplies detailed controls, and the CIS Controls offer a prioritised starting set for an organisation that needs a defensible baseline quickly.

Frameworks also import a governance vocabulary that questions exploit. **Risk ownership** belongs to the manager who owns the business activity, never to the security function, which advises — that is what "**accountability does not outsource**" means, and it holds for suppliers and cloud providers exactly as it holds internally. Accepting a risk is legitimate, but only as a **time-bound risk acceptance**: recorded, with a named owner and a review date, because an acceptance with no expiry is how a temporary decision becomes permanent in silence. Work is organised as a **portfolio** of programmes and **projects**, planned on a **rolling multiannual strategy** broken into an **annual plan**, and divided into **strategic**, **tactical** and **operational** horizons — the horizon of a decision tells you who is entitled to take it.

Measurement has its own trap. A **key performance indicator** measures how well a process runs: patch compliance, the **reporting rate** for suspicious messages, mean **time to report**, training completion. A **key risk indicator** is forward-looking — it tracks a condition that signals rising exposure, such as the number of internet-facing systems past their remediation deadline. The error the exam likes is the **compliance-for-security substitution**: reporting that every control in the catalogue is implemented says nothing about whether risk fell, and **baseline measures** are a floor, not an achievement. **Maturity models** exist to describe capability rather than compliance, and **continual improvement** is the clause that makes the whole cycle turn rather than repeat.

### Risk assessment

Four approaches, and knowing when each is appropriate is itself examinable:

- **Baseline** — apply a standard set of controls from a recognised framework. Cheap, requires no analysis, and is either over- or under-protective for any specific system.
- **Informal** — expert judgement. Fast, but unrepeatable and dependent on who did it.
- **Detailed (formal)** — systematic identification of assets, threats, vulnerabilities and controls, with likelihood and consequence assessed. Expensive and slow, justified for critical systems.
- **Combined** — baseline everywhere, detailed analysis where the stakes justify it. This is what organisations actually do.

The vocabulary: an **asset** is what you protect; a **threat** is a potential cause of an unwanted incident; a **vulnerability** is a weakness a threat can exploit; **risk** is the effect of that uncertainty, conventionally a function of likelihood and consequence; **residual risk** is what remains after treatment. Treatment options are **reduce/mitigate**, **transfer**, **avoid** and **accept** — and transferring the financial consequence, by insurance or contract, never transfers accountability.

### Controls, plans and people

Controls classify along two independent axes, and questions exploit the fact that they are independent. By **function**: preventive, detective, corrective, plus deterrent and compensating. By **nature**: technical, management (administrative) and operational. A firewall is technical and preventive; an access review is administrative and detective; a backup is technical and corrective.

An **IT security plan** states what will be done, by whom, by when, and with what resources, and it is followed by implementation, training, and monitoring — plans that stop at the document are the common failure.

**Physical security** protects against environmental threats (fire, water, temperature, power) and human ones, and the modern point is the **integration of physical and logical security**: a badge system and an identity system that do not talk to each other permit a person to be logically present in a building they have not physically entered.

**Human resources security** is where a large proportion of incidents actually originate. It spans hiring practices and background checks proportionate to the role, acceptable-use policies, separation of duties, and — critically — the leaver process. The book's distinction between **awareness** (making the whole workforce able to recognise and report), **training** (building a competence in a role) and **education** (understanding the discipline) is exactly the one that makes a single annual course inadequate for developers.

**Computer security incident response teams** are the standing capability. The lifecycle to know is NIST SP 800-61's: preparation; detection and analysis; containment, eradication and recovery; and post-incident activity. Preparation determines how the rest goes, and the post-incident review is the phase most often skipped.

Preparation has a concrete content. A **playbook** is the pre-agreed sequence for one incident type — ransomware, business email compromise, data exfiltration — naming the decisions, the thresholds, the roles and the people to call, and its purpose is to remove improvisation from the hours when improvisation is worst. A **tabletop exercise** rehearses it without touching production, and its real output is the list of things that did not work: an out-of-date contact, an authority nobody holds at three in the morning, a decision that turns out to need a lawyer.

Evidence handling has one rule the exam asks for reliably: the **order of volatility**. Collect from the most volatile to the least — registers and cache, memory and running state, network connections, temporary files, disk, then logs and archives held elsewhere — because acquiring in the wrong order destroys what you did not take first. Pulling the power preserves the disk and loses the memory, which is exactly where a modern intrusion often lives; the choice has to be deliberate rather than reflexive. Recovery targets belong to the same conversation: **RTO** is how long the service may be down, **RPO** how much data may be lost, and the pair drives the architecture rather than following it.

### Security auditing and SIEM

An **audit trail** is a record of activity sufficient to reconstruct and examine events. Auditing differs from intrusion detection in intent: detection asks "is something happening now", auditing asks "what happened, and can we prove it". Both consume the same data.

The architecture has to answer: what to collect (at system, application, user and physical level), how to protect it, and how to analyse it. **Log integrity** is the crux — logs are the first thing a competent attacker edits, so they must be shipped off the host promptly, be tamper-evident, be time-synchronised (an incident cannot be reconstructed across systems whose clocks disagree), and be retained long enough to investigate an intrusion that went unnoticed for months.

**SIEM** centralises collection, normalises formats, correlates across sources, and supports both alerting and investigation. **SOAR** is the layer above that orchestrates and automates response. The distinction — aggregation and correlation versus orchestration and automation — is a standard exam item.

### Legal and ethical aspects

**Cybercrime** and computer crime cover offences where a computer is the target, the tool, or incidental to the offence. Investigation is complicated by jurisdiction, by the volatility of evidence, and by the standard of proof, which is why **chain of custody** and forensically sound imaging exist: findings that cannot be shown to be unaltered are not usable.

**Intellectual property** distinguishes copyright (expression), patents (invention), and trademarks — relevant here because software licensing, including open-source obligations, is a compliance surface that security teams inherit through the dependency tree.

**Privacy** is where an EU post differs most from the book's American framing. **GDPR** requires a lawful basis for processing, data minimisation, purpose limitation, and the protection of data subjects' rights; a **personal data breach** must be notified to the supervisory authority within **72 hours** of awareness unless it is unlikely to result in risk, and communicated to the data subjects themselves only where the risk is **high**. A GDPR breach and a NIS2 incident are different triggers with different regulators and different clocks, and one event can require both. **Privacy by design and by default** is a legal requirement, not a slogan.

**Ethics** matters professionally, not decoratively: security work confers access that is easy to abuse, and the standard codes converge on protecting society, acting honourably, providing diligent service and advancing the profession. The practical form is knowing what to do when the right technical answer and the convenient organisational answer diverge — and documenting the advice you gave.

### The EU institutional layer

This is where an EU post departs furthest from the textbook, and it is the material the field-related test assumes you already have.

**Regulation (EU, Euratom) 2023/2841** lays down the cybersecurity rules for the Union's own institutions, bodies, offices and agencies. Each entity must establish an **internal cybersecurity risk-management, governance and control framework**, adopt a **cybersecurity plan** approved at the highest management level, carry out a **cybersecurity maturity assessment** periodically, and designate a **local cybersecurity officer** as its point of contact. The regulation created the **Interinstitutional Cybersecurity Board (IICB)** to monitor and steer implementation, and it put **CERT-EU** on a permanent footing as the **cybersecurity service for the Union entities**: threat intelligence, incident coordination, detection support and advice for the institutions, distinct from the national CSIRTs and from ENISA.

**Directive (EU) 2022/2555 (NIS2)** applies to essential and important entities in the member states rather than to the institutions themselves, but its reporting chain is the one everyone is measured against: an **early warning within 24 hours** of becoming aware of a significant incident, an **incident notification within 72 hours** carrying an initial assessment, and a **final report within one month**. That chain is about *incidents*; the GDPR's single 72-hour deadline is about *personal data breaches*, with a different regulator, and one event can start both clocks independently.

The institutional map is worth memorising, because questions turn on who does what:

- **ENISA**, the EU Agency for Cybersecurity — capacity building, advice, the annual threat landscape, and the certification schemes. It is not an operational incident-response body.
- The **CSIRTs network** — operational cooperation between the national CSIRTs, at technical level.
- **EU-CyCLONe** — the cyber crisis liaison organisation network, which sits above the technical layer and coordinates *crisis management* for a large-scale incident.
- **CERT-EU** — the institutions' own service, as above.

The rest of the legal layer, in one pass. **Regulation (EU) 2019/881 (the Cybersecurity Act)** gave ENISA its permanent mandate and created the European cybersecurity certification framework. **Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 (the Cyber Resilience Act)** imposes security by design and by default, an SBOM, vulnerability handling and reporting duties on products with digital elements. **Regulation (EU) 2022/2554 (DORA)** does the equivalent for the financial sector, with a heavy emphasis on ICT third-party risk. **Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (the AI Act)** adds accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity requirements for high-risk AI systems. And **Regulation (EU) 910/2014 (eIDAS)**, amended by **Regulation (EU) 2024/1183**, governs electronic identification and trust services — including qualified **digital signatures** — and introduces the European Digital Identity Wallet.

### Module 1 — Security concepts, threats and design principles (Q1–10)

**1.** A system logs nothing. After an incident, management asks who deleted a set of records. Which security property was never designed in, and what follows?

A. Integrity: the records were modified, so integrity was lost when they were deleted.
B. Availability: the records cannot be consulted, so availability failed.
C. Accountability: actions must be traceable to the responsible party, and that is a design requirement — a system that does not log cannot be made accountable after the fact.
D. Confidentiality: the deletion was not disclosed, so confidentiality was the property at stake.

**2.** An attacker sits on a network link and records the timing, volume and endpoints of encrypted traffic between two offices, without altering anything. How is this classified, and what follows for the defence?

A. A passive attack — specifically traffic analysis. Passive attacks are difficult to detect because nothing changes, so they are countered by prevention rather than detection: padding, tunnelling and traffic shaping conceal the pattern that encryption alone leaves visible.
B. An active attack, because the attacker is present on the link and could modify traffic at any moment.
C. An inside attack, because the attacker is within the network perimeter.
D. Not an attack at all, because encryption protects the contents and nothing was altered.

**3.** Which statement correctly separates a vulnerability, a threat and an attack?

A. A vulnerability is an act of exploitation; a threat is the weakness that permits it; an attack is the resulting loss.
B. A vulnerability is the attacker; a threat is the tool; an attack is the target.
C. They are three names for the same thing at different levels of severity.
D. A vulnerability is a weakness in the system; a threat is the potential for that weakness to be exploited; an attack is a threat actually carried out.

**4.** A web application caches the result of an authorisation check for the duration of a session, so that later requests are served without re-evaluating permissions. Which design principle is violated, and what is the concrete risk?

A. Economy of mechanism; the risk is that the cache adds complexity.
B. Complete mediation, which requires every access to be checked against the access-control mechanism every time. The concrete risk is that a permission revoked mid-session keeps working until the session ends — including one revoked because the account was compromised.
C. Open design; the risk is that the caching strategy is not published.
D. Psychological acceptability; the risk is that users find re-authorisation annoying.

**5.** A firewall's rule evaluation ends with an explicit rule that denies anything not previously matched. Which principle does this implement, and why does it matter?

A. Fail-safe defaults: access decisions rest on permission rather than exclusion, so the default is no access and the rule set names what is allowed. It matters because a failure — a missing rule, an unparsed line, a new protocol — then denies rather than permits.
B. Least privilege, because the firewall has minimal rights.
C. Separation of privilege, because two rules must agree before traffic passes.
D. Least common mechanism, because the deny rule is shared by all traffic.

**6.** What is the practical purpose of an attack surface analysis, as distinct from a vulnerability assessment?

A. It replaces vulnerability scanning by finding the same weaknesses more cheaply.
B. It produces a severity score for each known vulnerability so they can be ranked.
C. It certifies the system against a recognised standard.
D. It enumerates what is reachable and exploitable — open ports and their listeners, code that parses input, interfaces, and people susceptible to social engineering — so that the surface can be measured and then deliberately reduced, which is a gain even where no specific vulnerability is known.

**7.** A security team classifies its attack surface into network, software and human categories. A phishing campaign that harvests credentials from staff attacks which one, and what does that imply about the countermeasure?

A. The software attack surface, so the countermeasure is patching the mail client.
B. The human attack surface, so the countermeasures are awareness and reporting culture, and technical controls that make a stolen password insufficient — phishing-resistant authentication in particular.
C. The network attack surface, so the countermeasure is a firewall rule.
D. None of the three: social engineering is outside the attack surface model.

**8.** What does an attack tree add that a list of known vulnerabilities does not?

A. It assigns a numerical probability to each attack, allowing exact risk calculation.
B. It replaces threat modelling with an automated process.
C. It decomposes an attacker's goal into the alternative paths that reach it, which forces enumeration of routes nobody had considered and shows which single defence closes the most branches.
D. It records which vulnerabilities have already been exploited in the wild.

**9.** An organisation adds a new security appliance to inspect traffic. Six months later the appliance itself is found to have a remotely exploitable flaw. What general point does this illustrate?

A. That countermeasures can themselves introduce vulnerabilities, so adding controls is not monotonically safe and each addition expands the attack surface it is meant to reduce.
B. That inspection appliances should never be deployed.
C. That the flaw was the vendor's responsibility and not a risk to the organisation.
D. That defence in depth had failed, since one control was compromised.

**10.** Under FIPS 199, the impact of a breach is rated low, moderate or high. What is that rating *about*?

A. The technical severity of the vulnerability exploited.
B. The probability that the breach occurs.
C. The cost of the security controls required to prevent it.
D. The effect that a loss of confidentiality, integrity or availability would have on organisational operations, organisational assets or individuals.

### Module 2 — Cryptographic tools (Q11–20)

**11.** Two parties share a symmetric key and exchange encrypted messages. What does successful decryption prove, and what does it not?

A. It proves the message is authentic and came from the other party specifically.
B. It proves only that the sender knew the key. Because the key is shared, it does not identify which holder of the key sent the message, and it gives no non-repudiation; symmetric encryption alone provides confidentiality, not origin authentication.
C. It proves both confidentiality and integrity, since altered ciphertext will not decrypt.
D. It proves nothing at all, because any ciphertext decrypts to something.

**12.** What does a message authentication code provide that a digital signature does not, and vice versa?

A. A MAC provides non-repudiation and a signature does not.
B. A MAC provides confidentiality and a signature provides integrity.
C. A MAC is slower but stronger than a signature in every respect.
D. A MAC is faster and needs only a shared secret, but because both parties can compute it, neither can prove to a third party who created the message; a digital signature uses a private key, so it adds non-repudiation at a higher computational cost.

**13.** Which pairing of key and operation is correct?

A. Sign with the private key, verify with the public key; encrypt with the recipient's public key, decrypt with the recipient's private key.
B. Sign with the public key, verify with the private key; encrypt with the private key, decrypt with the public key.
C. Sign and encrypt both with the private key; verify and decrypt both with the public key.
D. The keys are interchangeable, since either can undo the other.

**14.** A developer stores user passwords as plain SHA-256 digests, with no salt. The database is stolen. What is the specific weakness?

A. SHA-256 is reversible, so the passwords can be decrypted directly.
B. Nothing is wrong; hashing is the recommended practice.
C. Two problems compound. Without a salt, identical passwords produce identical digests, so precomputed tables apply and one cracked password reveals every account that shares it. And SHA-256 is fast by design, so an attacker can test billions of candidates per second; password storage needs a deliberately slow, memory-hard function such as Argon2, bcrypt or scrypt.
D. SHA-256 produces collisions, so different passwords will unlock the same account.

**15.** Why are real-world protocols such as TLS *hybrid*, using both asymmetric and symmetric cryptography?

A. Because using two algorithm families doubles the effective key length.
B. Because regulators require both.
C. Because symmetric cryptography is insecure over the internet.
D. Because asymmetric cryptography solves key establishment between parties who have never met but is orders of magnitude slower, while symmetric cryptography is fast but cannot solve key distribution alone; so the asymmetric part establishes a session key and the symmetric part carries the data.

**16.** Which property of a cryptographic hash function is broken first in practice, and why does that matter most for digital signatures?

A. Preimage resistance, because it lets an attacker recover the original message.
B. Collision resistance — the ability to find *any* two messages with the same digest. It matters most for signatures because an attacker who can craft two documents with the same digest can have a benign one signed and then present the signature as covering the malicious one.
C. Second preimage resistance, because it lets an attacker forge a MAC.
D. Determinism, because a broken hash returns different digests for the same input.

**17.** An application encrypts a database column at rest. An attacker compromises the application server and obtains a shell as the application user. What has the encryption achieved?

A. It prevents the attacker reading the data, because the column is encrypted.
B. It prevents the attacker writing to the column, though reading is still possible.
C. Very little against this attacker. Encryption at rest defends against theft of the storage medium — a stolen disk, a discarded backup, a seized server — but the application must be able to decrypt in order to function, so an attacker with the application's privileges inherits that ability.
D. It provides non-repudiation for every read of the column.

**18.** Why is the quality of the random number generator a security-critical property rather than an implementation detail?

A. Because poor randomness slows the algorithm down.
B. Because keys, nonces and initialisation vectors must be unpredictable; a pseudorandom generator is deterministic given its seed, so predictable seeding — from a clock, a process ID or a low-entropy source — produces predictable keys, and this failure is invisible in testing because the output still looks random.
C. Because random numbers must be uniformly distributed to satisfy standards audits.
D. Because a true random generator is required by law for all cryptographic use.

**19.** What is perfect forward secrecy, and what does it protect against?

A. That past sessions cannot be decrypted even if a long-term key is later compromised, because session keys are ephemeral and are not recoverable from the long-term key. It protects against an adversary who records traffic now and obtains the server's key later.
B. That the key is stored in a hardware security module and cannot be extracted.
C. That every message uses a different algorithm, so breaking one does not break the others.
D. That future sessions remain secure after a compromise, once the key has been rotated.

**20.** An organisation's cryptography is strong, standards-based and correctly implemented, yet an auditor rates it high risk. What is the most likely reason?

A. The algorithms are published rather than proprietary.
B. The key lengths exceed what regulation requires.
C. Key management: generation, distribution, storage, rotation, escrow, revocation and destruction. Cryptographic systems in practice fail far more often through keys in source repositories, keys shared by email, keys never rotated and keys that cannot be revoked than through any weakness in the mathematics.
D. The use of hybrid encryption rather than a single algorithm.

### Module 3 — Cryptographic algorithms (Q21–30)

**21.** Why is ECB mode essentially never the right choice for encrypting a message longer than one block?

A. Because it encrypts each block independently with the same key, so identical plaintext blocks produce identical ciphertext blocks and the structure of the plaintext remains visible in the ciphertext.
B. Because it is slower than the alternatives.
C. Because it requires an initialisation vector that is difficult to generate.
D. Because it cannot be parallelised.

**22.** What does AES-GCM provide that AES-CBC does not?

A. A longer key, and therefore more resistance to brute force.
B. Resistance to quantum attack.
C. Authentication as well as confidentiality — it is an authenticated encryption mode, so tampering with the ciphertext is detected. CBC provides confidentiality only, and a CBC design without a separate integrity check is vulnerable to padding-oracle attacks.
D. The ability to encrypt messages that are not a multiple of the block size.

**23.** Two parties complete a Diffie–Hellman exchange over an open network and derive a shared secret. What have they achieved, and what have they not?

A. They have achieved confidentiality of the exchange itself and mutual authentication.
B. They have established a shared secret without transmitting it, but neither party has authenticated the other, so plain Diffie–Hellman is vulnerable to a man-in-the-middle who completes a separate exchange with each side. Authentication must be supplied separately, typically by certificates or a pre-shared key.
C. They have exchanged public keys, which is equivalent to a certificate exchange.
D. They have encrypted their communication; Diffie–Hellman is an encryption algorithm.

**24.** Why must an RSA key be so much longer than an AES key for comparable security?

A. Because RSA encrypts more data per operation.
B. Because RSA keys include both a public and a private component.
C. Because RSA is a stream cipher and AES is a block cipher.
D. Because the two rest on different problems: attacking AES means searching the key space, while attacking RSA means factoring, for which algorithms far better than brute force exist — so the modulus must be large enough to defeat those algorithms, not merely to defeat exhaustive search.

**25.** HMAC is not simply the hash of the key concatenated with the message. What does its nested construction defend against?

A. Collisions in the underlying hash function.
B. Brute-force recovery of the key.
C. Length-extension attacks, to which the naive construction is vulnerable with Merkle–Damgård hashes such as SHA-2: an attacker who knows a digest and the message length can append data and compute a valid tag for the extended message without knowing the key.
D. Timing attacks on the comparison of the tag.

**26.** What is the effect of a large-scale quantum computer on symmetric cryptography, as opposed to public-key cryptography?

A. Public-key schemes based on factoring and discrete logarithms are broken by Shor's algorithm, while symmetric ciphers and hashes are only weakened by Grover's quadratic speedup, which is answered by using larger keys and digests such as AES-256 and SHA-384.
B. Both are broken equally and must be replaced together.
C. Symmetric cryptography is broken and public-key cryptography is unaffected.
D. Neither is affected; the concern applies only to quantum key distribution.

**27.** An institution wants to begin post-quantum migration. Which first step gives the most leverage, and why?

A. Replacing every certificate with a post-quantum certificate immediately.
B. Waiting until vendors ship support, to avoid rework.
C. Procuring quantum key distribution hardware between data centres.
D. Building an inventory of where cryptography is used — protocols, algorithms, key sizes, certificate lifetimes, embedded and third-party dependencies — together with the data whose confidentiality horizon outlasts the migration window, because nothing can be prioritised before it has been mapped, and crypto-agility is the actual objective.

**28.** Why does the number of keys required for pairwise symmetric communication motivate public-key cryptography?

A. Because symmetric keys expire more quickly than asymmetric ones.
B. Because *n* parties communicating pairwise need *n(n−1)/2* distinct keys, so the distribution problem grows quadratically and becomes unmanageable, whereas each party in a public-key system needs only one key pair and a way to obtain authentic public keys.
C. Because symmetric algorithms cannot be used over public networks.
D. Because asymmetric keys are shorter and therefore easier to distribute.

**29.** In an authenticated encryption construction, why is *encrypt-then-MAC* preferred over MAC-then-encrypt?

A. It is faster, because the MAC is computed over shorter data.
B. It uses only one key instead of two.
C. It allows the receiver to reject tampered ciphertext before decrypting it, and it has the strongest security proof; MAC-then-encrypt forces the receiver to decrypt attacker-controlled data first, which is the shape that produced real padding-oracle vulnerabilities.
D. It is required by FIPS.

**30.** Which statement about AES is correct?

A. AES has a fixed 128-bit block size and supports key sizes of 128, 192 and 256 bits; it is a block cipher, and its security in practice depends heavily on the mode of operation chosen.
B. AES has a variable block size matching its key size.
C. AES is a stream cipher and therefore does not need a mode of operation.
D. AES-256 is required for all uses, since AES-128 is considered broken.

### Module 4 — User authentication (Q31–40)

**31.** A system asks for a password and then for the user's mother's maiden name. Is this multifactor authentication?

A. Yes, because two separate challenges are presented.
B. Yes, provided the second answer is stored hashed.
C. Yes, because knowledge questions count as a possession factor.
D. No. Both are *something you know*, and multifactor authentication requires factors from different groups — knowledge, possession, inherence. Two knowledge factors add friction without adding a second, independent basis for the claim.

**32.** An organisation enforces a strict account lockout after five failed attempts. Which password attack does this stop, and which does it not?

A. It stops all password guessing, since no account can be attacked repeatedly.
B. It stops a specific-account attack, where an attacker tries many passwords against one user. It does not stop a popular-password attack, which tries one common password across thousands of accounts and never reaches the threshold on any of them — that is countered by screening against known-breached passwords.
C. It stops offline dictionary attacks against a stolen hash file.
D. It stops nothing useful, and lockout should be removed entirely.

**33.** What does a salt achieve in password storage, and what does it not?

A. It encrypts the password so it can be recovered if needed.
B. It slows down the hash function, which is its primary purpose.
C. It makes identical passwords produce different digests, so precomputed rainbow tables do not apply and an attacker must attack each account separately. It does not make a weak password strong, and it does not by itself slow the attacker down — that requires a deliberately slow function.
D. It hides the length of the password from an attacker.

**34.** Why are FIDO2 or passkey authenticators described as phishing-resistant, while SMS one-time codes are not?

A. Because SMS messages are unencrypted while FIDO2 uses TLS.
B. Because passkeys require biometric verification, which cannot be phished.
C. Because FIDO2 codes expire faster.
D. Because the FIDO2 credential is bound to the origin: the authenticator signs a challenge tied to the site's identity, so a proxy phishing site receives a signature that is invalid for it. An SMS code is a bearer secret that the user can be tricked into typing into the attacker's page, and SIM swapping compromises the channel entirely.

**35.** In a biometric system, what is the trade-off governed by the decision threshold?

A. Between the enrolment time and the verification time.
B. Between the false match rate — an impostor accepted — and the false non-match rate — a legitimate user rejected. Tightening the threshold reduces one and increases the other, and the right setting depends on whether wrongful admission or wrongful exclusion is the more costly error.
C. Between the size of the template and the accuracy of the comparison.
D. Between the cost of the sensor and the strength of the cryptography.

**36.** Which property makes biometrics categorically different from passwords as a credential?

A. Biometrics are always more accurate.
B. Biometrics do not need to be stored.
C. Biometrics cannot be captured remotely.
D. A compromised password can be changed and a compromised biometric cannot, which is the argument for treating biometrics as a local unlock for a device-bound key rather than as a secret transmitted to and stored by a server.

**37.** Why does remote authentication use a challenge–response exchange rather than sending the password over the link?

A. Because the password would be too long to transmit efficiently.
B. Because the server does not store passwords and therefore cannot compare them.
C. Because a value sent over the network can be captured and replayed. A fresh random challenge means the response is valid only once, so an eavesdropper who records it gains nothing.
D. Because challenge–response provides confidentiality of the session as well.

**38.** Current guidance discourages forced periodic password rotation. What is the reasoning?

A. That rotation is expensive to administer.
B. That rotation is unnecessary because modern hashes are unbreakable.
C. That passwords should never be changed once set.
D. That mandatory rotation drives predictable behaviour — small incremental changes and reuse — which weakens passwords rather than strengthening them, while doing little against the threats that actually occur; screening against breached-password lists and changing on evidence of compromise are the controls that work.

**39.** What is the difference between a memory card and a smart card as an authentication token?

A. A memory card stores data only, while a smart card contains a processor and can perform cryptographic operations — which lets it prove possession of a key by computation without ever disclosing the key.
B. A memory card is contactless and a smart card is not.
C. A memory card holds more data than a smart card.
D. There is no functional difference; the terms are interchangeable.

**40.** NIST frames digital authentication as *establishing confidence* in a claimed identity. Why is that framing useful rather than pedantic?

A. Because it means authentication can be skipped when confidence is already high.
B. Because it makes authentication a matter of degree rather than a binary verdict, which is what allows risk-based and step-up authentication: a routine action may proceed on moderate confidence while a sensitive one demands more, evaluated from device, location and behaviour.
C. Because it shifts legal responsibility to the user making the claim.
D. Because it means a failed authentication can be overridden by an administrator.

### Module 5 — Access control (Q41–50)

**41.** Which sequence correctly orders identification, authentication, authorisation and accounting?

A. Authorisation determines identity, authentication grants rights, accounting verifies them.
B. Authentication and authorisation are the same step, followed by identification.
C. Identification presents a claimed identity; authentication establishes that the claim is valid; authorisation determines what the authenticated subject may do; accounting records what it did.
D. Identification and accounting happen first, then authorisation, then authentication.

**42.** What is the structural weakness of discretionary access control?

A. An authorised subject can pass its access rights on to another subject, so the owner's decision does not bind transitively and DAC cannot control where information ultimately flows.
B. It cannot express read, write and execute separately.
C. It requires a central authority to approve every grant.
D. It cannot be implemented on modern operating systems.

**43.** When is attribute-based access control the better choice over role-based access control, and what does it cost?

A. Always, since ABAC is strictly more expressive.
B. Never, since RBAC can express any policy ABAC can.
C. When roles alone cannot express the policy — access that depends on the relationship between subject and resource, on time, on device posture or on location. The cost is analysability: with RBAC you can enumerate who has access to what, and with ABAC you generally cannot without evaluating the policy against every possible context.
D. Only in classified environments, where labels are mandatory.

**44.** An access matrix can be stored by column or by row. What are the two resulting structures, and how do they differ operationally?

A. By column it is a capability list and by row an access control list; they are identical in every respect.
B. By column it becomes an access control list, attached to each object and listing which subjects may do what; by row it becomes a capability list, held by each subject and listing what it may access. Revoking one subject's access is straightforward with ACLs and hard with capabilities, while answering "what can this subject reach" is the reverse.
C. By column it is a role hierarchy and by row an attribute policy.
D. The two forms differ in expressive power: capability lists cannot represent group permissions.

**45.** Access reviews repeatedly find staff holding rights from previous positions. Which measure addresses the cause rather than the symptom?

A. Running the access review quarterly instead of annually.
B. Requiring written justification for each right during the review.
C. Replacing RBAC with DAC so resource owners decide.
D. Fixing the *mover* step of joiner–mover–leaver so that a change of role automatically revokes the rights of the previous one, driven by authoritative HR data — leaving the periodic review as a detective control that confirms the preventive one works.

**46.** A service uses OAuth 2.0 access tokens to identify its users. What is wrong with this?

A. Nothing; OAuth 2.0 is the standard authentication protocol for web applications.
B. OAuth 2.0 is an authorisation framework: an access token says that some client is permitted to act on some resource, not who the user is. Treating token possession as proof of identity accepts a token issued for another application. OpenID Connect exists precisely to add the identity layer, and its ID token is what authenticates.
C. OAuth 2.0 is deprecated in favour of SAML for all uses.
D. OAuth 2.0 tokens cannot be validated by the receiving service.

**47.** What is the objective of a privileged access management programme, and how is progress measured?

A. To record every privileged session so that misuse can be investigated afterwards.
B. To ensure administrators use longer passwords than other users.
C. To reduce the number of administrators to a single individual.
D. To eliminate standing privilege: credentials vaulted, access granted just in time and for a bounded period, sessions recorded. Progress is measured by how few permanently privileged accounts remain, not by how many sessions were recorded.

**48.** In mandatory access control, why can a user who owns a file still be prevented from sharing it?

A. Because ownership is not recognised in MAC systems.
B. Because the file's permissions are set by the administrator rather than the owner.
C. Because access is decided by comparing system-wide labels — the subject's clearance against the object's classification — under rules that ordinary users cannot override. The owner's wishes are not an input to the decision, which is exactly the flow control DAC cannot provide.
D. Because MAC requires two-person approval for every share.

**49.** A contractor needs access to one project folder for three weeks. An administrator adds them to an existing broad group that already has access. Which principle is violated and what is the concrete risk?

A. Least privilege: the account holds standing access far beyond the task, enlarging the blast radius if it is compromised and leaving the excess rights in place after the engagement ends.
B. Separation of duties: the contractor could now complete a transaction alone.
C. Complete mediation: the group membership is not re-checked.
D. Open design: the group's membership is not published.

**50.** In the access control model, why does it matter that a subject is usually a process rather than a person?

A. It does not matter; the terms are equivalent for access control purposes.
B. Because processes cannot be authenticated, only people can.
C. Because permissions must be assigned to people for accountability.
D. Because privileges attach to the running process, so a compromised process exercises every right it was given regardless of what the user intended — which is why least privilege applies to service accounts and application identities, not only to human users.

### Module 6 — Malicious software (Q51–60)

**51.** What distinguishes a worm from a virus?

A. A worm damages data while a virus only replicates.
B. A worm propagates autonomously across a network by exploiting a vulnerability, with no user action, while a virus attaches to a host file or document and replicates when that host is opened or run. That autonomy is why worm outbreaks are measured in minutes.
C. A worm targets servers and a virus targets workstations.
D. A worm is written in a scripting language and a virus in machine code.

**52.** Why is it useful to classify malware separately by propagation mechanism and by payload?

A. Because they are independent dimensions: the same payload can arrive by a worm, a trojan or a drive-by download, and the same propagation route can deliver ransomware, a keylogger or a bot. Confusing them leads to defences aimed at the wrong axis.
B. Because propagation determines severity and payload determines speed.
C. Because the two are set by different attackers.
D. Because antivirus signatures are written for propagation only.

**53.** What is the defining characteristic of a rootkit, and what does it imply about remediation?

A. That it encrypts files and demands payment, so remediation is restoration from backup.
B. That it spreads without user action, so remediation is network isolation.
C. That it subverts the system's own reporting so that the compromised state is invisible to the tools running on it. The deeper it sits — kernel, hypervisor, firmware — the less any in-system check can be trusted, which is why the only dependable remediation is a rebuild from known-good media.
D. That it opens a network port, so remediation is closing the port.

**54.** An advanced persistent threat is best described as what?

A. A class of adversary rather than a class of malware: well-resourced, often state-sponsored, pursuing specific targets persistently over long periods with bespoke tooling and deliberate stealth. The implication is that signature-based defence is structurally inadequate, because the adversary can build something nobody has seen.
B. A particularly sophisticated computer virus that resists removal.
C. Any malware that remains on a system for more than thirty days.
D. A botnet used for distributed denial-of-service attacks.

**55.** A security team relies primarily on signature-based antivirus. What is the structural limitation, and what addresses it?

A. Signatures identify only what has been seen and catalogued, so a recompiled or newly written sample passes. Behavioural detection addresses it by watching what a process does — a program enumerating files and encrypting them at speed is ransomware regardless of its hash — which is the basis of EDR and XDR.
B. Signatures only work on Windows; the answer is cross-platform tooling.
C. Signatures consume too much disk space; the answer is cloud lookup.
D. Signatures cannot be updated frequently enough; the answer is hourly updates.

**56.** What is a logic bomb?

A. Malware that overloads a system with traffic until it fails.
B. Code that lies dormant until a predefined condition is met and then triggers its payload — a date, the absence of a name from a payroll file, a particular system state. It is a payload trigger, not a propagation mechanism.
C. A tool that automatically generates new malware variants.
D. A backdoor placed by a developer for legitimate maintenance.

**57.** A drive-by download differs from a trojan in what respect?

A. A drive-by download is always ransomware.
B. A drive-by download requires administrative privileges and a trojan does not.
C. A trojan relies on the user choosing to run something that appears useful, while a drive-by download exploits a browser or plug-in vulnerability when a compromised page is merely viewed, requiring no decision by the user at all.
D. A trojan spreads across networks and a drive-by download does not.

**58.** Which combination of controls most directly reduces the impact of ransomware, as distinct from its likelihood?

A. Awareness training and email filtering.
B. Application allow-listing and macro blocking.
C. Multi-factor authentication and password rotation.
D. Backups following 3-2-1 with at least one immutable or offline copy, tested by actual restoration — because ransomware targets reachable backups first, and an untested backup is a hypothesis rather than a control.

**59.** What role does sandboxing play in malware analysis, and what is its limitation?

A. It permanently isolates infected machines from the network.
B. It executes suspicious content in an isolated environment so its behaviour can be observed without risking a production system. Its limitation is that competent malware detects the sandbox — by timing, by absent user activity, by virtualisation artefacts — and stays dormant.
C. It removes malware from infected files by stripping the malicious sections.
D. It encrypts suspicious files so they cannot execute.

**60.** A downloader is found on a compromised host. What does its presence tell an incident responder?

A. That the attacker's first-stage code has established a foothold whose purpose is to fetch a larger package, so the responder should assume further components were retrieved, look for what was downloaded and for its command-and-control traffic, and treat the host as fully compromised rather than as carrying a single file.
B. That the machine was used as a command-and-control server.
C. That the compromise is limited, since a downloader has no payload of its own.
D. That the infection came from removable media.

### Module 7 — Denial of service, intrusion detection and firewalls (Q61–70)

**61.** In a SYN flood, what resource is exhausted and what does the standard defence do differently?

A. The target's table of half-open connections: the attacker sends connection requests from spoofed sources and the target allocates state for handshakes that never complete. SYN cookies defend by encoding the necessary state in the sequence number instead of allocating it, so no resource is committed until the handshake finishes.
B. The target's bandwidth, which is defended by upstream rate limiting.
C. The target's disk space, which is defended by log rotation.
D. The target's CPU, which is defended by adding processing capacity.

**62.** How does an amplification attack differ from a plain reflection attack, and which countermeasure addresses the root cause?

A. Amplification uses more attacking machines; reflection uses fewer.
B. Amplification targets applications; reflection targets the network layer.
C. Reflection sends requests with the victim's spoofed address to third parties, which reply to the victim; amplification additionally chooses a protocol whose reply is far larger than the request — DNS, NTP, memcached — multiplying the attacker's bandwidth. Both depend entirely on source-address spoofing, so ingress filtering at the network edge is the root-cause fix.
D. Amplification requires compromised hosts and reflection does not.

**63.** What is the essential difference between an IDS and an IPS, and what risk does the difference create?

A. An IDS works on hosts and an IPS on networks.
B. An IDS detects and reports; an IPS sits inline with the authority to drop traffic. The risk is symmetrical with the benefit: a false positive in an IDS wastes an analyst's time, while a false positive in an IPS is an outage.
C. An IDS uses signatures and an IPS uses anomaly detection.
D. An IDS is a commercial product and an IPS is an architectural pattern.

**64.** A detector flags compromised accounts with a 99 % true-positive rate and a 1 % false-positive rate. Of 100 000 accounts, 10 are genuinely compromised on a given day. What proportion of the day's alerts are true, and what follows?

A. About 99 %, so the alerts can be actioned automatically.
B. About 90 %, so a light review is sufficient.
C. About 50 %, so a second analyst halves the workload.
D. About 1 % — roughly 10 true positives against about 1 000 false ones — because at very low prevalence even a small false-positive rate dominates. This is the base-rate fallacy, and it is why detector output is enriched, correlated and risk-scored before it becomes an alert, and why narrowing the population scored is often more effective than improving the model.

**65.** What can a host-based IDS see that a network-based IDS cannot, and vice versa?

A. A HIDS sees what happens on the machine — system calls, file integrity, local logs, and the plaintext of traffic after decryption — but only on that machine. A NIDS sees relationships and flows across many hosts, including devices that cannot run an agent, but is defeated by encryption and must guess how the endpoint will reassemble what it observes.
B. A HIDS sees everything a NIDS sees, plus local activity, so a NIDS is redundant.
C. A NIDS sees everything a HIDS sees, since all activity eventually generates traffic.
D. They see the same events; the difference is only where the software runs.

**66.** Why is a honeypot a high-precision sensor?

A. Because it runs more detection rules than a production system.
B. Because it is placed outside the firewall where attacks are most visible.
C. Because it uses machine learning rather than signatures.
D. Because it has no production purpose, so it has no legitimate users and no normal traffic; any interaction with it is by definition anomalous. That inverts the base-rate problem instead of fighting it.

**67.** Which type of firewall can permit the return traffic of an outbound session without also permitting an unsolicited inbound connection on the same port?

A. A packet-filtering firewall, which examines each packet's header.
B. A circuit-level gateway, which relays at transport level.
C. A stateful inspection firewall, which tracks connection state and therefore distinguishes a packet belonging to an established session from one that starts a new one.
D. Any firewall, since return traffic is always implicitly allowed.

**68.** What is the purpose of a DMZ, and what property makes it work?

A. To provide a fast path for trusted traffic that bypasses inspection.
B. To place systems that must be reachable from outside in a segment between an external and an internal firewall, so that compromising a public-facing server does not put the attacker on the internal network — the second firewall still stands between them.
C. To isolate the firewall management interface from user traffic.
D. To hold systems that are being decommissioned.

**69.** Two modern developments qualify the classical firewall model. Which pair is correct?

A. Faster hardware and cheaper storage.
B. IPv6 adoption and the decline of NAT.
C. Ubiquitous encryption, which has made deep inspection at the network boundary far less effective and pushed detection to the endpoint and to identity; and zero trust, which removes implicit trust based on network location in favour of per-request verification of identity, device posture and context.
D. Cloud computing, which removes the need for firewalls entirely.

**70.** An anomaly-based detector is deployed on a network that was already compromised during its training period. What is the consequence?

A. The attacker's activity is learned as part of the baseline, so the ongoing intrusion becomes the definition of normal and is never flagged — which is why anomaly detection requires a training period that can be shown to be clean, and why it is complemented by signature detection and by hunting.
B. The detector produces more false positives than usual but still detects the intrusion.
C. Nothing: anomaly detection is independent of the training data.
D. The detector fails to start, because the baseline cannot be computed.

### Module 8 — Software security (Q71–80)

**71.** In a classic stack buffer overflow, what makes the vulnerability exploitable rather than merely a crash?

A. That the program writes to a buffer at all.
B. That the buffer is allocated on the heap rather than the stack.
C. That the input contains characters outside the expected alphabet.
D. That the memory adjacent to the buffer includes the saved return address, so an attacker who controls the overflowing data controls where the function returns — turning a memory-corruption bug into control of execution.

**72.** Non-executable memory (NX/DEP) prevents injected code from running on the stack. How do attackers respond?

A. By injecting the code into the heap instead, which is always executable.
B. With return-oriented programming: chaining together short fragments of code that already exist in the program or its libraries, which needs no injected code at all and is therefore unaffected by making memory non-executable.
C. By disabling NX from user space before the overflow.
D. By using a longer overflow that reaches past the protected region.

**73.** What do SQL injection, command injection and cross-site scripting have in common, and what follows for the defence?

A. All three are cases of data supplied by a user being interpreted as code by a downstream interpreter — the database, the shell, the browser. It follows that the fix is to keep data as data: parameterised queries, APIs that do not invoke a shell, and contextual output encoding. Filtering dangerous characters is a blocklist and will always miss a representation.
B. All three require the attacker to have an authenticated account.
C. All three are prevented by input length limits.
D. All three occur only in web applications.

**74.** A form validates its input in JavaScript before submitting. Is this a security control?

A. Yes, provided the validation is thorough.
B. Yes, because it prevents malformed data reaching the server.
C. No. Client-side validation is a usability feature: the client is entirely under the attacker's control, who can submit any request directly. Validation must be repeated on the server, where it is the only place it counts.
D. No, because JavaScript can be disabled, but it is sufficient if the site requires JavaScript.

**75.** What is a canonicalisation vulnerability?

A. A flaw in the canonical implementation of a cryptographic algorithm.
B. A flaw where the same input has several representations — percent-encoded, double-encoded, alternative Unicode forms, mixed path separators — and a security check performed before the input is normalised can be bypassed by choosing a representation the check does not recognise but the underlying system does.
C. A flaw where an application accepts input in more than one character set.
D. A flaw caused by normalising input, which should be avoided.

**76.** A program checks that a file is safe to open, and then opens it. What class of vulnerability does this pattern risk, and why?

A. A time-of-check to time-of-use race condition: between the check and the use, an attacker can replace the file — typically by swapping a symbolic link — so that the program acts on something other than what it validated. The fix is to operate on a handle obtained once rather than re-resolving the name.
B. A buffer overflow, because the filename may be too long.
C. An injection vulnerability, because the filename is user input.
D. An information disclosure, because the check reveals whether the file exists.

**77.** How do SAST and DAST differ in what they can find?

A. SAST finds only syntax errors; DAST finds only configuration errors.
B. They are alternative names for the same technique.
C. SAST runs the application and DAST reads the source.
D. SAST analyses source code without executing it, so it covers paths that are never exercised but reports flaws that may be unreachable in practice; DAST exercises the running application from outside, so its findings are real but limited to what it can reach and authenticate to. Neither subsumes the other.

**78.** What is the practical value of a software bill of materials?

A. It documents which developers wrote which modules for accountability purposes.
B. It replaces vulnerability scanning by listing known vulnerabilities directly.
C. It is a machine-readable inventory of components and their versions, so that when the next widely exploited library vulnerability is announced, the question "are we affected, and where" is answered in hours rather than weeks. It is now a legal requirement for products with digital elements under the Cyber Resilience Act.
D. It certifies that the software contains no open-source code.

**79.** Why is cross-site scripting best understood as an output problem rather than an input problem?

A. Because input validation is impossible for web applications.
B. Because the payload arrives in the response rather than the request.
C. Because XSS is not a real vulnerability class.
D. Because the flaw occurs when untrusted data is placed into a page without encoding appropriate to *where it lands* — HTML body, attribute, URL, JavaScript and CSS each require different encoding — so a single input filter cannot be correct for all destinations, while correct contextual output encoding is.
 
**80.** Why do national security agencies now recommend memory-safe languages for new development?

A. Because they compile faster and produce smaller binaries.
B. Because they eliminate an entire vulnerability class — buffer overflows, use-after-free and related memory-corruption bugs — at the language level, rather than mitigating exploitation of bugs that remain present, which is what canaries, NX and ASLR do.
C. Because they are required by the Cyber Resilience Act.
D. Because they prevent injection attacks as well as memory-corruption ones.

### Module 9 — Operating systems, virtualisation, cloud and IoT (Q81–90)

**81.** What is the first step in securing a new system, before any hardening is applied?

A. Installing endpoint protection and a host firewall.
B. Applying all available patches.
C. Planning: fixing the system's purpose, the categories of data it will hold, its users and their privilege levels, the software it must run, and how it will be maintained. Every hardening decision afterwards is a consequence of that, and hardening without it is guesswork.
D. Running a vulnerability scan to establish a baseline.

**82.** Why is "install the minimum necessary" a security control rather than merely good housekeeping?

A. Because unused software consumes memory that security tools need.
B. Because licensing costs are reduced.
C. Because it shortens the time required to apply patches.
D. Because every component that is not installed is attack surface that does not exist and does not need to be patched, monitored or configured. Removal is the only mitigation that cannot fail.

**83.** How does a type 2 hypervisor differ from a type 1 in security terms?

A. A type 2 hypervisor cannot run more than one guest.
B. A type 1 hypervisor runs directly on the hardware, while a type 2 runs on a host operating system and therefore inherits that host's entire vulnerability surface — a compromise of the host OS is a compromise of every guest.
C. A type 1 hypervisor provides no isolation between guests.
D. There is no security difference; the distinction is about performance.

**84.** What is a VM escape, and why is it the defining virtualisation threat?

A. Code running inside a guest reaches the hypervisor or another guest, breaking the isolation that the whole arrangement depends on. Because the hypervisor is the security kernel of the platform, its compromise is total in a way that compromising a single guest is not.
B. A virtual machine consuming more resources than allocated, degrading its neighbours.
C. A virtual machine being migrated between hosts without encryption.
D. An administrator copying a virtual machine image off the platform.

**85.** In the shared responsibility model, what does the customer retain regardless of service model?

A. Physical security of the data centre.
B. Patching of the hypervisor.
C. Availability of the underlying platform.
D. Accountability for its own data, its identities and its configuration, and for regulatory compliance. The provider's certification is evidence to be assessed — its scope often does not cover the specific service contracted — and never a transfer of responsibility.

**86.** Moving from IaaS to SaaS changes the boundary of responsibility. Which description is correct?

A. Under SaaS the customer secures the operating system; under IaaS the provider does.
B. The boundary does not move; only the pricing model changes.
C. Under IaaS the provider secures the physical infrastructure and virtualisation layer while the customer secures the operating system upward; under PaaS the provider additionally covers the OS and runtime; under SaaS the provider covers nearly all the technical stack and the customer is left with data, identities and configuration.
D. Under SaaS the customer has no remaining responsibilities.

**87.** What is the leading cause of cloud security incidents, and which side of the shared responsibility model does it fall on?

A. Customer misconfiguration — public storage buckets, over-permissive identity policies, exposed management interfaces — which falls on the customer in every service model, and which is why cloud posture management and control-plane monitoring exist.
B. Breach of the provider's hypervisor by another tenant.
C. Physical intrusion into the provider's data centre.
D. Failure of the provider's encryption at rest.

**88.** Why is IoT security typically enforced at the network rather than on the device?

A. Because IoT devices are not connected to networks by design.
B. Because devices are constrained in power, computation and memory, are deployed in numbers and in physically accessible places, and often outlive any support commitment — so hardcoded credentials, absent update mechanisms and weak cryptography cannot be assumed away. The controls therefore move outward: segmentation so a compromised device reaches nothing that matters, gateway-enforced authentication and encryption, and behavioural monitoring.
C. Because network controls are cheaper than device controls.
D. Because IoT devices cannot run cryptography of any kind.

**89.** Why do containers provide weaker isolation than virtual machines?

A. Because containers share the host kernel, so a kernel vulnerability is a path between containers, whereas each virtual machine has its own kernel behind a hypervisor boundary.
B. Because containers cannot use namespaces or control groups.
C. Because containers always run as root.
D. Because containers have no network isolation.

**90.** An organisation has hundreds of virtual machines, many created for short-lived projects and never removed. What is the security concern?

A. Licensing exposure from unused instances.
B. Performance degradation of the hypervisor.
C. VM sprawl: unmanaged machines that nobody owns, that fall outside patching and monitoring, and — for dormant images — that come back online carrying months of missing patches into a network that has moved on without them.
D. Backup storage costs growing without control.

### Module 10 — Database and data centre security (Q91–100)

**91.** Why does a database need access control at a finer granularity than a file system provides?

A. Because databases are larger than file systems.
B. Because the unit that must be protected is not the file but the table, the column, the row or even the cell — a user may legitimately read a table while being barred from one column of it — and because the data has relationships, so what is disclosed can exceed what is read.
C. Because database files are stored in a proprietary format.
D. Because databases are always accessed remotely.

**92.** Which measure actually fixes SQL injection, as opposed to reducing its impact?

A. Parameterised queries or prepared statements, which send the query structure and the data separately so that supplied data can never be parsed as SQL.
B. A web application firewall filtering suspicious request patterns.
C. Restricting the database account's privileges so that a successful injection is bounded.
D. Suppressing detailed error messages so the attacker gets no feedback.

**93.** An attacker sends inputs that produce no visible difference in the response, but the page takes noticeably longer to load for some inputs than others. What is happening?

A. A denial-of-service attack, since response times are degrading.
B. A cross-site scripting attempt whose payload is not executing.
C. A blind (inferential) SQL injection using a timing side channel: no data is returned in the response, so the attacker makes the database delay conditionally and reads the answer one bit at a time from how long the request takes.
D. A canonicalisation attack on the URL.

**94.** What is an inference attack against a database?

A. An attacker guessing the database password from public information.
B. An attacker inferring the database schema from error messages.
C. An attacker using machine learning to predict future records.
D. A user who is not authorised to see a particular value deriving it from values they *are* authorised to see — classically by aggregate queries over populations small enough that the aggregate reveals the individual. Countermeasures are structural at design time, or query restriction and noise at run time.

**95.** Encrypting a column inside the database protects against which threat, and against which does it not?

A. It protects against theft of the storage medium and against a database administrator who should not read the contents; it does not protect against a compromised application, which must be able to decrypt in order to function.
B. It protects against SQL injection, because injected queries return ciphertext.
C. It protects against all threats to the data at rest and in use.
D. It protects against a compromised application but not against a stolen disk.

**96.** What is the cascading authorisation problem in discretionary database access control?

A. That revoking a right requires the database to be restarted.
B. That a subject granted a right *with the option to grant it onward* can pass it to others, who can pass it further — so the set of people holding a privilege can grow beyond the original grantor's knowledge, and revocation has to be traced through the whole chain.
C. That granting a right to a role automatically grants it to every other role.
D. That privileges cascade from the database level to the operating system.

**97.** What does a database view provide as a security mechanism?

A. Encryption of the underlying table.
B. An audit trail of every access to the table.
C. A restricted presentation of the data — a subset of rows or columns — against which permissions can be granted, so a user is given access to exactly what they need without duplicating the data or exposing the full table.
D. A backup copy of the table that can be restored after corruption.

**98.** In the TIA-942 data centre tier classification, what does Tier 3 add over Tier 2?

A. Physical access control at the perimeter.
B. A second internet service provider.
C. Fire suppression suited to electronic equipment.
D. Concurrent maintainability: any component can be taken out of service for maintenance or replacement without stopping the load, which is a different property from merely having redundant components available.

**99.** Why should an application connect to its database with a limited-privilege account rather than an administrative one?

A. To reduce the licensing cost of the database.
B. Because a successful injection or application compromise then inherits only those limited rights — reading the tables the application needs rather than reading every database, writing to system tables, or, on some platforms, executing commands on the host.
C. Because administrative accounts are slower to authenticate.
D. Because auditors require a separate account for each application.

**100.** Which pairing of physical data centre threat and control is correct?

A. Humidity is controlled by fire suppression; power failure by access logging.
B. Physical intrusion is controlled by environmental monitoring; water ingress by redundant cooling.
C. Electrostatic discharge is controlled by CCTV; theft by humidity control.
D. Environmental threats — fire, water, temperature, humidity, power — are controlled by environmental monitoring, redundant power and cooling and appropriate suppression; human threats by layered perimeters, controlled and logged access and surveillance. Treating one family's controls as covering the other is the error.

### Module 11 — Network security protocols and PKI (Q101–110)

**101.** A user sees a valid padlock on a phishing site. What does the certificate actually attest?

A. That the site has been reviewed and approved by a certification authority.
B. That the content of the site is safe to interact with.
C. That the site belongs to a legitimate organisation.
D. That a certification authority attested to the *name*, and that the connection to that name is encrypted and authenticated. It says nothing about the trustworthiness of whoever holds the name, and obtaining a domain-validated certificate for a look-alike domain is trivial.

**102.** What did TLS 1.3 change that matters most for confidentiality of recorded traffic?

A. It increased the minimum key length for symmetric ciphers.
B. It added certificate pinning as a mandatory feature.
C. It removed static RSA key exchange, making ephemeral key agreement — and therefore forward secrecy — mandatory. Traffic recorded today can no longer be decrypted by later obtaining the server's private key.
D. It made client certificates compulsory.

**103.** Compare CRL, OCSP and OCSP stapling for certificate revocation.

A. A CRL is a periodically published list of revoked serials that the client must fetch and that is always somewhat stale; OCSP queries the issuer about one certificate, adding latency and disclosing the client's browsing to the CA; stapling has the server present a recent signed status it obtained itself, which removes both the latency and the privacy problem.
B. All three are equivalent in freshness and differ only in message format.
C. OCSP is the oldest mechanism and CRLs replaced it.
D. Stapling requires the client to contact the CA directly.

**104.** What is the structural weakness of the public certification authority trust model, and what mitigates it?

A. That certificates expire, so trust is temporary.
B. That a browser trusts many CAs and *any* of them can issue a certificate for *any* name, so the security of every site is bounded by the least trustworthy CA in the store. Certificate Transparency mitigates it by requiring issuance to be publicly logged, which makes mis-issuance detectable even though it remains preventable only by the CA.
C. That private keys must be stored on the server.
D. That certificate chains can be arbitrarily long.

**105.** What distinguishes IPsec from TLS in terms of what has to change to adopt it?

A. IPsec requires each application to be modified to call its API.
B. TLS requires kernel modifications and IPsec does not.
C. IPsec operates at the network layer, so it protects all traffic between endpoints transparently and applications need no changes; TLS operates above the transport layer, so each application must be written or configured to use it.
D. Neither requires any change; they are interchangeable.

**106.** In IPsec, what is the difference between AH and ESP, and between transport and tunnel mode?

A. AH provides integrity and origin authentication but no confidentiality; ESP additionally encrypts and is what is normally deployed. Transport mode protects the payload between two hosts leaving the original header visible; tunnel mode encapsulates the entire original packet in a new one, which is what site-to-site VPNs use and what conceals the internal addressing.
B. AH encrypts and ESP authenticates; transport mode is for site-to-site and tunnel mode for host-to-host.
C. AH and ESP are alternative names for the same protocol; the modes differ only in MTU handling.
D. AH is used in IPv4 and ESP in IPv6; the modes are version-specific.

**107.** Why does Kerberos require reasonably synchronised clocks, and what is the consequence of compromising the KDC?

A. Clocks are needed to expire passwords; KDC compromise affects only new logins.
B. Kerberos uses timestamps to limit replay of tickets and authenticators, so hosts whose clocks disagree beyond the allowed skew cannot complete authentication — clock skew is the classic Kerberos failure. Compromising the KDC is catastrophic rather than incremental: whoever holds its key can mint valid tickets for any principal and any service.
C. Clocks are used to order tickets in the database; KDC compromise is contained by ticket expiry.
D. Clocks are irrelevant to Kerberos; the KDC holds no long-term secrets.

**108.** SPF, DKIM and DMARC together address domain spoofing. What do they not address?

A. Messages sent from domains that publish no records.
B. Messages containing attachments.
C. Messages relayed through mailing lists.
D. A message sent from a genuinely compromised account in the domain: it will pass all three checks, because the domain really did send it. Authentication of the sending domain says nothing about the intent of the message.

**109.** Why should an enterprise wireless network use 802.1X with EAP rather than a pre-shared key?

A. Because pre-shared keys cannot use AES encryption.
B. Because 802.1X is faster to associate.
C. Because a pre-shared key is shared by everyone: it cannot be revoked for one departing employee without re-keying every device, it cannot identify who connected, and anyone holding it can derive the session keys of others who join. 802.1X authenticates each user individually against a directory.
D. Because pre-shared keys are prohibited by WPA3.

**110.** What does HTTPS conceal from a network observer, and what does it not?

A. It conceals the request and response contents and the URL path; it does not conceal the destination host, which remains visible through DNS resolution and the TLS handshake unless encrypted variants are in use, nor the volume and timing of traffic, which support traffic analysis.
B. It conceals everything about the connection, including which server is being contacted.
C. It conceals only the password fields in submitted forms.
D. It conceals the contents but not the response body, which is compressed rather than encrypted.

### Module 12 — Security management, auditing, law and ethics (Q111–120)

**111.** An organisation applies a standard control set from a recognised framework to all its systems, without individual analysis. Which risk-assessment approach is this, and what is its weakness?

A. Baseline. It is cheap and requires no analysis, but because it is uniform it is simultaneously over-protective for low-value systems and under-protective for critical ones. The usual remedy is the combined approach: baseline everywhere, detailed analysis where the stakes justify it.
B. Detailed formal analysis, since a recognised framework was used.
C. Informal assessment, since no formal method was documented.
D. Combined, since a framework covers all systems.

**112.** An organisation buys cyber-insurance covering the financial losses of a breach. In risk-treatment terms, what has it done and what has it not?

A. Avoided the risk, so technical controls are no longer required.
B. Transferred part of the financial consequence. It has not transferred accountability — regulators and data subjects still hold the organisation responsible — and it has not reduced the likelihood of the incident at all.
C. Mitigated the risk, because insurers require controls as a condition of cover.
D. Accepted the risk, with the insurer as the new risk owner.

**113.** Controls classify by function and by nature. Which set of examples is correctly labelled?

A. A firewall is administrative and detective; a backup is technical and preventive.
B. An access review is technical and preventive; encryption is operational and corrective.
C. A security policy is technical and detective; a guard is administrative and preventive.
D. A firewall is technical and preventive; an access review is administrative and detective; a backup is technical and corrective. The two axes are independent, and questions exploit that.

**114.** Why does the distinction between awareness, training and education matter for a security programme?

A. It does not; the three terms are used interchangeably in practice.
B. Because only education is legally required.
C. Because awareness aims at the whole workforce recognising and reporting threats, training builds a competence in a specific role, and education develops understanding of the discipline. It follows that a single annual awareness course cannot serve developers, who need threat modelling, secure coding for their stack, and the ability to triage tool findings — a skill, not general alertness.
D. Because awareness is delivered online and training in person.

**115.** How does security auditing differ from intrusion detection, given that both consume the same data?

A. Auditing is automated and intrusion detection is manual.
B. They differ in question and therefore in requirement: intrusion detection asks "is something happening now" and is optimised for timeliness, while auditing asks "what happened, and can we demonstrate it" and is optimised for completeness, integrity and retention. The same log serves both, but only auditing requires it to be tamper-evident and kept for years.
C. Auditing examines network traffic and intrusion detection examines host logs.
D. Auditing is a legal obligation and intrusion detection is voluntary.

**116.** Why is time synchronisation a precondition for incident investigation rather than a convenience?

A. Because log files are rotated on a schedule.
B. Because compliance frameworks list NTP as a required control.
C. Because encryption keys depend on accurate time.
D. Because reconstructing an incident means ordering events recorded by different systems; if their clocks disagree, the sequence cannot be established, causation cannot be argued, and the evidence may not withstand challenge.

**117.** What is the difference between SIEM and SOAR?

A. SIEM is for networks and SOAR is for endpoints.
B. SOAR replaced SIEM and performs the same function more efficiently.
C. A SIEM collects, normalises, correlates and retains log and event data for detection, investigation and compliance; a SOAR orchestrates and automates the response workflow that follows — enrichment, containment actions, ticketing, notification. One tells you something happened; the other does something about it consistently.
D. SIEM is a service and SOAR is a product category.

**118.** A single incident exposes personal data held by an entity that also operates an essential service. Which reporting obligations apply?

A. Both, on separate clocks and to separate regulators: a personal data breach must be notified to the data protection supervisory authority within 72 hours of awareness unless it is unlikely to result in risk, while the incident-reporting regime requires an early warning within 24 hours, a notification within 72 hours and a final report within one month. Satisfying one does not satisfy the other.
B. Only the data protection obligation, since personal data is involved.
C. Only the incident reporting obligation, since the entity operates an essential service.
D. Whichever the entity chooses, since duplicate reporting is not required.

**119.** When must a personal data breach be communicated to the affected individuals themselves, rather than only to the supervisory authority?

A. Always, within the same 72 hours.
B. Only when the supervisory authority orders it.
C. Never, if the authority has been notified.
D. When the breach is likely to result in a *high* risk to the rights and freedoms of those individuals — a higher threshold than the one that triggers notifying the authority, and one that can be avoided where, for example, the data was strongly encrypted and the keys were not compromised.

**120.** Why is chain of custody a technical requirement in digital forensics rather than a bureaucratic one?

A. Because it determines who pays for the investigation.
B. Because it documents who handled each item of evidence, when and why, and without it the integrity of the evidence cannot be demonstrated — findings that cannot be shown to be unaltered since collection may be inadmissible, which makes the technical work worthless for the purpose it was done.
C. Because it is required before a system can be restored to service.
D. Because it establishes which employee caused the incident.

## ANSWERS

### Module 1 — Security concepts, threats and design principles

**1. C**

Accountability is the requirement that actions be traceable to the responsible party, and it is what makes investigation, intrusion detection and legal recourse possible. The point that makes it a design property rather than an operational one: a system that did not log cannot be made accountable retrospectively. The data simply does not exist.

**2. A**

Traffic analysis is the classic passive attack: nothing is altered, so there is no observable effect to detect, and detection-based defences have nothing to work with. That is why the passive/active split matters — passive attacks are countered by prevention (encryption, padding, tunnelling), active ones by detection and recovery, because active attacks are hard to prevent absolutely.

**3. D**

The chain runs weakness → potential → act. A vulnerability is the flaw, a threat is the possibility that something exploits it, an attack is the exploitation actually attempted. Risk, not offered here, is the fourth term: the expected effect of that uncertainty on objectives.

**4. B**

Complete mediation requires every access to be checked, every time. Caching an authorisation decision is precisely the shortcut it forbids, and the consequence is concrete rather than theoretical: a revoked permission — including one revoked because the account was found to be compromised — remains effective until the session expires.

**5. A**

A default-deny rule is fail-safe defaults in its purest form: access rests on explicit permission, so anything the rule set does not name is refused. The value is in the failure mode. A rule file that fails to parse, a protocol nobody anticipated, or a gap in the policy all result in denial rather than in silent permission.

**6. D**

A vulnerability assessment finds known flaws; an attack surface analysis enumerates what is *reachable*. The distinction matters because reducing the surface — closing a port, removing a parser, dropping a feature — is a security gain independent of any specific vulnerability, including the ones not yet discovered.

**7. B**

Phishing attacks the human attack surface, and naming it correctly points at the right controls. Awareness and a reporting culture reduce the likelihood; phishing-resistant authentication reduces the consequence, because a credential the user can be tricked into disclosing stops being sufficient.

**8. C**

An attack tree decomposes a goal into the alternative routes that reach it. Its value is generative rather than descriptive: it makes you enumerate paths you had not thought of, and it supports the question a vulnerability list cannot answer — which single control closes the most branches.

**9. A**

Every control is also code, also configuration, also an interface. Security appliances have repeatedly turned out to be the way in, which is why "add another control" is not automatically a reduction in risk and why the marginal control has to justify the surface it brings with it.

**10. D**

FIPS 199 rates *impact*, not likelihood and not technical severity: the effect a loss of confidentiality, integrity or availability would have on operations, assets or individuals. Confusing impact with severity is what produces the patching queue that fixes a CVSS 9.8 on an isolated test box before a medium on an internet-facing system holding personal data.

### Module 2 — Cryptographic tools

**11. B**

The recipient learns that whoever encrypted the message held the key. Where the key is shared by two parties that is weak evidence of origin, and where it is shared by more it is almost none. Symmetric encryption provides confidentiality; origin authentication requires a MAC or a signature.

**12. D**

Both give integrity and origin authentication; the difference is who can produce them. A MAC uses a shared secret, so either party could have created it and neither can prove otherwise to a third party — no non-repudiation. A signature uses a private key held by one party, which is exactly what makes it non-repudiable, and what makes it slower.

**13. A**

Two operations, two directions, and distractors that reverse them. Signing uses the signer's *private* key, and verification the corresponding public key. Encrypting for confidentiality uses the recipient's *public* key, and only their private key decrypts. The asymmetry is the whole point: the key that is secret is the one only its owner can use.

**14. C**

Two independent failures. No salt means identical passwords hash identically, so a precomputed table applies and cracking one account cracks every account sharing the password. A fast hash means an attacker with the file can test candidates at enormous rates. Both must be fixed, and adding a salt to a fast hash fixes only half of it.

**15. D**

Asymmetric cryptography solves the problem symmetric cryptography cannot — agreeing a secret with someone you have never met — but at a cost per operation that makes it unusable for bulk data. The hybrid split assigns each to what it is good at, and recognising it explains why a TLS handshake looks the way it does.

**16. B**

Collision resistance is the strongest of the three properties and the first to fall, which is why MD5 and SHA-1 became unusable for signatures long before anyone could invert them. The attack shape is specific: craft two documents with the same digest, get the harmless one signed, and the signature verifies against the other.

**17. C**

The question a control must answer is *against whom*. Encryption at rest defends against someone who obtains the storage — a stolen disk, an old backup, a decommissioned server. It cannot defend against an attacker operating with the application's own privileges, because the application must be able to decrypt to do its job.

**18. B**

Predictable randomness is the most invisible cryptographic failure there is: the output still looks random, every test passes, and the keys are guessable. Real incidents have come from seeding with the clock or a process ID. Keys, nonces and IVs all depend on unpredictability, not merely on statistical uniformity.

**19. A**

Forward secrecy is about the *past*. If session keys are ephemeral and cannot be derived from the long-term key, then an adversary who records traffic today and compromises the server's key next year gains nothing from the recording. That threat model — record now, decrypt later — is why TLS 1.3 removed static RSA key exchange entirely.

**20. C**

Cryptography in practice fails at its edges, not at its core. Keys committed to repositories, distributed by email, never rotated and impossible to revoke defeat any algorithm. Key management across the whole lifecycle is where an auditor looks, and where the risk usually is.

### Module 3 — Cryptographic algorithms

**21. A**

ECB encrypts each block independently under the same key, so the mapping from plaintext block to ciphertext block is fixed. Structure survives encryption — which is why the encrypted-penguin image remains recognisable — and repeated blocks are visible to anyone watching. There is essentially no situation where ECB is the correct choice for a multi-block message.

**22. C**

GCM is an authenticated encryption mode: it produces a tag that detects any modification of the ciphertext. CBC gives confidentiality only, and a CBC design that decrypts before checking integrity — or checks no integrity at all — is where padding-oracle attacks live. Modern practice is AEAD by default.

**23. B**

Diffie–Hellman establishes a shared secret over an open channel, which is remarkable and insufficient. It authenticates nobody, so an attacker in the middle can run one exchange with each party and relay. That is why it is always paired with authentication, and why "DH provides authentication" is a reliable distractor.

**24. D**

Attacking AES means searching a key space, so 128 bits is 128 bits of work. Attacking RSA means factoring, and the best known factoring algorithms are far faster than exhaustive search — so the modulus has to be large enough to defeat those, not merely large enough to be un-guessable. That is why 2048-bit RSA is roughly comparable to 112–128-bit symmetric security.

**25. C**

With a Merkle–Damgård hash such as SHA-2, knowing H(key‖message) and the message length lets an attacker compute H(key‖message‖padding‖extra) without knowing the key. HMAC's nested structure blocks exactly this. It is a good example of a construction that exists because the obvious approach is broken in a non-obvious way.

**26. A**

The two families are affected quite differently and the exam relies on candidates blurring them. Shor breaks factoring and discrete logarithms outright, taking RSA, Diffie–Hellman and elliptic curves with it. Grover halves the effective strength of symmetric primitives, which larger keys and digests answer without any change of algorithm.

**27. D**

Every subsequent decision depends on the inventory: which systems are affected, which the vendor can upgrade, which embed cryptography that cannot change without redesign, and which data must stay confidential beyond the migration window. Buying hardware or swapping certificates first spends effort without reducing the risk that matters.

**28. B**

The quadratic growth is the practical argument. Ten parties need 45 keys; a thousand need almost half a million, each of which must be generated, distributed securely, stored and revoked. Public-key cryptography replaces that with one key pair per party plus the separate — and real — problem of authenticating public keys.

**29. C**

Encrypt-then-MAC lets the receiver verify the tag over the ciphertext and discard anything tampered with *before* decryption. The alternatives force the receiver to process attacker-controlled data first, which is the shape that produced padding oracles. It also has the cleanest security proof, which is why it is the recommended construction.

**30. A**

AES has a fixed 128-bit block regardless of key size, and supports 128, 192 and 256-bit keys. The point worth carrying away is the last clause: AES is a block cipher, and a block cipher is not a complete encryption scheme. The mode of operation determines whether the result is secure, and it is where most real failures occur.

### Module 4 — User authentication

**31. D**

Both a password and a maiden name are *something you know*. Multifactor means factors from different groups — knowledge, possession, inherence — because the point is that compromising one does not compromise the other. Two knowledge factors fall to the same phishing page or the same breach dump.

**32. B**

Lockout defends the account it is applied to, so it defeats an attacker grinding many passwords against one user. It is useless against the inverse strategy — one very common password tried against thousands of accounts — because no individual account ever reaches the threshold. That attack is countered by screening against breached-password lists.

**33. C**

A salt does one thing well: it makes each password's hash unique even when the passwords are identical, which kills precomputation and forces per-account work. It does not slow anything down, and it does not rescue a weak password. Slowness is the job of the key derivation function, and the two controls are often confused because they usually appear together.

**34. D**

Origin binding is the mechanism. The authenticator signs a challenge tied to the site's identity, so a signature produced for a phishing proxy does not verify for the real site — the attacker gains nothing by relaying it. An SMS code is a bearer secret with no such binding, and the delivery channel is itself attackable through SIM swapping.

**35. B**

Every biometric system has a threshold, and moving it trades one error against the other: a stricter setting rejects more impostors and also more legitimate users. There is no setting that removes both. The right point is a business decision about which error costs more, which is why a border control and a phone unlock are tuned differently.

**36. D**

Revocability is the structural difference. A password compromised today is replaced today; a fingerprint is not. That argues for using biometrics as a local gesture that unlocks a device-bound cryptographic key — where the biometric never leaves the device and what is transmitted is revocable — rather than as a secret held on a server.

**37. C**

Anything sent over the network can be captured; the question is whether capturing it is useful. Challenge–response makes the response valid for one fresh random challenge only, so a recording is worthless. That is also why the randomness and freshness of the challenge is the security-critical part, not the strength of the function.

**38. D**

The guidance changed because the evidence did. Mandatory rotation produces predictable transformations of the same password and reuse across systems, weakening the credential while imposing a cost. The controls that address the actual threats are screening against known-breached passwords, encouraging length, and changing on evidence of compromise.

**39. A**

A memory card holds data; a smart card computes. That difference is what allows a smart card to prove possession of a private key by performing an operation with it, without the key ever leaving the card — which is qualitatively stronger than transmitting a stored secret and is the basis of certificate-based authentication.

**40. B**

Treating authentication as confidence rather than certainty is what makes proportionate authentication coherent. A low-risk action can proceed on ordinary confidence; a payment or a privilege change can demand more, with the extra evidence drawn from device, location, and behaviour. A binary model has no way to express that.

### Module 5 — Access control

**41. C**

Four steps in a fixed order, and the exam reverses them constantly. Identification is the claim, authentication is the verification of the claim, authorisation is the decision about what the verified subject may do, and accounting is the record of what it did. Only the last is optional in a technical sense, and it is the one that makes the others auditable.

**42. A**

Because the owner can pass rights on, and so can the recipient, DAC controls the first grant and nothing after it. Information can therefore reach places the original owner would have refused, without any rule being broken. That transitivity gap is precisely what mandatory access control exists to close.

**43. C**

ABAC earns its complexity when the policy genuinely depends on context that a role cannot encode — relationship, time, device, location. The price is real: RBAC lets you answer "who can read this" by enumeration, and ABAC generally does not, which makes review, audit and incident scoping much harder. The right answer is the one that names both sides.

**44. B**

Same matrix, two slicings, opposite operational properties. ACLs make "who can access this object" cheap and "what can this subject reach" expensive, and revocation per subject requires visiting every object. Capabilities invert both. Neither is more expressive; they differ in which question is cheap.

**45. D**

A recurring review finding is a symptom of a process that grants well and revokes badly. Reviewing more often finds the same problem sooner without fixing it. Making a role change trigger revocation automatically, from authoritative HR data, removes the accumulation at source and leaves the review to do what a detective control should: confirm the preventive one worked.

**46. B**

An OAuth access token is a statement about permission, not identity: it says a client may act on a resource. Accepting one as proof of who the user is admits a token minted for a different application — the confused-deputy shape. OpenID Connect exists to supply the missing layer, and its ID token is the artefact that authenticates.

**47. D**

Recording sessions is useful and is not the objective. The objective is that no account holds administrative rights permanently — credentials vaulted, elevation granted for a bounded task and withdrawn afterwards. Measuring recorded sessions measures activity; measuring remaining standing privilege measures the risk the programme exists to remove.

**48. C**

In MAC the decision comes from comparing labels under system-wide rules, and the owner is not a party to it. That is the whole design: the organisation, not the individual, decides where information may flow, which is why MAC is what classified environments use and why DAC cannot be made to do the same job by policy alone.

**49. A**

Least privilege has two dimensions and this violates both. The scope is wrong — the account gains everything the group can reach, not the one folder — and the duration is wrong, because group membership outlives the three weeks unless someone remembers to remove it. A time-bounded grant scoped to the folder is the correct shape.

**50. D**

Access control decisions are made about the running process, not the person who started it. A compromised browser, parser or service exercises exactly the rights it was granted, whatever the user intended. That is why least privilege applies with full force to service accounts, application identities and automation — which typically hold more privilege than any human.

### Module 6 — Malicious software

**51. B**

Autonomy is the discriminator. A worm exploits a vulnerability to move by itself, so its spread is bounded by network reachability and scanning speed rather than by human behaviour — which is why worm outbreaks historically saturated the internet in minutes. A virus needs a host file and a user to open it.

**52. A**

Propagation and payload vary independently, and defences act on different points of each. Blocking macros and patching addresses propagation; behavioural detection, backups and segmentation address payload. A taxonomy that mixes the two produces defences aimed at the wrong axis and blind spots where a familiar payload arrives by an unfamiliar route.

**53. C**

A rootkit's function is concealment, not damage. Because it subverts the very reporting the defender relies on, any check performed by the compromised system is suspect, and the deeper it sits the less can be trusted. That is why the responsible answer to a suspected rootkit is a rebuild from known-good media rather than a cleaning tool.

**54. A**

APT names an adversary, not a family of code: resourced, patient, targeted, and willing to develop tooling nobody has catalogued. That is exactly the profile signature detection cannot address, which is why behavioural detection, threat hunting and an assume-breach posture are the structural responses rather than better signatures.

**55. A**

A signature identifies what has been seen. Recompiling, packing or simply writing something new defeats it, which puts the defender permanently behind. Behavioural detection asks what a process is doing rather than what it is — mass file enumeration followed by encryption is ransomware regardless of hash — and that is the basis of EDR and XDR.

**56. B**

A logic bomb is a trigger, not a delivery mechanism: dormant code that fires on a condition. It is worth keeping separate from propagation terms because it explains a category of insider incident where nothing spreads and nothing is downloaded, and where the payload activates long after the person responsible has left.

**57. C**

The difference is whether the user chooses. A trojan depends on someone deciding to run something that looks useful; a drive-by download exploits the browser or a plug-in when a page is merely viewed. That distinction determines the control: user judgement helps against one and is irrelevant against the other, where patching and content isolation are what work.

**58. D**

The question separates likelihood from impact deliberately. Awareness, filtering and allow-listing reduce the chance of infection; only recoverable backups reduce what happens once it succeeds. The qualifiers carry the weight: reachable backups are encrypted along with everything else, and a backup that has never been restored is an assumption rather than a control.

**59. B**

A sandbox trades safety for fidelity: you learn what the sample does, in an environment the sample can often tell is not real. Timing checks, absent user activity and virtualisation artefacts let mature malware stay dormant and appear benign, which is why sandbox output is evidence rather than a verdict.

**60. A**

A downloader is a first stage whose entire purpose is to fetch more. Its presence means the interesting components are elsewhere: the responder should assume a second stage was retrieved, hunt for command-and-control traffic and for what came down, and treat the host as fully compromised rather than as carrying one file to delete.

### Module 7 — Denial of service, intrusion detection and firewalls

**61. A**

The exhausted resource is state, not bandwidth: an entry in the table of half-open connections for every spoofed SYN. SYN cookies are elegant because they attack the premise rather than the volume — the server encodes what it would have stored into the sequence number and allocates nothing until the handshake completes, which generalises to a useful rule about never committing resources to an unauthenticated request.

**62. C**

Reflection borrows a third party's reply; amplification chooses a third party whose reply is much larger than the request. Both are built on the same foundation — the attacker can put the victim's address on a packet — so ingress filtering at the network edge is the one countermeasure that removes the technique rather than absorbing its output.

**63. B**

Detect and report against detect and drop. The consequence is what makes the choice hard: an IPS converts a detection error into an availability incident, which is why organisations often run detection in blocking mode only for the rules they trust most and in alerting mode for the rest.

**64. D**

With 10 true cases a 99 % true-positive rate yields about 10 detections; with 99 990 clean accounts a 1 % false-positive rate yields about 1 000. Precision is therefore around 1 %. This is the base-rate fallacy, and its practical lesson is that improving a model's rates is often less effective than reducing the population it scores — which is exactly why honeypots and risk-based triage work.

**65. A**

Neither subsumes the other, which is the answer's point. A HIDS sees the machine's own behaviour and the plaintext after decryption, but only that machine. A NIDS sees flows and relationships across everything on the wire, including devices that cannot run an agent, but is defeated by encryption and must infer how the endpoint will reassemble what it saw.

**66. D**

A honeypot has no legitimate users, so its base rate of malicious interaction is essentially one. That is a structural advantage no detection algorithm can match: instead of trying to separate rare attacks from abundant normality, it removes the normality. The lesson generalises — narrowing the population is often more powerful than sharpening the detector.

**67. C**

Statefulness is what lets a firewall distinguish "this packet belongs to a session we permitted" from "this packet is starting a new one". A stateless packet filter has to approximate that with rules about ports and flags, which is both permissive and fragile.

**68. B**

The DMZ is a containment design, not a performance one. Systems that must be reachable from outside are the ones most likely to be compromised, so they are placed where a compromise still leaves a firewall between the attacker and the internal network. The property that makes it work is that the second boundary exists at all.

**69. C**

Both developments undercut the same assumption — that the interesting security decisions can be made at a network boundary. Encryption removed the visibility, and zero trust removed the premise that being inside the boundary means anything. Neither abolishes firewalls; both demote them from primary control to one control among several.

**70. A**

Anomaly detection defines normal from observation, so an intrusion present during training becomes part of the definition of normal. The practical consequences are that the baseline period must be defensible, that baselines need periodic revalidation, and that anomaly detection cannot be the only method — which is why signature detection and hypothesis-driven hunting sit alongside it.

### Module 8 — Software security

**71. D**

Overflowing a buffer is a bug; overwriting the saved return address turns it into control of execution. That is the step that matters, and it is why the classic defences all target it specifically — canaries detect the overwrite, NX makes the destination unusable, ASLR makes the destination unknown.

**72. B**

Return-oriented programming was the answer to non-executable memory, and it is a good illustration of how mitigations shift attacks rather than ending them. If injected code cannot run, an attacker assembles the behaviour from fragments of code that are already there and already executable, which no amount of marking memory non-executable prevents.

**73. A**

One class, three interpreters. Recognising the shape gives the fix directly: keep data as data so the interpreter never parses it as code — parameterised queries, APIs that do not shell out, contextual output encoding. Filtering dangerous characters is a blocklist, and blocklists fail against encodings and representations nobody enumerated.

**74. C**

The client is the attacker's territory. Anything the browser checks, the attacker can skip by issuing the request directly, so client-side validation belongs in the usability column: it gives fast feedback and reduces round trips. The server must repeat every check, because that is where the trust boundary actually is.

**75. B**

Canonicalisation bugs are ordering bugs. The check and the use disagree about what the input *is*, because one sees the encoded form and the other the decoded one. The rule that follows is to normalise first and validate second, and never to validate a string you are going to transform afterwards.

**76. A**

TOCTOU races exist wherever a program validates a name and then acts on it, because the mapping from name to object can change in between. The fix is not a faster check but a different one: resolve once to a handle and operate on that, so the thing validated and the thing used are the same object.

**77. D**

Each sees what the other cannot. Static analysis reads all paths but cannot know which are reachable or how the system is configured, so it over-reports. Dynamic analysis exercises the real system so its findings are genuine, but only for what it reached and authenticated to. Treating either as complete is the error.

**78. C**

The SBOM's value is answering one question fast: are we affected, and where. Organisations that took weeks to answer that for Log4Shell were not lacking tools but lacking an inventory. Its status has changed too — under the Cyber Resilience Act it is an obligation for products with digital elements, not a good practice.

**79. D**

The same value is safe in one place and dangerous in another, and the encoding that neutralises it differs between an HTML body, an attribute, a URL, JavaScript and CSS. That is why no input filter can be correct for all destinations, and why the reliable fix is encoding at the point of output, chosen for the context the value lands in.

**80. B**

Canaries, NX and ASLR make exploitation harder while the bugs remain. A memory-safe language removes the possibility of the bug, which is a different kind of intervention — and given that memory-corruption flaws have accounted for a large share of critical vulnerabilities in system software for decades, that is why the recommendation is now about language choice for new code rather than about mitigations.

### Module 9 — Operating systems, virtualisation, cloud and IoT

**81. C**

Hardening decisions are answers, and without the plan there is no question. What the system is for, what data it holds, who uses it and at what privilege, what software it must run and how it will be maintained — every later decision about services, accounts, controls and patching follows from those. Hardening without them is applying someone else's checklist.

**82. D**

Removal is the only mitigation with no failure mode. A component that is not installed cannot be exploited, does not need patching, does not need monitoring, does not need configuring, and does not appear in the next advisory. Every other control can be misconfigured or bypassed.

**83. B**

A type 2 hypervisor sits on a general-purpose operating system with its own users, services and vulnerabilities, and everything above it depends on that OS staying intact. A type 1 removes that layer, which is why production virtualisation uses it. The isolation between guests exists in both; what differs is how much else has to hold.

**84. A**

The hypervisor is the trust anchor for every guest, so breaking out of a guest into it is a total compromise rather than an incremental one — a different category from compromising one virtual machine. That is why hypervisor patching is treated with a seriousness that guest patching usually is not.

**85. D**

The model shifts operational work, not accountability. Whatever the service model, the customer answers to regulators and to data subjects for its own data, identities and configuration. A provider's certificate is evidence whose scope has to be read — it frequently covers a different service or region than the one contracted — and is not a transfer of responsibility.

**86. C**

The boundary moves upward through the stack as you move from IaaS to SaaS, and knowing where it sits for each model is what prevents the classic gap where both parties assume the other patches something. Note the constant: data, identities and configuration never leave the customer.

**87. A**

The dominant cause is not a provider failure but a customer one, and it is a customer one in every service model. Publicly readable storage, over-permissive identity policies and exposed management interfaces are configuration, and configuration is on the tenant's side of every line in the shared responsibility model.

**88. B**

The device cannot be relied upon, so the control has to sit somewhere that can. Constrained hardware, physical accessibility, long lifetimes and absent update paths mean device-level hardening is at best partial, which pushes segmentation, gateway-enforced authentication and behavioural monitoring to the front — and makes updateability a procurement requirement rather than a hope.

**89. A**

Containers isolate by kernel features — namespaces and cgroups — while sharing one kernel. A kernel vulnerability is therefore a path between containers, where a virtual machine would have its own kernel and a hypervisor boundary in the way. That is a structural difference, not a configuration one, and it is why sensitive workloads are often separated at the VM level.

**90. C**

Sprawl is a governance failure with security consequences: machines nobody owns fall out of patching and monitoring, and dormant images are worse than absent ones because they return to a network that has moved on, carrying months of missing patches and stale credentials.

### Module 10 — Database and data centre security

**91. B**

Two reasons, and the answer needs both. Granularity: the protected unit is a column or a row rather than a file, and a user may legitimately read a table while being barred from one field of it. And relationships: because the data is connected, what a query discloses can exceed what it literally reads — which is the door to inference attacks.

**92. A**

Only parameterisation removes the possibility, by sending the query structure and the data through separate channels so that supplied data is never parsed as SQL. The other three are worth doing and are all mitigations: a WAF buys time, least privilege bounds the damage, and suppressing errors removes feedback — none of them closes the flaw.

**93. C**

No data comes back and the timing varies with the input: that is inferential injection using a time-based side channel. The attacker makes the database sleep conditionally and reads one bit per request. It is slow, entirely automatable, and it is why "the application returns no data or errors" is not evidence that injection is impossible.

**94. D**

Inference is the attack where no rule is broken. Every individual query is authorised, and the combination discloses what none of them was allowed to. The aggregate over a population of one is the standard illustration. The countermeasures split into design time — restructuring or restricting views — and run time, where queries whose result sets are too small are refused or perturbed.

**95. A**

Encryption inside the database is aimed at people who obtain the storage or who administer the database, and it works against them. It cannot work against a compromised application, because the application holds the ability to decrypt by necessity. Naming the adversary a control defeats is the whole substance of the answer.

**96. B**

The problem is that a grant can carry the right to grant. Privileges then propagate beyond the original grantor's knowledge, and revoking cleanly means tracing and undoing the whole chain — which is why role-based administration, where privileges attach to roles rather than being passed between individuals, is what scales.

**97. C**

A view is the standard mechanism for least privilege in a database: define the subset of rows and columns a role legitimately needs, grant on the view rather than the table, and the user's access is exactly that subset without any duplication of data. It is also a design-time countermeasure to inference.

**98. D**

Tier 2 has redundant components; Tier 3 adds that maintenance can be performed on any component without taking the load down. The distinction is between owning spares and being able to use them without an outage, which is a design property of the distribution paths rather than a count of equipment.

**99. B**

Least privilege applied to a machine identity. If the application connects as an administrator, a successful injection or an application compromise inherits administrative rights over every database on the server, and on some platforms the ability to execute commands on the host. A scoped account converts a catastrophe into an incident.

**100. D**

The two threat families need different controls and the distractors mix them deliberately. Environmental threats are answered by monitoring, redundancy and suppression appropriate to electronics; human threats by layered perimeters, access control and logging. A site with excellent fire suppression and an unlocked door is not protected.

### Module 11 — Network security protocols and PKI

**101. D**

A domain-validated certificate attests that whoever obtained it demonstrated control of the name, and nothing more. It says nothing about the honesty of the holder, and such certificates are free and instant. That is why the padlock stopped being presented as a safety signal, and why "the site is encrypted" and "the site is legitimate" are unrelated claims.

**102. C**

Removing static RSA key exchange made ephemeral key agreement — and therefore forward secrecy — mandatory rather than optional. The consequence is exactly the harvest-now-decrypt-later threat: with TLS 1.3, obtaining the server's private key later does not decrypt traffic recorded earlier, which was not true of the RSA key transport it replaced.

**103. A**

The three differ in freshness, cost and privacy. A CRL is stale by design and can be large; OCSP is fresh but adds a round trip to a third party and tells that third party what the client is visiting; stapling has the server fetch and present the signed status itself, which fixes both problems at once and is why it is the recommended deployment.

**104. B**

The trust store is a flat list, so the weakest CA in it can issue for any name, and historically some have. Certificate Transparency does not prevent that — only the CA can — but it makes it publicly visible, which converts an undetectable attack into a detectable one and gives domain owners a way to notice certificates they did not request.

**105. C**

The layer determines what has to change. IPsec protects everything between two endpoints without applications knowing it exists, which is why it suits site-to-site and remote-access VPNs. TLS sits above transport, so each application must use it deliberately — which is more work and gives finer control over what is protected and how it is authenticated.

**106. A**

Two independent choices. AH authenticates without encrypting and is rarely deployed; ESP encrypts as well and is what is used. Transport mode leaves the original IP header exposed and protects the payload between two hosts; tunnel mode wraps the whole packet, which is what allows gateway-to-gateway VPNs and what hides the internal topology.

**107. B**

Timestamps are how Kerberos limits replay, so a host whose clock has drifted beyond the permitted skew simply cannot authenticate — the single most common Kerberos failure in practice. And the KDC holds the keys from which all tickets derive, so its compromise is not a step in an intrusion but the end of one.

**108. D**

All three authenticate the *domain*, and a compromised account is the domain. The message will pass SPF, DKIM and DMARC because it genuinely originated where it claims. That is why domain authentication is a necessary control against spoofing and no control at all against business email compromise, which needs behavioural detection and process controls instead.

**109. C**

A pre-shared key is a shared secret with all the problems shared secrets have: it identifies nobody, it cannot be revoked for one person, and anyone holding it can derive others' session keys. 802.1X authenticates each user against a directory, which restores accountability and makes deprovisioning a directory operation rather than a re-keying exercise.

**110. A**

HTTPS protects contents and path, and leaves a good deal visible. The destination host leaks through DNS and, unless encrypted extensions are in use, through the handshake; and volume and timing support traffic analysis regardless. Knowing precisely what remains observable is what separates a considered answer from "it is encrypted".

### Module 12 — Security management, auditing, law and ethics

**111. A**

The baseline approach applies a standard control set without analysis: cheap, quick and uniform, and uniformity is exactly the weakness, because the same set is excessive for a low-value system and inadequate for a critical one. The combined approach — baseline everywhere, detailed analysis where the stakes justify it — is what organisations converge on.

**112. B**

Insurance transfers financial consequence and nothing else. It does not reduce the probability of the incident, and it does not move accountability: the regulator and the data subjects still look to the organisation. Treating transferred risk as though it were mitigated or avoided is the trap the question is built on.

**113. D**

The two axes are independent, and the distractors work by pairing a control with a label from the wrong axis. A firewall stops something happening and is a technology; an access review notices something that already happened and is a process; a backup restores after the fact and is a technology. Any control has one label from each axis.

**114. C**

Different objectives, different audiences, different evidence of success. Awareness makes everyone able to notice and report; training builds a specific competence; education develops understanding of the discipline. The practical consequence is the one in the answer: developers make design decisions that awareness material cannot reach, so they need training in a skill.

**115. B**

The same log serves both, which is why the distinction is easy to miss and why it matters. Detection is optimised for timeliness and tolerates gaps; auditing is optimised for completeness, integrity and retention, because its output must survive challenge. Only the auditing requirement forces tamper-evidence and multi-year retention.

**116. D**

An incident is reconstructed by ordering events across systems, and ordering is impossible if the clocks disagree. Beyond the practical difficulty, an evidential one: a timeline assembled from unsynchronised sources cannot support an argument about causation and will not withstand scrutiny. NTP is a forensic prerequisite, not a hygiene item.

**117. C**

A SIEM answers "what happened and is something happening"; a SOAR answers "do the response, the same way, every time". The distinction is aggregation and correlation against orchestration and automation, and organisations that buy the second before the first automate responses to alerts they do not yet trust.

**118. A**

Two regimes, two regulators, two clocks, one event. The data protection obligation runs to the supervisory authority within 72 hours of awareness unless risk is unlikely; the incident regime runs to the CSIRT with an early warning within 24 hours, a notification within 72 and a final report within a month. Satisfying either does not satisfy the other, and the preparation work is knowing that before the day it matters.

**119. D**

There are two thresholds, and the higher one governs communication to individuals. The authority is notified where the breach is likely to result in *a* risk; the individuals are told where it is likely to result in a *high* risk. The exemptions matter in practice — strong encryption with uncompromised keys is the standard example — because they are what makes the higher threshold meaningful.

**120. B**

Chain of custody is what makes technical findings usable. Without a documented record of who held the evidence, when and why, integrity cannot be demonstrated and the analysis may be inadmissible — which means the forensic work, however good, fails at the only purpose it had. It is a technical requirement in exactly the sense that a hash of an image is.
