# 3. Succeeding in Abstract Reasoning Tests

Abstract reasoning (sometimes called diagrammatic or non-verbal reasoning) is the third pillar of the EPSO reasoning battery, alongside verbal and numerical reasoning. Where verbal reasoning tests your ability to work with written information and numerical reasoning tests your ability to work with figures, tables and data, abstract reasoning strips language and numbers away entirely. EPSO's own description of the test is:

> *"Assesses your ability to identify logical patterns and relationships between concepts, without relying on language, numbers, or spatial orientation."*

In practice, this means the test presents you with figures — circles, triangles, squares, stars, diamonds, arrows — arranged in a sequence or a grid, and asks you to work out the rule that governs how they change from one figure to the next. Because no reading or calculation is involved, the test is designed to be equally fair to candidates of every mother tongue and educational background: the only skill being measured is your ability to spot a pattern.

For this competition, the format is:

| | |
|---|---|
| Questions | 10 |
| Duration | 10 minutes |
| Scoring | 0–10 |
| Pass mark | Corrected together with Numerical Reasoning; combined pass mark **10/20** |

Note that abstract and numerical reasoning are corrected *together*: there is no separate minimum for abstract reasoning on its own, but the ten abstract-reasoning questions and the ten numerical-reasoning questions are added up and compared against a single combined threshold of 10 out of 20. This means a strong abstract reasoning score can compensate for a weaker numerical reasoning score, and vice versa — but you should not rely on that and should aim to perform solidly on both. With only ten minutes for ten questions, the real exam gives you almost exactly one minute per item, so speed and a reliable method matter as much as raw pattern-spotting ability.

> **A note on the notation used in this book.** The real EPSO abstract reasoning test is entirely visual: every question is a picture, not a line of text. This document is plain Markdown, and no image generation is available while writing it, so the practice items in the next chapter use a disciplined Unicode symbol notation instead of drawn figures (the notation is explained in full at the start of that chapter). This is a genuinely useful way to train the underlying skill — scanning for a rule, checking one attribute at a time, eliminating options that break the rule — but it is an approximation, not a pixel-perfect substitute for the real computer-based interface. Before the exam, make sure to also work through EPSO's own official sample tests (see `Referencias.txt`) so that you get used to the actual visual format, the on-screen answer mechanism and the timing under realistic conditions.

## How EPSO Designs Abstract Reasoning Items

Every abstract reasoning item, however it is dressed up, is built from the same two ingredients:

1. **A rule** (or a small set of rules) governing how one or more visual attributes change across a sequence of figures, across the rows and columns of a grid, or across a group of related figures.
2. **A set of answer options**, exactly one of which satisfies every part of the rule, while the others each violate at least one part of it — sometimes obviously, sometimes very subtly.

This is structurally similar to how verbal reasoning items are built (one correct statement, several flawed ones), and the same general discipline applies: your job is not to "see" the right answer by intuition, but to *test* each option methodically against the rule you have identified, and discard every option that fails even one check.

EPSO items are typically presented in one of a few canonical layouts:

- **A sequence**: four or five figures in a row, each one transformed from the previous one according to a rule (or a combination of rules), followed by a blank position you must fill.
- **A matrix**: a 3×3 grid of figures (in the style of Raven's Progressive Matrices) where each row and/or each column follows a consistent rule, and the bottom-right cell is missing.
- **A set of related figures** where four share a hidden common rule and one does not (odd-one-out).

We use all three layouts in the practice chapter, because each one trains a slightly different skill: sequences train you to track *change over time* (or over position), matrices train you to track *two independent rules at once* (row and column), and odd-one-out sets train you to spot a *shared property* without being told in advance what it is.

## The Attributes That Vary

Almost every abstract reasoning item, no matter how it looks, is built by varying one or more of the following attributes. Learning to name these attributes explicitly — rather than vaguely reacting to "does this look right?" — is the single biggest improvement you can make to your accuracy and speed:

- **Shape identity** — which figure is used (circle, triangle, square, star, diamond, arrow…) and whether it cycles, alternates or stays fixed.
- **Count** — how many copies of an element appear in a panel, and whether that count increases, decreases, or follows a fixed step.
- **Rotation / direction** — the orientation of a figure (most easily seen with triangles or arrows), and whether it turns by a fixed angle (45°, 90°…) in a fixed direction each step.
- **Fill / shading** — whether a shape is solid (filled) or outlined (empty), and whether this alternates, follows a row/column rule, or stays constant.
- **Size** — whether elements grow, shrink, or follow a fixed cycle of sizes; sometimes size trades off against another attribute (for example, count going down as size goes up).
- **Position / movement** — where within the frame an element sits, and whether it moves in a consistent direction (for example, stepping clockwise around the four corners).
- **Symmetry** — whether a figure or a grid is mirrored (left-right, top-bottom, or point-symmetric about its centre).
- **Addition or subtraction of elements** — whether new elements are added to (or removed from) a panel as the sequence progresses, sometimes cumulatively.
- **Alternation** — a simple repeating cycle (A-B-A-B, or A-B-C-A-B-C) applied to any of the attributes above.

Simple items vary exactly one of these attributes while holding all the others constant. Harder items combine two, three, or even four attributes simultaneously, each following its own independent rule — and the hardest items combine attributes that interact (an inverse relationship between size and count, for instance), or interleave two separate patterns within a single sequence.

## A Practical Method: Attribute-by-Attribute Elimination

When candidates struggle with abstract reasoning items, it is almost always because they try to judge the whole figure "at a glance" and rely on a gut feeling about which answer "looks right". This works for the easiest items but breaks down quickly once two or three attributes are combined, because a distractor option can easily get most of the figure right and only one small detail wrong. The reliable method is instead:

1. **Scan the whole item first** — read every panel of the sequence, or every cell of the matrix, before looking at the answer options at all. Do not let the options anchor your thinking prematurely.
2. **Name the attributes that are changing.** Go down the list above one at a time and ask, for each attribute, "is this constant, or does it follow a rule?" Most items only vary two or three attributes; the rest stay fixed, and noticing which ones stay fixed is just as useful as spotting which ones move.
3. **For a matrix, check rows and columns separately.** A rule that is not obvious reading left-to-right along a row is often perfectly clear reading top-to-bottom along a column (or vice-versa) — and in the hardest items, the true rule runs along the diagonals.
4. **State the rule in words** before you look at the options — for example, "the shape cycles circle, triangle, square; the count equals the position number." A rule you can state in plain language is a rule you can check quickly and confidently against every option.
5. **Test every option against every part of the rule, not just the part that first catches your eye.** A well-built distractor will satisfy one or two attribute rules and fail only one — if you stop checking as soon as one attribute matches, you will be fooled.
6. **Eliminate rather than select.** Instead of asking "which option looks right?", ask "which options can I rule out, and why?" Cross off every option that breaks at least one rule; whatever survives is the answer. If more than one option survives, you have missed part of the rule and need to look again.

### Distractors: Options That Are "Almost" Right

The wrong options in a well-designed abstract reasoning item are never random. They are built by taking the correct figure and deliberately breaking exactly one attribute rule at a time: one distractor has the right shape and fill but the wrong count; another has the right count and shape but the wrong fill; a third repeats the previous panel outright (as if no rule had been applied at all); a fourth introduces an element that never appears anywhere else in the item. Recognising this design pattern is itself a shortcut: once you have identified the two or three attributes genuinely at play, you can expect to see roughly one flawed option per attribute, plus perhaps one "did nothing" option that simply repeats an earlier panel.

### Worked Example

Consider the following matrix:

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| ● | ●● | ●●● |
| ▲ | ▲▲ | ▲▲▲ |
| ■ | ■■ | ? |

A. ■■■  B. ■■  C. ▲▲▲  D. □□□

Following the method above:

- **Shape**: each *row* uses one shape throughout (circles, then triangles, then squares) — this is a row rule.
- **Count**: each *column* uses one count throughout (1, then 2, then 3) — this is a column rule.
- **Fill**: every figure in the grid is solid — this attribute is constant, not varying.

The missing cell is in the square row and the "3" column, so it must be three filled squares: **■■■**. Checking the options confirms this by elimination: B has the right shape and fill but the wrong count (2, not 3); C has the right count and fill but the wrong shape (it belongs to the triangle row); D has the right shape and count but the wrong fill (outline instead of solid). Only A satisfies all three checks at once. Notice how each wrong option isolates a single broken attribute — this is exactly the "one distractor per attribute" design described above, and you will see it throughout the practice set.

## The EPSO "Logic Types" — a Field-Tested Taxonomy

The attribute list above is a general framework. Ya Semos Europeos (YSE), the Spanish EPSO-preparation community referenced in `Referencias.txt`, publishes a manual built specifically around real EPSO abstract-reasoning items, and its own taxonomy of recurring "logic types" is worth learning by name — recognising which type you're looking at narrows down what to check first:

- **Giro (Rotation).** One or more elements rotate step by step around a real or imaginary axis. Classic culprits: a rotating "windmill" or arrow shape. If it isn't obvious, mentally "grab" the rotating element and turn it yourself, step by step, rather than trying to see the whole rotation at once.
- **Desplazamiento (Shift/movement).** One or more elements slide across the frame from one step to the next — often at *different speeds for different elements in the same item* (e.g. one block moves +1 position per step, another +2, another +3), and elements that slide off one edge often wrap back in from the opposite edge.
- **Giro o desplazamiento combinado (Rotation + shift combined).** Both happen at once, sometimes at different speeds around a shared path (e.g. several elements moving around the perimeter of a square, each at its own pace). When a rule you've found only explains some but not all of the panels, suspect a second, combined rule rather than discarding the first outright.
- **Solapamiento (Overlap).** An element's movement causes it to pass behind or in front of another element, partially hiding it. This is easy to mistake for alternation (an element simply appearing and disappearing) — check whether the "disappearing" element is actually still there, just occluded.
- **Suma o resta de elementos (Addition/subtraction of elements).** Pieces, sides, or sub-areas are added or removed step by step — for example, a shape gaining one side per panel (triangle → square → pentagon → hexagon), often combined with the growing shape also stepping to a new corner of the frame each time.
- **Cambio de colores (Colour/shading change).** Fill cycles through a fixed sequence (EPSO typically limits itself to black / grey / white). Track the cycle position of each *individual* element separately — don't assume every element in the frame shares the same colour-cycle phase.
- **Cambio de tamaño (Size change).** Elements grow or shrink step by step. Size changes are usually visually obvious; if you're genuinely unsure whether something has changed size, it's more likely an optical effect from a neighbouring element than a real size rule.
- **Simetrías (Symmetries).** A (real or imaginary) axis reflects part of the figure onto another part, and the reflected copy may *also* change along some other attribute (commonly colour) as it crosses the axis. In any single item, expect only one axis type (vertical, horizontal, or diagonal) — EPSO does not mix two different symmetry axes within the same item.
- **Órdenes o instrucciones (Embedded instructions).** Part of the figure functions as a hidden "instruction" that dictates how another part behaves — for example, a domino-style dot count in the top half telling you whether the bottom half adds or subtracts a dot, or an arrow's coloured tip signalling that the arrow's position resets. These items reward treating the figure as encoding a small rule-system, not just a picture.
- **Alternativas (Alternating sub-sequences).** Any of the logic types above, but applied on odd/even positions separately (rule A governs panels 1-3-5, a *different* application of a rule governs panels 2-4-6) rather than uniformly across the whole sequence — or grouped in pairs or triples instead of a strict odd/even split. If a single rule seems to explain some panels but is flatly contradicted by others, check whether two interleaved rules are running at once before concluding the item is unsolvable.
- **La lógica desconocida (The unknown logic).** This taxonomy is built from experience, not an exhaustive EPSO specification — you may occasionally meet a rule that doesn't fit any category above. When that happens, extract the rule directly from the panels you're given (as described in the attribute-by-attribute method above) and check that it's consistent with every panel *and* the correct answer, rather than trying to force it into one of the named types.

A few extra field-tested tips from the same source, specific to how this test actually plays out under real conditions:

- **Look at the panels, not the answer options, first.** Unlike verbal reasoning (where the wording of a statement can sometimes hint at whether it is designed to be the correct one), the answer options in abstract reasoning give you no such shortcut — the logic has to come from the sequence or matrix itself.
- **Use your hands, or the on-screen scratch tools, freely.** If a rotation or shift is hard to picture, physically "hold" the element with your fingers and turn or slide it, or sketch the sequence on the notepad/whiteboard the test provides. There is no reward for solving it purely in your head.
- **Let elimination tell you when you're missing a rule.** Correctly identifying one governing rule should usually eliminate two of the wrong options outright, leaving three candidates; find a second rule and you should be down to one. If you're stuck between two options with no way to choose, that itself is the signal that a rule is still hiding — go back and look again rather than guessing blindly.
- **Don't marry a shape to a logic type.** The same visual motif (say, small squares, or circles) can carry a rotation rule in one item and a size or addition/subtraction rule in the next. Judge each item on its own panels, not on what a similar-looking item did last time.
- **Guard your time ruthlessly.** With roughly one minute per question and no partial credit for a half-finished analysis, a firm personal rule — for example, "if I can't state the rule in words within 45-50 seconds, I mark my best guess and move on" — protects the rest of the test from one stubborn item.

## Practice Methods

A few suggestions for building genuine speed and accuracy before exam day:

- Work through the practice set in this book **untimed first**. Force yourself to name the rule in words for every item before selecting an answer — resist the temptation to guess from a gut feeling, even when you suspect you already know the answer.
- Once you are consistently getting items right, start timing yourself. **The real exam gives you roughly one minute per question** (10 questions in 10 minutes) — time yourself against that pace on batches of ten items at a time.
- When you get an item wrong, do not just note the correct letter — go back and identify *which attribute* you missed or misjudged. Over time you will notice you consistently struggle with one or two attribute types (rotation and interleaved sequences are common culprits); once you know your weak spots, you can drill them specifically.
- Practice on EPSO's own official sample tests (linked in `Referencias.txt`) to get used to the real visual figures, the on-screen interface and the genuine time pressure — the text notation in this book is a training tool for the underlying logic, not a replacement for that visual practice.
- As with the other reasoning tests, try to recreate exam conditions: a quiet room, no interruptions, a countdown timer rather than a clock you can glance at leisurely.

## ANSWERS


**1. E**

Three attributes cycle independently: the shape rotates triangle → circle → square (period 3); the fill runs in blocks of two — filled, filled, empty, empty, filled, filled; and the attached arrow rotates 45° anticlockwise every panel, completing a four-step compass cycle. Panel 6 falls on "square" in the shape cycle, in the "filled" half of the fill block, with the arrow continuing on to ↗ — a filled square with an up-right arrow, exactly option E (confirmed directly against the scan: E's arrow was easy to miss because it sits right at the crop edge).

**2. C**

The bent pointer rotates 90° clockwise each panel (↑→↓←↑→); the circle's shading cycles black → grey → white; and the circle's position independently steps one slot anticlockwise around the pointer each panel. Panel 6 needs the pointer at →, the circle white, and the circle back in the slot it occupied at panel 2 — option C.

**3. B**

The triangle (always left as an outline, never filled) keeps rotating 45° clockwise every panel, including the panel where it's hidden; the square's fill cycles black → striped → white → black…; and which shape is the big "outer" one versus the small "inner" one alternates every panel. **Rotation / direction:** two symbol families are used:
