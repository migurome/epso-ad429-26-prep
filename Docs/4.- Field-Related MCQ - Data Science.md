# 4. Succeeding in the Data Science Field-Related MCQ Test

The Field-Related Multiple Choice Test is the second hurdle in the EPSO/AD/429/26 (4) competition, and the first one that is specific to the Data Science field rather than to general cognitive aptitude. Where the reasoning tests of Phase 1 measure how you think, this test measures what you know: your professional command of the concepts, trade-offs and vocabulary a data scientist works with day to day, at the level expected of an AD7 administrator designing data architectures, pipelines and analytical systems for the European institutions.

Format, in brief: **30 questions, 40 minutes, scored 0–30, pass mark 15/30**. Passing the mark is necessary but not sufficient — because this stage is classifying rather than purely eliminatory, EPSO only carries forward the best-scoring candidates in the Data Science field, up to a multiple of the number of posts available (184 posts in this field, per the competition notice). In practice this means 15/30 is a floor to clear comfortably, not the actual target; your real goal is a score that holds up well against your competitor pool. The test is taken in your **Language 2** (typically English, French or German), not your strongest language, which adds a layer of terminology pressure on top of the technical content itself.

Unlike the verbal reasoning test discussed in the previous chapter, there is **no passage of text** to analyse here: each item is a self-contained question drawing on your own subject-matter knowledge. This changes the shape of your preparation substantially — it is less about technique and more about making sure your working knowledge of a defined set of topic areas is accurate, current, and precise enough to withstand deliberately similar-sounding wrong answers.

In the following, we will cover:

- How field-related MCQ items are typically designed, and the shapes wrong answers tend to take

## How These Questions Are Designed

Well-constructed technical MCQ items rarely rely on obscure trivia or version-specific syntax — that would be unfair across such a broad field, and easy to get wrong even for an expert. Instead, they test whether you can tell apart two concepts that are genuinely easy to confuse under time pressure. Recognising the typical shapes wrong answers take will save you time and help you avoid the traps set for the unwary:

- **The reversed pair.** Two related concepts (ETL/ELT, taxonomy/ontology, data lake/data warehouse) are swapped, so the distractor describes concept B correctly but attaches concept A's name to it.
- **The half-truth.** A statement that starts out correct and then quietly introduces a false or overreaching claim (for example, "a very small p-value proves the effect is large").
- **The outdated or oversimplified best practice.** A claim that used to be, or sounds like it should be, good practice, but that mainstream consensus has since qualified or superseded (for example, treating notebook accuracy as sufficient evidence of production readiness).
- **The category error.** An answer that correctly describes a real concept, just not the one the question actually asked about (for example, describing class imbalance when asked about training–serving skew).
- **The common misconception.** A statement that "feels" right because it is a widely repeated but technically incorrect interpretation (the classic misreading of what a p-value measures).

When you are unsure between two options, ask yourself: which one names the actual mechanism or root cause, and which one only sounds plausible? The correct answer in a well-designed item is nearly always the one that is both technically precise and addresses the real underlying issue, not just a symptom of it.

Each is addressed explicitly below.

## 1. Data Engineering and Distributed Ecosystems

At its core, data engineering is about moving and reshaping data reliably at scale, and this is the area where the **ETL vs. ELT** distinction is most tested. ETL (Extract, Transform, Load) transforms data in a dedicated processing layer before it reaches its destination; ELT (Extract, Load, Transform) instead loads raw data straight into the target platform — typically a cloud warehouse or lakehouse — and lets that platform's own compute perform the transformation. ELT has become dominant as warehouses have grown powerful enough to transform data cheaply in place, but ETL remains preferable when the target system should not be trusted with raw or sensitive data, or lacks the compute to transform economically.

A parallel distinction is **batch vs. streaming** processing: batch jobs process data in scheduled, bounded chunks (an hourly load, a nightly refresh), trading latency for simplicity and cost efficiency, while streaming systems process events continuously as they arrive, trading complexity for near-real-time freshness. **Change data capture (CDC)** bridges the two worlds, capturing only the rows that changed in a source system (often by reading its transaction log) so downstream systems can be kept incrementally in sync without re-processing everything.

Storage architecture is the other pillar: **data warehouses** hold structured, curated data under schema-on-write for fast, governed querying; **data lakes** hold raw and semi-structured data cheaply under schema-on-read for maximum flexibility; a **lakehouse** tries to combine warehouse-grade transactions and query performance on top of lake-cheap storage. Finally, robust pipelines must be **idempotent** — safely re-runnable after a failure without duplicating or corrupting results — and must account for **distributed join skew**, where a handful of overloaded partition keys make a few tasks (and therefore the whole job) run far longer than the rest.

## 2. Advanced Statistical Modelling

This area probes whether you can reason about a model's real-world reliability, not just recite formulas. **Overfitting** occurs when a model learns noise specific to its training data rather than the generalisable signal, producing a large gap between training and held-out performance; the usual remedies are regularisation, more representative data, simpler model classes, and rigorous cross-validation. **Data leakage** is a related but distinct failure: information from outside the legitimate training scope (often statistics computed on the full dataset before splitting, or a feature that indirectly encodes the target) contaminates training and produces unrealistically optimistic — and unreproducible — results. Correct **train/test splitting** must respect the data's structure: a random shuffle is fine for independent, identically distributed data, but for time series it lets the model "see" the future, so a chronological split is required instead.

A frequently tested pair is **accuracy vs. calibration**. Accuracy asks how often predictions are right; calibration asks whether predicted probabilities match observed frequencies (a model that assigns 0.7 to events that occur 70% of the time is well calibrated, even if its accuracy is unremarkable). A model can be accurate but poorly calibrated, or vice versa, and each failure has different operational consequences — calibration matters most when the predicted probability itself is used downstream, such as in risk scoring.

On the inferential side, remember that a small **p-value** signals that an effect is unlikely to be due to chance, not that it is large or practically important — that is what effect size measures. The **bias–variance tradeoff** ties these ideas together: increasing model complexity typically reduces bias but increases variance, so generalisation error is minimised at some intermediate point, not at either extreme.

## 3. Data Architecture and Semantic Technologies

Semantic technologies exist to make data meaning explicit and machine-actionable across organisational boundaries — a recurring concern for an institution that must reconcile data from 27 member states. The foundational building block is the **RDF** (Resource Description Framework) triple: a subject–predicate–object statement (for example, "Paris — isCapitalOf — France") that lets facts be represented as a graph rather than a table. **SPARQL** is the query language for RDF graphs, playing roughly the role SQL plays for relational tables. Entities and concepts in this world are identified by persistent, dereferenceable **URIs** rather than local database keys, which is precisely what allows the same concept to be unambiguously referenced from multiple, independently run systems — the essence of **linked data**.

The most commonly tested distinction here is **taxonomy vs. ontology**. A taxonomy is a hierarchical classification of concepts using simple broader/narrower relationships (a tree or near-tree structure); an ontology goes further, formally defining classes, properties, and logical relationships and constraints between them, which supports richer machine reasoning and consistency checking. **SKOS** (Simple Knowledge Organization System) is the standard vehicle for publishing taxonomies, thesauri and other **controlled vocabularies** as linked data, using relations such as broader, narrower and related. Finally, **DCAT** (Data Catalog Vocabulary) — and its European profile, DCAT-AP — is the standard used to describe and publish dataset metadata in open-data portals such as data.europa.eu, enabling datasets to be discovered and compared consistently across catalogues. Together, these pieces are what makes a **knowledge graph** more than just a database with arrows.

## 4. HPC and Parallel Processing

This area tests whether you understand the limits of throwing more hardware at a problem. **Amdahl's Law** is the starting point: the maximum speedup achievable by parallelising a program is capped by the fraction of the work that must remain serial — even with an unlimited number of processors, that serial portion still has to run sequentially, so speedup asymptotically approaches a ceiling rather than growing without bound. This is why profiling to find the actual bottleneck matters more than simply adding compute.

The **roofline model** formalises that bottleneck-hunting: a workload's achievable performance is bounded either by the processor's peak arithmetic throughput or by memory bandwidth, depending on its arithmetic intensity (operations performed per byte moved). A **memory-bound** workload is limited by how fast data can be moved to and from memory, so faster processors alone barely help; a compute-bound workload is limited by arithmetic throughput instead, which is where faster or more processors do pay off. **GPUs** deliver their largest gains specifically on data-parallel, compute-bound workloads — applying the same simple operation across large, regular arrays — and much less on workloads with heavy branching or fine-grained shared-state access.

Scalability itself splits into two regimes, and this is also where **batch vs. streaming** trade-offs resurface at the infrastructure level: **strong scaling** measures how runtime falls as more processors are applied to a fixed-size problem, while **weak scaling** measures performance as both the problem size and processor count grow together, keeping the work per processor roughly constant. Both regimes are undermined by **load imbalance**, where work is unevenly distributed across workers so that a few slow "stragglers" make the whole job wait, regardless of how much total compute is available.

## 5. Advanced Data Integration and APIs

Integration questions test whether you design interfaces that stay correct when things go wrong — networks time out, consumers retry, and producers change. Good **REST API design** favours resource-oriented URLs, correct use of HTTP verbs and status codes, and predictable, well-documented behaviour under failure. A client-supplied **idempotency key** is the standard defence against duplicate side effects: if a request (say, "create payment") is retried after a timeout, the server recognises the key and returns the original result instead of performing the operation twice — a payment retry must never double-charge, just as a duplicate event must never double-count.

**Pagination** matters at scale: cursor-based (keyset) pagination is generally preferred over simple offset-based pagination for large or frequently-changing datasets, because it avoids skipping or duplicating rows when records are inserted or deleted between requests, and its performance does not degrade for deep pages the way scanning-and-skipping does.

For systems that integrate multiple services, **contract testing** (typically consumer-driven) lets a team verify — before deployment, without a full end-to-end environment — that a provider's change will not break something its consumers actually rely on, catching breaking changes early. **API versioning** exists for the same reason: it lets producers evolve an interface without silently breaking existing clients, who have no way to opt in only to compatible changes. Finally, event-driven integrations built on **queues** typically offer only *at-least-once* delivery rather than exactly-once, so consumers must be designed to tolerate and safely handle duplicate messages.

## 6. MLOps and Production Model Operations

The central lesson of this area is that **a model that performs well in a notebook is not automatically production-ready** — the distinction between offline model performance and production readiness is one of the most consistently tested ideas in this field. Production readiness requires reproducible training, documented data lineage, versioning of data, code and model together, automated monitoring and alerting, a rollback plan, and evidence that performance holds up on live, possibly shifting data — none of which a single offline accuracy number demonstrates. A **model registry** operationalises part of this: it centrally versions trained models, tracks their lineage and metadata, and manages their lifecycle stage (staging, production, archived, and so on), giving teams a single source of truth about what is actually running.

A **feature store** addresses a related failure mode: it computes, stores and serves features consistently for both training and real-time inference, so the same feature logic and values are used in both contexts. Without one, it is easy to introduce **training–serving skew** — a discrepancy between how a feature behaves at training time versus serving time — which can silently degrade a model's real-world performance even though nothing changed about the model itself.

Once deployed, models must be watched for **data drift** (also called covariate shift): a change in the distribution of production input data relative to the training data, which can erode prediction quality with no code change at all. Finally, **explainability** techniques (such as SHAP values) matter operationally, not just academically: they let stakeholders and auditors understand what drove a given prediction, supporting debugging, trust, and compliance with transparency expectations in a public-sector setting.

## 7. Analytics, Visualisation, and Self-Service Insights

A chart is never neutral: the same numbers, shown differently, can lead an audience to different — and sometimes wrong — conclusions, which is why this area rewards deliberate design choices over default ones. For precise comparison across many categories, a sorted bar chart generally beats a pie chart, because position along a common baseline is easier for the human eye to judge accurately than angle or area. Uncertainty should be shown, not hidden: a forecast presented purely as a single point invites false precision, whereas pairing it with a confidence or prediction band helps decision-makers calibrate how much weight to place on it.

The distinction between a **dashboard and a governed metric definition** is one of the more subtle but important ideas here. Two teams can build dashboards from the very same underlying tables and still report different numbers for "active users" or "on-time delivery" simply because each team applied its own filters, time windows, or business logic. The fix is not a better chart — it is a single, documented, centrally governed metric definition that every dashboard consumes, so numbers reconcile across reports.

Related to this is **Simpson's paradox**: an aggregated trend can reverse, or even contradict, the trend seen in every one of its subgroups, typically because the relative weighting of the subgroups shifted — a trap for anyone who reads only the headline number without checking the underlying mix. Good self-service analytics practice therefore pairs easy data access with a shared semantic layer, clear denominators, and visible handling of missing data, rather than leaving every user to reinvent business logic independently.

## 8. Data Governance and Stewardship

Governance is not bureaucracy for its own sake — done well, it is what makes data usable, trustworthy and safe to share, and a strong answer in this area typically names an owner, points to a standard, and explains how uncertainty or risk is being managed. **Data lineage** is the documented trail of where a piece of data originated and how it was transformed and moved as it travelled from source to consumption; without it, neither debugging a quality issue nor demonstrating compliance is realistically possible. A **data owner** or **steward** is the person accountable for a given dataset's quality, appropriate use and access decisions — the point of contact when something about that dataset is unclear or wrong, not necessarily the person who wrote the pipeline that produces it.

Access follows the **principle of least privilege**: users and systems should receive only the minimum access needed for their role, nothing more, which limits the blast radius of both mistakes and malicious action. **Data classification** schemes tier data by sensitivity, and personal data in particular must be handled under principles such as purpose limitation, data minimisation and a documented legal basis for processing, consistent with GDPR — directly identifying data is never "just data" and should never default to open publication. Finally, **quality accountability** is best embedded structurally: a named owner plus automated data-quality checks built into the producing pipeline, with alerting when they fail, beats relying on whoever happens to notice a problem first.

An idempotency key lets the server detect that a retried request is a duplicate of one it already processed, so it returns the original result rather than repeating the side effect (e.g. charging twice).

**23. D**

Keyset pagination anchors each page to a value (a cursor) rather than a row offset, so it is unaffected by concurrent inserts/deletes shifting row positions, and it avoids the performance cost of scanning-and-skipping large offsets.

**24. D**

Consumer-driven contract tests encode what consumers actually rely on from a provider's API and run them against the provider in CI, catching breaking changes early without needing a full integrated environment.

**25. C**

Most message brokers default to at-least-once delivery — a message can be redelivered after a timeout or a failed acknowledgement — so consumers must be designed to handle duplicates safely (typically via idempotent processing), since true exactly-once delivery is difficult and rarely guaranteed end-to-end.

**26. D**

Without versioning, clients built against the old contract have no way to keep using it while they migrate, so a breaking change can fail or silently corrupt their behaviour the moment it ships.

### Topic 6 — MLOps and Production Model Operations

**27. A**

A model registry centralises versioning, lineage/metadata tracking, and lifecycle-stage management (staging, production, archived) for trained models — the backbone of reproducible, auditable model deployment.

**28. B**

A feature store's core value is consistency: the same feature computation logic and values are used at both training and serving time, which directly reduces training–serving skew.

**29. D**

Training–serving skew is specifically a mismatch between how a feature is computed or behaves in the training pipeline versus the serving path — a subtle, code-level cause of production performance degradation that offline metrics won't reveal.

**30. A**

Notebook accuracy says nothing about reproducibility, lineage, monitoring, alerting, or how the model will behave on data distributions that shift after deployment — all of which are required for genuine production readiness.

**31. A**

Data drift is a shift in the input data's distribution between training and production, independent of any change to the model's code — it can silently erode performance and must be actively monitored.

**32. A**

Explainability techniques expose which features drove individual predictions or overall model behaviour, which is essential for stakeholder trust, debugging, and meeting transparency obligations in a regulated, public-sector context — not for improving raw accuracy.

### Topic 7 — Analytics, Visualisation, and Self-Service Insights

**33. A**

Perceptual studies consistently show that position along a common baseline (as in a sorted bar chart) is judged more accurately by viewers than angle or area (as in a pie chart), especially once the number of categories grows.

**34. D**

Divergent numbers from the same underlying data almost always trace back to inconsistent business logic (different definitions, filters, or time windows) applied independently by each team; the durable fix is a single governed metric definition, not a tooling change.

**35. C**

Showing uncertainty alongside a point estimate (e.g. a confidence or prediction band) helps decision-makers avoid treating a forecast as more precise or certain than it actually is.

**36. A**

This is a classic Simpson's paradox pattern: the aggregate metric improves even though every subgroup worsened, because the relative volume (weighting) of the subgroups shifted between periods — a mix-shift effect invisible in the headline number alone.

**37. A**

Without a shared semantic layer, different users independently reinvent filters and business logic over the same raw tables, so reports that should agree end up producing conflicting numbers.

### Topic 8 — Data Governance and Stewardship

**38. D**

Lineage is the traceable record of a data item's origin and every transformation and movement it underwent on its way to being consumed — essential for debugging, trust and compliance.

**39. D**

The data owner/steward is accountable for quality, appropriate use and access decisions for a dataset, and is the go-to contact for questions about it — a governance role, not necessarily a technical/engineering one.

**40. B**

Least privilege means granting only the minimum access required for a role or task, which limits exposure if credentials are misused or a mistake occurs — the opposite of the "give everyone admin access" approach in option A.

**41. D**

Directly identifying personal data warrants a higher sensitivity classification with corresponding access controls, and its processing should follow core data-protection principles such as purpose limitation, minimisation, and a documented legal basis — not open-by-default publication.

**42. A**

Durable quality accountability comes from pairing a clearly named owner with automated, alerting data-quality checks embedded in the pipeline itself, rather than relying on ad hoc, after-the-fact discovery of problems.

### Topic 1 — Data Engineering and Distributed Ecosystems

**43. B**

Streaming is chosen when the business need genuinely requires near-real-time freshness; its added complexity (state management, ordering, backpressure) is a deliberate trade-off for latency, not a default choice made for its own sake.

**44. C**

Partition pruning lets the query engine skip reading files/partitions that fall outside the filter predicate entirely, so a date-range query only scans the relevant slice of the table instead of the whole dataset.

**45. C**

"Exactly-once" is a processing guarantee usually implemented via idempotent writes, deduplication, or transactional commit of offsets together with output — it does not require preventing duplicate delivery at the network level, only ensuring duplicates have no additional effect.

**46. B**

Idempotent tasks make backfills safe: re-running a task for a given historical partition simply overwrites it with the same correct result, so operators can safely re-run backfills without fear of duplicating or corrupting data.

**47. D**

Schema-on-read defers schema interpretation to query time, so producers can add optional fields without a coordinated migration — but this pushes the burden of handling missing/extra fields gracefully onto every downstream consumer.

### Topic 2 — Advanced Statistical Modelling

**48. C**

Multicollinearity inflates the variance of the correlated coefficients, making them unstable and hard to interpret individually (small data changes can swing them a lot), even though the model's overall predictions can remain reasonably accurate.

**49. D**

Higher statistical power means a lower chance of missing a real effect (a lower false-negative rate), which requires more evidence — i.e. a larger sample — to achieve, all else being equal.

**50. B**

A confidence interval is about the uncertainty of the estimated mean; a prediction interval is about the uncertainty of one new individual observation, which is inherently more variable, so prediction intervals are wider than confidence intervals at the same level.

**51. A**

This is the classic accuracy paradox: with 0.5% positives, always predicting the majority class scores 99.5% accuracy while being useless at the actual task, which is exactly why precision/recall/F1 on the minority class matter for imbalanced problems.

**52. C**

Random folds can leak future information into training when the data has a time order, inflating apparent performance; walk-forward (time-series) validation preserves the chronological training-then-testing structure the model will actually face in deployment.

### Topic 3 — Data Architecture and Semantic Technologies

**53. B**

Persistent, dereferenceable URIs give every system a shared, resolvable way to refer to the same real-world entity, which is what allows independently run systems to interoperate around common facts — the essence of linked data.

**54. A**

What elevates a graph to a "knowledge graph" is the layer of formal semantics (ontology-defined classes, properties and constraints) that supports automated consistency checking and richer reasoning, beyond what an unlabelled or loosely-typed graph structure alone provides.

**55. D**

DCAT-AP standardises dataset-metadata description across member states' portals, which is what makes cross-border discovery and comparison of datasets on platforms like data.europa.eu practically possible.

**56. B**

The golden record is the single, reconciled, most-trustworthy version of an entity's data, produced by resolving duplicates and conflicts across the multiple source systems that each hold a partial or inconsistent view of it.

**57. C**

RDF plus ontologies and SPARQL is optimised for standardised, cross-organisation interoperability and formal reasoning; property graphs are often simpler to model with rich attributes and are commonly faster for traversal-heavy queries within a single application's own data.

### Topic 4 — HPC and Parallel Processing

**58. D**

Under the roofline model, a workload sitting below the memory-bandwidth roof is memory-bound, so the effective fix is reducing data movement or improving reuse (caching, blocking), not simply adding more compute, which will not relieve a memory bottleneck.

**59. C**

Weak scaling grows the problem size in step with the processor count, so the workload per processor stays roughly constant; the metric of interest is whether performance (throughput) holds up, not how much wall-clock time drops for a fixed-size problem (that is strong scaling).

**60. C**

Stragglers are the textbook symptom of load imbalance: if work isn't evenly spread, the job's completion time is set by its slowest task, regardless of how quickly the other workers finished.

**61. D**

Checkpointing lets a long job resume from its last saved state after a failure, avoiding a costly full restart — its purpose is fault tolerance and efficient recovery, not accuracy or automatic parallelisation.

**62. C**

Higher data reuse per byte loaded means higher arithmetic intensity (operations per byte), which the roofline model places further toward the compute-bound region — Workload X reuses values, so it is more likely to be compute-bound than Workload Y, which touches each value only once.

### Topic 5 — Advanced Data Integration and APIs

**63. C**

Webhooks let the provider push events as they happen, avoiding the wasted requests, latency, and "is there anything new yet?" overhead inherent in polling on a fixed schedule.

**64. C**

GraphQL's core proposition is letting the client declare exactly the fields it needs in one request, directly addressing the over-fetching (and under-fetching, requiring multiple round trips) that a fixed REST response shape can cause.

**65. B**

A circuit breaker stops calling a failing downstream dependency for a cooldown period, protecting both the caller (from piling up slow, doomed requests) and the struggling downstream service (from further load), then probes cautiously before fully reopening.

**66. A**

Rate limiting exists to protect a shared resource from being overwhelmed by any one consumer (accidental or malicious), preserving availability and a fair share of capacity for everyone else using the API.

**67. B**

Adding a new optional field with a default, while leaving existing fields untouched, is the textbook backward/forward-compatible schema change: old readers simply ignore the unfamiliar field, and new readers can typically still parse old messages.

### Topic 6 — MLOps and Production Model Operations

**68. B**

A canary release routes only a small slice of real traffic to the new version first, letting teams observe its real-world behaviour and catch problems before committing the rest of production traffic to it.

**69. A**

Shadow deployment runs the new model alongside the current one on live traffic for comparison purposes only — its predictions are logged, not served to users — which lets teams validate real-world behaviour with zero user-facing risk.

**70. D**

Data drift is a shift in the input distribution; concept drift is a shift in the actual input-output relationship, meaning even the same inputs may now genuinely warrant a different prediction — a more fundamental and often more serious failure mode than data drift alone.

**71. D**

An evaluation gate exists precisely because retraining does not guarantee improvement — a data-quality problem or an unfavourable shift in the new training window could silently produce a worse model, which the gate is designed to catch before it reaches production.

**72. C**

Because labelled ground truth for live predictions is often delayed (or never fully available), watching the input feature distributions gives an earlier, indirect warning sign of drift than waiting to accumulate enough labelled outcomes to measure accuracy directly.

### Topic 7 — Analytics, Visualisation, and Self-Service Insights

**74. B**

A log scale compresses the visual distance between large values, so patterns among the bulk of lower- and mid-range observations become visible instead of being squashed flat by a handful of extreme high values on a linear scale.

**75. D**

Because a meaningful share of viewers cannot reliably distinguish red from green, robust charts encode the key distinction redundantly (shape, pattern, direct labelling) in addition to using a colour-blind-safe palette, rather than relying on colour — especially red/green — alone.

**76. B**

Pre-aggregation trades some flexibility and upfront storage/compute for much faster read-time response, since common slice-and-dice queries hit already-computed summaries instead of scanning and aggregating raw rows on every interaction.

**77. C**

Cohort analysis groups users by a shared starting point (like signup month) and tracks each group's behaviour over time, which isolates genuine behavioural trends from the confounding effect of an overall user base that is constantly growing or changing composition.

### Topic 8 — Data Governance and Stewardship

**78. C**

Data minimisation means collecting and retaining only what is actually necessary for the stated, legitimate purpose — not gathering data speculatively "in case it's useful," and not keeping it longer than that purpose requires.

**79. C**

Honouring an erasure request in practice requires knowing everywhere that individual's data lives — every system, copy and downstream derivative — which is precisely what accurate, maintained data lineage makes tractable; without it, erasure cannot be verified as complete.

**80. B**

A retention policy ties how long data is kept to the legal/business justification for keeping it, limiting the risk and liability carried by data that has outlived its original purpose — rather than defaulting to indefinite retention "just in case."

**81. A**

Anonymisation is meant to be irreversible, permanently severing the link to an individual; pseudonymisation only substitutes an artificial identifier for the real one, so re-identification remains possible for whoever holds the separately-kept mapping — which is why pseudonymised data is still treated as personal data under most frameworks.

**82. C**

FAIR is about making data and its metadata easy to find, retrievable under clear (not necessarily unrestricted) conditions, structured for interoperability with other tools and data, and documented well enough to be reused correctly — it does not mandate that every dataset be made fully public.
