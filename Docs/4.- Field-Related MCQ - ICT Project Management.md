# 4. Succeeding in the ICT Project Management Field-Related MCQ Test

The Field-Related Multiple Choice Test is the second hurdle in the EPSO/AD/429/26 (4) competition, and the first one that is specific to the ICT Project Management field rather than to general cognitive aptitude. Where the reasoning tests of Phase 1 measure how you think, this test measures what you know: your professional command of the concepts, frameworks and vocabulary an ICT project manager works with day to day, at the level expected of an AD7 administrator planning, running and governing digital projects and programmes for the European institutions.

Format, in brief: **30 questions, 40 minutes, scored 0–30, pass mark 15/30**. Passing the mark is necessary but not sufficient — because this stage is classifying rather than purely eliminatory, EPSO only carries forward the best-scoring candidates in the ICT Project Management field, up to a multiple of the number of posts available (228 posts in this field, per the competition notice). In practice this means 15/30 is a floor to clear comfortably, not the actual target; your real goal is a score that holds up well against your competitor pool. The test is taken in your **Language 2** (typically English, French or German), not your strongest language, which adds a layer of terminology pressure on top of the technical content itself.

Unlike the verbal reasoning test discussed in the previous chapter, there is **no passage of text** to analyse here: each item is a self-contained question drawing on your own subject-matter knowledge. This changes the shape of your preparation substantially — it is less about technique and more about making sure your working knowledge of a defined set of topic areas is accurate, current, and precise enough to withstand deliberately similar-sounding wrong answers.

In the following, we will cover:

- How field-related MCQ items are typically designed, and the shapes wrong answers tend to take

## How These Questions Are Designed

Well-constructed technical MCQ items rarely rely on obscure trivia or version-specific jargon — that would be unfair across such a broad field, and easy to get wrong even for an expert practitioner. Instead, they test whether you can tell apart two concepts that are genuinely easy to confuse under time pressure. Recognising the typical shapes wrong answers take will save you time and help you avoid the traps set for the unwary:

- **The reversed pair.** Two related concepts (risk/issue, mitigate/transfer, programme/portfolio) are swapped, so the distractor describes concept B correctly but attaches concept A's name to it.
- **The half-truth.** A statement that starts out correct and then quietly introduces a false or overreaching claim (for example, "a project that is under budget is, by that fact alone, a successful project").
- **The outdated or oversimplified best practice.** A claim that used to be, or sounds like it should be, good practice, but that mainstream consensus has since qualified or superseded (for example, treating a fully populated Gantt chart as sufficient evidence that a project is genuinely under control).
- **The category error.** An answer that correctly describes a real concept, just not the one the question actually asked about (for example, describing an assumption when the question actually asked about a dependency).
- **The common misconception.** A statement that "feels" right because it is a widely repeated but technically incorrect interpretation (the classic misreading of what a RACI matrix's "Accountable" role actually means).

When you are unsure between two options, ask yourself: which one names the actual mechanism or root cause, and which one only sounds plausible? The correct answer in a well-designed item is nearly always the one that is both technically precise and addresses the real underlying issue, not just a symptom of it.

## 1. Project Management Fundamentals and Life Cycle

Every project management framework, whatever its brand name, organises work around some version of a life cycle. The most widely referenced generic model — used by PMI's PMBOK Guide — describes five process groups that a project cycles through, sometimes repeatedly across phases: Initiating (defining and authorising the project or a phase), Planning (elaborating scope, schedule, cost, quality and risk into an actionable plan), Executing (doing the work the plan describes), Monitoring & Controlling (tracking performance and directing corrective action), and Closing (formally finishing the project or phase and capturing lessons). These are process groups, not a rigid sequence — Monitoring & Controlling in particular runs concurrently with Executing throughout the project's life, not as a discrete step tacked on at the end.

For this competition, one framework deserves special attention: **PM²**, the project management methodology developed and maintained by the European Commission's Centre of Excellence in Project Management (CoEPM²) within DG DIGIT, and made freely available to all EU institutions, member states and the public under the Open PM² initiative. PM² organises a project into four sequential phases — Initiating, Planning, Executing and Closing — with **Monitoring & Control** running as a continuous activity across all four rather than as a fifth phase in the sequence. PM² pairs each phase with defined roles (Project Owner, Business Manager, Solution Provider, Project Manager, Project Steering Committee) and a set of standard artefacts (Project Charter, Business Case, Project Handbook, Project Work Plan) that give EU project governance a common vocabulary across Directorates-General.

The final recurring distinction is **project vs. programme vs. portfolio**. A project is a temporary endeavour with a defined scope and end date, delivering a specific output; a programme is a coordinated group of related projects (and other work) managed together to realise benefits not obtainable by managing them individually; a portfolio is the even broader collection of projects, programmes and other work grouped to achieve strategic objectives, prioritised and balanced against organisational capacity rather than delivered to a fixed scope. A programme manager coordinates interdependencies and benefits across projects; a portfolio manager makes investment and prioritisation decisions across the whole collection.

## 2. Agile and Hybrid Delivery Approaches

Agile frameworks structure delivery around short, iterative cycles rather than a single upfront plan, and Scrum is the most widely tested example. Scrum defines exactly three **roles**: the Product Owner (owns and prioritises the product backlog, maximising the value delivered), the Scrum Master (accountable for the team's effective use of Scrum, removing impediments — not a traditional project manager who assigns tasks), and the Developers (the cross-functional group that builds the increment). Its **events** are the Sprint itself (a fixed-length iteration, typically one to four weeks), Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum, the Sprint Review (inspecting the increment with stakeholders) and the Sprint Retrospective (inspecting how the team works, not what it built). Its **artifacts** are the Product Backlog, the Sprint Backlog and the Increment, each paired with a commitment (respectively the Product Goal, the Sprint Goal, and the Definition of Done).

**Kanban** takes a different, flow-based approach: rather than working in fixed-length iterations, a Kanban team visualises work on a board and limits **work in progress (WIP)** at each stage, so that a bottleneck becomes immediately visible as items pile up behind a capped column, rather than staying hidden until a review meeting. Kanban prescribes no fixed roles or events of its own; it is typically layered on top of an existing process. **Velocity** — the amount of backlog (usually measured in story points) a Scrum team completes per sprint, averaged over recent sprints — is used by that same team to forecast how much future work it can realistically commit to; it is a team-specific, empirically-derived planning input, not a productivity score for comparing different teams against each other.

Because EU institutional projects often sit inside procurement, financial and governance cycles built around fixed milestones and deliverables, pure Scrum is rarely adopted wholesale. **Hybrid delivery** blends agile execution (iterative sprints, backlogs, frequent demonstrable increments) inside an overarching, more traditional governance structure (phase gates, a fixed contractual scope, formal steering-committee sign-off) — for example, running agile sprints to build a solution while still tracking overall progress against a PM²-style project plan and reporting to a Steering Committee at fixed milestones.

## 3. Scope, Schedule and Cost Management

Scope, schedule and cost are the three classic constraints of the "iron triangle", and this area tests whether you can use the standard tools that keep them under control. The **Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)** decomposes the total scope of a project into progressively smaller, manageable components; its lowest-level elements, **work packages**, are what gets scheduled, costed, resourced and assigned. A WBS organises deliverables, not chronological activities — sequencing and timing come later, in the schedule.

The **critical path method (CPM)** builds a network diagram of activities and their dependencies to identify the **critical path**: the longest sequence of dependent activities, which determines the shortest possible project duration. Any delay to an activity on the critical path delays the whole project; activities not on the critical path carry **float** (or slack) — the amount of time an activity can be delayed without affecting the project's overall finish date. An activity with zero float is, by definition, on the critical path.

**Earned Value Management (EVM)** measures performance objectively by comparing three figures at a given point in time: **Planned Value (PV)**, the budgeted cost of work scheduled to have been done; **Earned Value (EV)**, the budgeted cost of the work actually completed; and **Actual Cost (AC)**, what was really spent to do it. From these, the **Cost Performance Index (CPI = EV/AC)** shows cost efficiency (above 1 means under budget for the work done), and the **Schedule Performance Index (SPI = EV/PV)** shows schedule efficiency (above 1 means ahead of schedule) — critically, SPI measures schedule performance in cost/value terms, not in calendar days.

Finally, **three-point (PERT) estimating** produces a more defensible duration or cost estimate than a single guess by combining an optimistic (O), most likely (M) and pessimistic (P) estimate into a weighted average, typically E = (O + 4M + P) / 6, which gives the most-likely value four times the weight of the two extremes.

## 4. Risk and Issue Management

A **risk** is an uncertain future event or condition that, if it occurs, would have a positive or negative effect on the project's objectives — it has not happened yet, and may never happen. An **issue** is different in kind, not just degree: it is a problem that has already occurred and now requires active management to resolve. This distinction is tested constantly, because the correct response to each is different — risks are planned for in advance through response strategies, while issues are logged, assigned an owner, and tracked to closure through an issue log.

Identified risks are recorded in a **risk register**, typically alongside a **probability-impact matrix** that scores each risk on its likelihood of occurring and the severity of its effect if it does, so that limited management attention can be focused on the highest-scoring risks first rather than spread evenly across every risk identified. Four standard **risk response strategies** apply to threats: **avoid** (eliminate the threat entirely, typically by changing the plan so the risk can no longer occur), **mitigate** (reduce the probability or impact, without eliminating it), **transfer** (shift the financial or delivery consequence to a third party, for example through insurance or a fixed-price contract with a supplier), and **accept** (consciously decide to do nothing proactive, usually because the risk is low-priority or a response would cost more than the risk itself — sometimes paired with a contingency reserve for if it occurs).

Two further concepts complete the picture, together forming what is commonly logged as a **RAID log** (Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies): an **assumption** is a factor believed to be true for planning purposes without proof, which introduces risk if it turns out to be false; a **dependency** is a relationship where one activity, team or deliverable relies on another being completed or available first, and can be internal (between two work packages of the same project) or external (on a supplier, another programme, or a decision outside the project's control).

## 5. Stakeholder Management and Governance

Governance is about who decides what, and stakeholder management is about knowing who needs to be informed, consulted or satisfied along the way. The **RACI matrix** is the standard tool for clarifying roles on a given deliverable or decision: **Responsible** (the person or people who actually do the work), **Accountable** (the single person ultimately answerable for the outcome being correct and complete — there should be exactly one Accountable per task, even if several people are Responsible), **Consulted** (whose input is sought, typically two-way, before a decision), and **Informed** (who is told afterwards, one-way). A common misreading treats "Responsible" and "Accountable" as interchangeable; in a well-run RACI they are frequently different people, and ambiguity over who is Accountable is a classic governance failure.

Project **governance structures** typically place a **Project Steering Committee** (or project board) above the Project Manager: a small group of senior stakeholders — in PM², typically chaired by the Project Owner and including the Business Manager and Solution Provider — that makes the decisions the Project Manager is not authorised to make alone, such as approving changes to the baselined scope, schedule or budget, or deciding whether the project proceeds past a phase gate. The Project Manager runs the project day to day; the Steering Committee owns its strategic direction and major decisions.

To manage stakeholders systematically rather than by instinct, projects commonly use a **power-interest grid**, plotting each stakeholder by their power (ability to influence the project) against their interest (how much the outcome matters to them). High power and high interest need to be managed closely; high power but low interest should be kept satisfied; high interest but low power should be kept informed; low power and low interest need only minimal monitoring. This grid then drives the **communication plan** directly — the same message, at the same frequency and level of detail, is rarely appropriate for a Steering Committee sponsor and for an end-user representative.

## 6. Procurement and Contract Management

Procurement in the EU institutions is not simply a commercial choice but a matter of law: public procurement is governed by principles derived from the EU treaties and codified in instruments such as Directive 2014/24/EU, chief among them **transparency** (procedures and criteria must be public and predictable, so all potential bidders can compete on equal information), **equal treatment / non-discrimination** (every economic operator must be treated identically, with no criterion that unjustifiably favours a particular bidder, nationality or technology), and **proportionality** (requirements imposed on bidders must be proportionate to the actual subject matter and value of the contract, not disproportionately burdensome). These principles apply regardless of the specific procedure used, and a procurement decision that cannot be justified against them is vulnerable to challenge.

A **framework contract** (or framework agreement) is not itself a contract for specific goods or services; it is an umbrella agreement with one or more suppliers, established through a single competitive procedure, under which individual specific contracts ("call-offs") can later be awarded quickly — often through a simplified mini-competition or direct order — without re-running a full procurement procedure each time. This is the standard mechanism EU institutions use for recurring ICT services.

Contract **pricing models** carry different risk allocations, and confusing them is a common trap: a **fixed-price contract** sets a firm price for a defined scope in advance, so the supplier bears the risk of cost overruns (and the buyer bears the risk of a poorly specified scope leading to disputes or costly change requests); a **time-and-materials (T&M) contract** pays the supplier for actual hours worked and materials used, so the buyer bears the risk of the effort taking longer than expected, but gains flexibility when scope cannot be fully defined upfront. Effective **vendor/supplier management** does not end at contract signature: it requires ongoing performance monitoring against agreed service levels, and active management of the relationship, deliverables and disputes for the contract's duration.

## 7. Quality Management and Project Assurance

Quality management is frequently tested through three terms that sound similar but describe different activities at different points in the project. **Quality planning** happens upfront: identifying the relevant quality standards, requirements and acceptance criteria for the project and its deliverables, and deciding how they will be satisfied and verified. **Quality assurance (QA)** is a proactive, process-focused activity carried out during the project: auditing whether the defined processes are actually being followed correctly, on the reasoning that a sound process reliably produces a sound output. **Quality control (QC)** is a reactive, product-focused activity: inspecting or testing the actual deliverables produced to verify they meet the specified requirements, identifying defects before handoff. A useful shorthand: QA is about the process ("are we building it right"), QC is about the product ("did we build it correctly").

In agile delivery, the **Definition of Done (DoD)** operationalises quality control at the level of a single increment: a shared, explicit checklist (for example, code reviewed, tests passing, documentation updated, deployed to a test environment) that a piece of work must satisfy before it can be considered complete. Without an agreed Definition of Done, "done" quietly means different things to different team members, and partially-finished work accumulates hidden technical debt that only surfaces later.

**Project audits** provide an independent, structured check — sometimes internal, sometimes external — of whether a project is being run in compliance with its methodology, governance rules and quality plan, distinct from the day-to-day quality assurance carried out by the project team itself. Finally, **lessons-learned** exercises (in agile terms, **retrospectives**) capture what worked well and what did not, so improvements can be carried forward; a retrospective specifically inspects the team's own process and collaboration, not the product it built — that is the job of the Sprint Review.

## 8. Programme, Portfolio Management and Benefits Realisation

Where Topic 1 introduced the basic distinction between a project, a programme and a portfolio, this area goes deeper into how programmes and portfolios are actually managed, and the concept that ties them together: **benefits**. A project delivers **outputs** (a new system, a migrated platform); a programme exists specifically to translate a coordinated set of outputs into **outcomes** and, ultimately, measurable **benefits** for the organisation — cost savings, improved service levels, regulatory compliance — that typically could not have been realised by managing the same projects independently, because the benefit depends on their combined effect.

**Benefits realisation management** is the discipline of identifying, planning, tracking and confirming that those intended benefits actually materialise — critically, this activity does not stop when the project delivering the underlying capability closes. Many benefits only appear well after go-live, once the organisation has actually adopted and embedded the new way of working, so a named benefits owner typically continues tracking realisation on a defined timeline that extends beyond the project's own closure.

At portfolio level, the central activity is **prioritisation**: because organisational capacity (budget, skilled staff, change absorption) is always finite, a portfolio management function must continually rank and select which candidate projects and programmes to fund, pause or stop, balancing strategic value, risk, interdependency and available capacity — not simply approving every proposal that arrives with a positive business case in isolation.

The **business case** is the governance artefact that underpins these decisions at every level: it articulates the problem or opportunity, the options considered, the expected costs and benefits, and the justification for proceeding, and it should be revisited — not just approved once and filed away — at each major decision point (a phase gate, a programme review, a portfolio prioritisation cycle) to confirm the investment still makes sense as circumstances change. A business case that is no longer valid is a legitimate reason to stop a project, even one that is otherwise on time and on budget.

### Topic 8 — Programme, Portfolio Management and Benefits Realisation (Q76–80)

**76.** In programme management, dividing a programme into a series of tranches, each ending with a formal review point before the next begins, is done primarily to:

A. Deliver the programme's benefits in a single, indivisible step at the very end, since tranches only apply to a programme's internal administrative paperwork.
B. Structure delivery of the programme's benefits as a series of step changes in capability, with each tranche-end review reassessing the business case and confirming the programme should proceed, redirect, or stop — providing programme-level governance analogous to a project's phase gates, but at a larger scale.
C. Guarantee that every constituent project within the programme finishes on exactly the same date.
D. Replace the need for a Programme Steering Committee or equivalent governance body.

**77.** In benefits realisation, which statement correctly distinguishes an enabling change from a business change?

A. An enabling change is a one-off change (typically delivered by a project, such as a new IT system) that creates a new capability; a business change is the change in ways of working, processes or behaviour that the business itself must then adopt to actually use that capability — without which the intended outcomes and benefits will not materialise.
B. Enabling changes are made by the business after the project closes, while business changes are made by the project team before it closes — the two are simply swapped in option A.
C. Business change refers exclusively to changes in an organisation's IT infrastructure, never to changes in working practices.
D. Enabling changes and business changes are interchangeable terms with no meaningful distinction between them.

**78.** Effective portfolio management involves balancing the portfolio's mix, which means that, beyond ranking individual projects by value, a portfolio management function should also:

C. Ensure that every project in the portfolio carries an identical level of risk, since risk diversity is considered undesirable at portfolio level.
D. Avoid categorising projects altogether, since categorisation is considered a project-level, not portfolio-level, concern.

**79.** In benefits realisation, which statement best captures the range of legitimate benefit types a benefits realisation plan should track?

A. Only cash-releasing benefits count as genuine benefits; anything that cannot be expressed as a direct financial saving should be dropped from the benefits realisation plan.
B. Benefits can be cash-releasing (quantifiable, direct financial savings, such as reduced staff costs), quantifiable but non-cash (measurable but not directly monetary, such as hours saved), or non-quantifiable/qualitative (real but not expressible in numbers, such as improved reputation or staff morale) — all three are legitimate and worth tracking, even though only some can be reduced to a euro figure.
C. Qualitative benefits are, by definition, not real benefits and should never appear in a business case.
D. A benefit can only be classified once, at project initiation, and can never move between categories as the project progresses.

**80.** Within a programme made up of several constituent projects, how should an individual project's business case relate to the programme's overarching business case?

A. Since the programme already has its own business case, none of its constituent projects need a separate business case of their own.
B. Each constituent project should still have its own business case, but it should explicitly demonstrate how that project contributes to, and remains aligned with, the strategic rationale and overall benefits target set out in the programme-level business case — not stand entirely independently of it.
C. A constituent project's business case must always show a larger financial return than the programme's own overarching business case.

## ANSWERS

### Topic 1 — Project Management Fundamentals and Life Cycle

**1. A**

Monitoring and Controlling is not a discrete, one-off step; it runs alongside Executing (and, to a lesser extent, the other process groups) for the whole duration of the project, tracking performance and triggering corrective action as needed.

**2. B**

PM² organises delivery into four sequential phases — Initiating, Planning, Executing, Closing — with Monitoring & Control running continuously across all of them, rather than as a separate fifth phase. This structure, developed by the European Commission's CoEPM² within DG DIGIT, is directly relevant to how ICT projects are actually run across EU institutions.

**3. B**

A programme coordinates related projects to realise benefits unattainable by managing them separately; a portfolio is the broader, strategically-prioritised collection of projects, programmes and other work. Option A reverses the two definitions.

**4. B**

The Business Case justifies why the investment should be made; the Project Charter (or equivalent) is the authorisation document that formally launches the project and empowers the project manager, building on that justification. They serve different governance purposes and are produced by different roles.

**5. A**

A phase gate is a formal governance checkpoint at which a decision is made — proceed, redirect, or stop — based on whether the project remains justified and on track, not merely an administrative or IT-security term.

### Topic 2 — Agile and Hybrid Delivery Approaches

**6. B**

The Scrum Master serves the Scrum Team by coaching its use of Scrum and removing impediments; this is explicitly not the same as a traditional command-and-control project manager who assigns individual tasks.

**7. A**

The Product Owner owns product value and backlog prioritisation; the Scrum Master owns the team's effective application of the Scrum framework itself. These are two distinct accountabilities, not one role under two names.

**8. B**

WIP limits force problems to surface early: once a stage is at its cap, new work cannot enter it until existing work moves on, which exposes bottlenecks immediately rather than letting them hide until a later review.

**9. A**

Velocity is a team-specific, historically-derived measure used for that team's own sprint-planning forecasts; because team composition, tooling and estimating habits differ, it is explicitly not designed or valid for comparing different teams against each other.

**10. B**

Combining iterative, sprint-based execution with a fixed, milestone-driven governance and reporting layer is the defining feature of a hybrid agile-waterfall approach — common in EU institutional ICT delivery, where procurement and financial cycles impose fixed external milestones.

### Topic 3 — Scope, Schedule and Cost Management

**11. B**

A WBS is a deliverable-oriented decomposition of scope, not a schedule; work packages (its lowest level) are what later gets sequenced, estimated, costed and assigned in the project schedule.

**12. B**

Zero float means an activity has no scheduling slack: any delay to it propagates directly to the project's finish date, which is exactly the defining property of the critical path.

**13. A**

CPI = EV/AC = 80,000/90,000 ≈ 0.89, indicating the work performed cost more than budgeted (over budget). SPI = EV/PV = 80,000/100,000 = 0.80, indicating less work was completed than planned by this point (behind schedule). Option D swaps which index measures cost versus schedule.

**14. B**

E = (O + 4M + P) / 6 = (4 + 4×6 + 14) / 6 = (4 + 24 + 14) / 6 = 42 / 6 = 7 days.

**15. B**

Scope creep is the uncontrolled, incremental expansion of scope without formal change control. Gold plating is a related but distinct concept (adding unrequested extras on the team's own initiative); crashing and fast tracking are schedule-compression techniques, not scope-control failures.

### Topic 4 — Risk and Issue Management

**16. A**

A risk is inherently uncertain and forward-looking; an issue has already happened and demands active resolution now. The two require different management responses, which is precisely why the distinction matters operationally.

**17. B**

Purchasing insurance does not stop the risk from occurring — it shifts who bears the financial consequence if it does, which is the definition of a transfer response, not mitigation (which would reduce probability or impact) or avoidance (which would eliminate the risk itself).

**18. B**

A probability-impact matrix exists to triage: it directs scarce risk-management effort toward the risks that are both likely and severe, rather than requiring equal attention to every risk regardless of how minor or improbable it is.

**19. B**

Despite the wording "we assume", what is actually described is a reliance on another team's deliverable being completed first — a dependency, not merely an assumption. An assumption would be an unverified belief taken as true for planning purposes (e.g., "we assume the migrated data will not require reformatting"), not a tracked reliance on someone else's delivery.

**20. C**

Deciding not to act proactively, while preparing a contingency reserve in case the risk does occur, is a textbook description of (active) acceptance — distinct from mitigation, avoidance or transfer, none of which apply here.

### Topic 5 — Stakeholder Management and Governance

**21. A**

Responsible identifies who does the work; Accountable identifies the single person answerable for its correct completion. Having more than one Accountable per task is exactly the kind of ambiguity a RACI matrix is designed to prevent.

**22. A**

Consulted implies two-way engagement before a decision is finalised; Informed implies one-way notification after the fact. Confusing the two is a common misreading of the RACI framework.

**23. B**

The Steering Committee owns decisions above the Project Manager's authority — baseline changes, phase-gate approval — while day-to-day execution remains the Project Manager's responsibility. It does not replace either role.

**24. B**

High power, low interest stakeholders should be kept satisfied: engaged enough to retain their support, without burying them in detail they have not asked for and are unlikely to want.

**25. B**

Stakeholders differ in power, interest and informational needs, so a single, one-size-fits-all report will typically over-inform some stakeholders and under-inform others; the fix is a communication plan tailored by stakeholder group, not a legal requirement or a language-formatting limitation.

### Topic 6 — Procurement and Contract Management

**26. A**

Transparency, equal treatment and proportionality (together with related principles such as mutual recognition) are the foundational principles underpinning EU public procurement law, derived from the EU treaties and reflected in directives such as 2014/24/EU.

**27. B**

A framework contract is an umbrella arrangement, not a specific delivery contract itself; it allows individual call-offs to be issued quickly against it later, without re-running the full competitive procedure each time.

**28. B**

Fixed-price contracts place cost-overrun risk on the supplier for a defined scope; T&M contracts place effort-overrun risk on the buyer in exchange for flexibility when scope cannot be fully defined upfront. Option A reverses this allocation.

**29. B**

Signing a contract only sets the terms; realising value requires continuously monitoring the supplier's actual performance against agreed service levels and actively managing deliverables, changes and disputes throughout the contract's life.

**30. B**

Above the relevant threshold, EU procurement principles require a competitive, transparent procedure open to qualified suppliers; an existing informal relationship is not, on its own, a lawful basis for direct award.

### Topic 7 — Quality Management and Project Assurance

**31. A**

QA is proactive and process-focused (are the right processes being followed); QC is reactive and product-focused (does this specific deliverable meet requirements). Option B reverses the two.

**32. B**

A Definition of Done is a shared completion checklist that prevents "done" from becoming a subjective, inconsistently-applied judgement, and stops partially-finished work from being reported as complete.

**33. B**

An audit is an independent, structured compliance check, typically conducted by someone outside the day-to-day delivery team, and is distinct from the ongoing quality assurance the project team applies to its own work.

**34. B**

The Sprint Review is about the product (inspecting the increment with stakeholders, adapting the backlog); the Sprint Retrospective is about the team's own process and collaboration. Conflating the two is a common Scrum misconception.

**35. B**

Capturing lessons learned only at final closure means the current project can never benefit from them; capturing them continuously allows improvements to be applied while the project is still running, not just passed on to future projects.

### Topic 8 — Programme, Portfolio Management and Benefits Realisation

**36. A**

A programme's reason for existing is precisely to capture benefits that depend on the combined, coordinated effect of multiple projects — benefits that independent, uncoordinated project management could not realise on its own.

**37. B**

Many benefits are only realised once the organisation has adopted and embedded a new capability in practice, which typically happens after the delivering project has closed — so benefits tracking must continue on its own timeline, owned by someone specifically accountable for it.

**38. B**

Portfolio management's defining activity is continual prioritisation under finite capacity: ranking and balancing competing candidate initiatives against strategic value, risk and interdependencies, rather than executing any one of them directly.

**39. B**

The conditions that originally justified a project can change; revisiting the business case at each governance gate confirms the investment is still worthwhile, and an invalid business case is sufficient grounds to stop a project regardless of its schedule or budget performance.

**40. B**

The standard benefits-realisation chain runs from output (the tangible deliverable) to outcome (the resulting change in behaviour or capability) to benefit (the measurable value that change creates for the organisation) — not the reverse, and the three terms are not interchangeable.

### Topic 1 — Project Management Fundamentals and Life Cycle

**41. A**

The Project Handbook is a living artefact, started early and maintained across the project's life, that consolidates the agreed management approach across areas such as scope, schedule, quality, risk and communication — distinct from the one-time Business Case (investment justification) or Project Charter (authorisation).

**42. A**

The Business Manager represents the Project Owner day to day, helps shape the business objectives that feed the Project Initiation Request and Business Case, and coordinates the Business Implementation Group — a distinct accountability from the Solution Provider's, which sits on the Provider side.

**43. A**

The Solution Provider holds overall accountability for the project's deliverables on the Provider side, whether that side is an internal IT unit or an external supplier; the role exists in both cases, and it is distinct from both the Business Implementation Group and the Steering Committee.

**44. A**

Formal closure includes confirming acceptance of deliverables, transitioning them (and support responsibilities) into operations, closing supplier contracts, releasing resources, and capturing a final report — options B, C and D each describe activities that belong to other phases or that continue on their own timeline, not to closure itself.

**45. A**

A project is temporary and ends once its unique objective is achieved; operations are the ongoing, repetitive work that keeps the organisation running with no built-in end date. Confusing the two — for example, treating a project as if it never closes — undermines both project governance and operational planning.

### Topic 2 — Agile and Hybrid Delivery Approaches

**46. A**

Backlog refinement is a continuous, collaborative activity between the Product Owner and Developers that keeps upcoming backlog items detailed, estimated and ordered, so Sprint Planning can proceed efficiently — it is not a single upfront event, and it is not the Scrum Master's task alone.

**47. B**

Sprint Planning sets the Sprint Goal and produces the Sprint Backlog by addressing both what can realistically be delivered and how the Developers plan to deliver it — it is a collaborative forecasting exercise, not a unilateral instruction or a retrospective look back.

**48. A**

Lead time spans from initial request to delivery; cycle time spans only the active working period within that. Because cycle time is contained within lead time, it can never exceed it — the gap between the two typically reflects queueing and waiting time before work actively starts.

**49. B**

Cross-team coordination mechanisms such as a "Scrum of Scrums," together with frameworks purpose-built for scaling agile across many teams, let representatives surface shared dependencies and risks without requiring every individual to attend every other team's events or abandoning agile practice altogether.

**50. A**

MoSCoW — Must have, Should have, Could have, Won't have (this time) — is a widely used technique for agreeing with stakeholders which requirements are non-negotiable and which can be descoped first if time or budget becomes tight.

### Topic 3 — Scope, Schedule and Cost Management

**51. A**

SV = EV − PV = 150,000 − 120,000 = +€30,000, a positive (favourable) variance meaning more value has been earned than was planned by this point — ahead of schedule. CV = EV − AC = 150,000 − 160,000 = −€10,000, a negative (unfavourable) variance meaning the work performed cost more than its earned value — over budget.

**52. A**

The critical chain method extends the critical path concept by also factoring in resource availability, not just task dependencies, and protects the finish date with strategically placed buffers (project buffer, feeding buffers) instead of inflating individual activity estimates.

**53. A**

Because neither over-allocated activity has spare float, resolving the conflict by shifting one of them to when the resource is actually available pushes out the schedule — this is resource levelling. Resource smoothing, by contrast, only reassigns work within existing float, so it cannot affect the finish date but also cannot resolve a conflict where no float exists.

**54. A**

EAC = BAC / CPI = 500,000 / 0.80 = €625,000. This formula is used specifically when the variance observed so far is expected to be typical of the whole remaining project, projecting the same rate of cost inefficiency forward onto the unfinished work.

**55. A**

Crashing shortens the critical path by adding resources to it, usually raising cost; fast tracking shortens the schedule by overlapping activities that were originally sequential, usually raising risk (particularly of rework) rather than cost directly. The two are commonly confused, but they trade off against different constraints.

### Topic 4 — Risk and Issue Management

**56. A**

Risk appetite is the broad, qualitative amount of risk-taking an organisation or project is comfortable with in general; risk tolerance translates that into a specific, measurable acceptable range for a given objective — the two operate at different levels of abstraction and are not interchangeable.

**57. A**

Exploiting an opportunity means actively acting to guarantee it occurs, removing the uncertainty entirely — the positive-risk mirror of "avoid" for threats. Assigning the most capable resource specifically to secure early completion is a textbook exploit response, not a passive acceptance or a third-party transfer.

**58. B**

A secondary risk is a new risk created specifically by implementing a response to another risk — here, outsourcing (the mitigation) introduces a fresh dependency risk on the supplier's own delivery. A residual risk, by contrast, is what remains of the original risk after the response, not a newly created one.

**59. B**

A risk owner is deliberately assigned to whoever is best positioned to monitor and act on a given risk, which may or may not be the person who first flagged it — ownership is about capability and authority to manage the risk, not about who spotted it first.

**60. B**

Monte Carlo simulation runs many randomised iterations across a project's risks and estimates to produce a probability distribution of overall outcomes (such as total cost or finish date), giving quantitative, aggregate insight that a qualitative probability-impact matrix — which ranks risks individually and relatively — cannot provide on its own.

### Topic 5 — Stakeholder Management and Governance

**61. B**

The stakeholder register captures who the stakeholders are, their roles, influence, interest and expectations — foundational information that later feeds tools like the power-interest grid and the communication plan, rather than serving as a contract or a substitute for a RACI matrix.

**62. A**

An escalation procedure defines, in advance, where and how quickly an issue or decision should be raised once it exceeds the current handler's authority to resolve — ensuring timely, orderly resolution through the right governance layer rather than unmanaged delay or someone acting outside their authority.

**63. A**

A Change Control Board exists specifically to evaluate the impact of proposed changes against the project's baseline and formally decide whether to accept, reject or defer them — an ongoing governance function throughout delivery, not a one-off approval at the start, and not itself an implementation team.

**64. B**

The salience model classifies stakeholders along three dimensions — power, legitimacy and urgency — to judge how much management attention their claims deserve; it complements rather than replaces the power-interest grid, which uses a different pair of dimensions (power and interest alone).

**65. B**

By plotting each stakeholder's current engagement level against the level actually needed for the project to succeed, the matrix highlights exactly where targeted engagement effort will have the most impact, rather than applying identical treatment to every stakeholder regardless of their starting point.

### Topic 6 — Procurement and Contract Management

**66. A**

The open procedure is single-stage: any operator may tender directly, and every tender received is evaluated. The restricted procedure is two-stage: any operator may request to participate, but only those who pass a prior selection stage are invited to submit a tender — option B simply reverses these two descriptions.

**67. B**

Competitive dialogue exists for genuinely complex requirements where the contracting authority cannot fully specify the technical or contractual solution in advance; a structured dialogue with selected candidates helps develop viable solutions before a final tender is requested against them.

**68. B**

A negotiated procedure without prior publication is a narrow exception, permitted only under specific conditions such as unforeseeable extreme urgency or genuine single-supplier situations for technical or exclusivity reasons — it is not a general convenience option for avoiding competitive procurement.

**69. B**

MEAT is most commonly applied through the best price-quality ratio, weighing criteria linked to the contract's subject matter (such as technical merit and quality) against price or cost. EU law also permits an award based on price or cost alone under a cost-effectiveness approach — Option A is wrong not because price-only evaluation is forbidden, but because it falsely claims this is the only method the law allows, with no other approach ever possible.

**70. A**

Selection criteria filter which bidders are capable and suitable enough to be allowed to compete at all; award criteria then evaluate the actual merits of each qualified bidder's tender to decide the winner. A bidder can clear selection and still lose on the award criteria, since the two serve different purposes at different stages.

### Topic 7 — Quality Management and Project Assurance

**71. A**

Prevention and appraisal costs (the cost of conformance — investing to avoid or catch defects early) are generally much smaller than internal and especially external failure costs (the cost of nonconformance), which grow sharply the later a defect is found, particularly after it has already reached the customer.

**72. A**

Root cause analysis techniques like a fishbone diagram or the "5 Whys" exist to trace a recurring defect back to its underlying systemic cause, enabling a durable process fix rather than repeatedly treating the same visible symptom without addressing what actually keeps producing it.

**73. B**

Applying the 80/20 rule to quality data means recognising that a small number of defect categories or root causes typically account for most of the reported problems, so improvement effort is most effective when concentrated there rather than spread evenly across every category regardless of frequency.

**74. B**

PDCA is deliberately iterative: each cycle's outcomes directly inform the planning of the next cycle, making continuous improvement an ongoing discipline rather than a single completed project or a one-time procurement scoring method.

**75. B**

A peer review catches defects while a work product is still early in its life, when fixes are cheapest and easiest — complementing, not replacing, later-stage testing, and applicable to documents and designs just as much as to code.

### Topic 8 — Programme, Portfolio Management and Benefits Realisation

**76. B**

Dividing a programme into tranches lets it deliver benefits as a series of capability step-changes, with each tranche-end review reassessing the business case and deciding whether to continue, adjust or stop — a programme-scale equivalent of a project's phase gates, not a purely administrative device.

**77. A**

An enabling change creates a new capability (typically a project deliverable, such as an IT system); a business change is the accompanying shift in how the organisation actually works that the business itself must make to exploit that capability — without it, the intended outcomes and benefits will not follow, no matter how well the enabling change was delivered.

**78. A**

Portfolio balancing goes beyond ranking individual projects by value alone: it also weighs the mix across categories like running versus transforming the business, and across differing risk profiles, so the portfolio as a whole reflects the organisation's strategic priorities and risk appetite.

**79. B**

Legitimate benefits span cash-releasing (direct financial savings), quantifiable non-cash (measurable but not monetary) and non-quantifiable qualitative benefits (real but not expressible as a number) — a sound benefits realisation plan tracks all three rather than only counting what can be reduced to a euro figure.

**80. B**

A constituent project still needs its own business case, but that case should explicitly show how the project's contribution supports and stays aligned with the programme's overarching strategic rationale and benefits target — the programme and project business cases are related and nested, not identical, and not mutually exclusive.
