# 4. Succeeding in the ICT Infrastructure Field-Related MCQ Test

The Field-Related Multiple Choice Test is the second hurdle in the EPSO/AD/429/26 (4) competition, and the first one that is specific to the ICT Infrastructure field rather than to general cognitive aptitude. Where the reasoning tests of Phase 1 measure how you think, this test measures what you know: your professional command of the concepts, trade-offs and vocabulary an infrastructure engineer or administrator works with day to day, at the level expected of an AD7 administrator responsible for the compute, storage, data-centre and service-management foundations that keep the European institutions' ICT operations running reliably.

Format, in brief: **30 questions, 40 minutes, scored 0–30, pass mark 15/30**. Passing the mark is necessary but not sufficient — because this stage is classifying rather than purely eliminatory, EPSO only carries forward the best-scoring candidates in the ICT Infrastructure field, up to a multiple of the number of posts available (204 posts in this field, per the competition notice). In practice this means 15/30 is a floor to clear comfortably, not the actual target; your real goal is a score that holds up well against your competitor pool. The test is taken in your **Language 2** (typically English, French or German), not your strongest language, which adds a layer of terminology pressure on top of the technical content itself.

Unlike the verbal reasoning test discussed in the previous chapter, there is **no passage of text** to analyse here: each item is a self-contained question drawing on your own subject-matter knowledge. This changes the shape of your preparation substantially — it is less about technique and more about making sure your working knowledge of a defined set of topic areas is accurate, current, and precise enough to withstand deliberately similar-sounding wrong answers.

In the following, we will cover:

- How field-related MCQ items are typically designed, and the shapes wrong answers tend to take
- A concise primer on the eight topic areas the test draws on
- An 80-question practice bank, organised by topic, with full answers and explanations
- A short list of free resources for further study

## How These Questions Are Designed

Well-constructed technical MCQ items rarely rely on obscure trivia or vendor-specific configuration syntax — that would be unfair across such a broad field, and easy to get wrong even for an expert. Instead, they test whether you can tell apart two concepts that are genuinely easy to confuse under time pressure. Recognising the typical shapes wrong answers take will save you time and help you avoid the traps set for the unwary:

- **The reversed pair.** Two related concepts (RTO/RPO, SLA/OLA, Type 1/Type 2 hypervisor, hot site/warm site) are swapped, so the distractor describes concept B correctly but attaches concept A's name to it.
- **The half-truth.** A statement that starts out correct and then quietly introduces a false or overreaching claim (for example, "RAID provides redundancy across disks, so dedicated backups become unnecessary").
- **The outdated or oversimplified best practice.** A claim that used to be, or sounds like it should be, good practice, but that mainstream consensus has since qualified or superseded (for example, treating a fixed quarterly patch cycle as adequate regardless of how urgently a newly disclosed vulnerability is being actively exploited).
- **The category error.** An answer that correctly describes a real concept, just not the one the question actually asked about (for example, describing a network firewall rule when the question asks what it means to "harden" a server).
- **The common misconception.** A statement that "feels" right because it is a widely repeated but technically incorrect interpretation (the classic assumption that a higher PUE value means better energy efficiency).

When you are unsure between two options, ask yourself: which one names the actual mechanism or root cause, and which one only sounds plausible? The correct answer in a well-designed item is nearly always the one that is both technically precise and addresses the real underlying issue, not just a symptom of it.

## 1. Compute, Storage and Virtualization Fundamentals

Virtualization decouples workloads from the physical hardware they run on, and the most consistently tested distinction here is **Type 1 vs. Type 2 hypervisors**. A Type 1 (bare-metal) hypervisor runs directly on the physical hardware, with no underlying host operating system in between — VMware ESXi, Microsoft Hyper-V and KVM are typical examples — and is the standard choice for production data-centre workloads because of its lower overhead and smaller attack surface. A Type 2 (hosted) hypervisor instead runs as an application on top of a conventional host operating system, which is convenient for development and testing but adds an extra layer of overhead and dependency that makes it unsuitable for production server workloads.

A related but distinct comparison is **virtual machines vs. containers**. A VM virtualizes the hardware: each VM bundles a complete guest operating system, kernel included, on top of a hypervisor, giving strong isolation at the cost of size and boot time. A container instead virtualizes the operating system: containers on the same host share that host's kernel and isolate only the application and its dependencies, which makes them far lighter and faster to start, but also means a kernel-level vulnerability or crash on the host can affect every container running on it.

On the storage side, **RAID** levels trade capacity, performance and fault tolerance against each other: RAID 1 mirrors data across drives for redundancy at the cost of half the raw capacity; RAID 5 stripes data with distributed parity, tolerating a single drive failure; RAID 6 adds a second parity block to tolerate two simultaneous failures; RAID 10 stripes across mirrored pairs, combining performance and redundancy at the cost of using only half the raw capacity, much like RAID 1. **SAN vs. NAS** is the other recurring pair: a SAN presents block-level storage over a dedicated high-speed network (Fibre Channel or iSCSI), appearing to a host as if it were local disk; a NAS presents file-level storage over a standard IP network via protocols such as NFS or SMB, appearing as a shared folder. Finally, **storage tiering** automatically moves data between faster, more expensive media (e.g. SSD) and slower, cheaper media (e.g. HDD or tape) based on how frequently it is accessed, balancing cost against performance without manual intervention.

## 2. Data Centre Design and Facilities Management

Data-centre resilience is usually graded against the **Uptime Institute's Tier classification**, which runs from Tier I to Tier IV. Tier I offers a single, non-redundant path for power and cooling with no built-in fault tolerance; Tier II adds redundant capacity components but still only a single distribution path; Tier III introduces a concurrently maintainable architecture with multiple distribution paths, so any single component can be taken down for planned maintenance without disrupting operations; Tier IV goes further still, adding fault tolerance so the site continues operating even through an *unplanned* failure of any single component, not just planned maintenance. The jump from Tier III to Tier IV — concurrently maintainable versus genuinely fault tolerant — is the distinction most worth being able to state precisely.

Underpinning these tiers is **redundancy notation**: N represents the bare capacity actually needed to run the load; N+1 adds one extra unit of capacity beyond what is needed, tolerating a single component failure; 2N duplicates the entire system end to end, giving two complete, independent paths so that one can fail (or be maintained) while the other carries the full load alone; 2N+1 goes further, duplicating the system and adding a spare on top of that.

Energy efficiency is measured by **PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness)**: the ratio of total facility energy consumption to the energy consumed by the IT equipment alone. A PUE of 1.0 is the theoretical ideal, meaning every watt drawn by the facility goes directly to computing; real-world data centres typically run higher, with the gap representing overhead such as cooling and power-distribution losses — so a *lower* PUE is always the goal, not a higher one. **Hot aisle/cold aisle containment** improves cooling efficiency by physically separating a rack's cool air intake from its hot air exhaust, preventing the two from mixing and letting cooling systems work against a more predictable, concentrated airflow rather than a mixed-temperature room. Finally, continuity of power itself relies on a **UPS bridging the short gap** between a power failure and a **generator** starting up and taking over sustained supply — the two are complementary, not interchangeable.

## 3. IT Service Management (ITIL 4)

ITIL 4 provides the shared vocabulary most EU institutions use to run and improve their ICT services, and the most frequently tested distinction is **incident vs. problem management**. Incident management exists to restore normal service operation as quickly as possible after an unplanned interruption — a workaround that gets users working again is a perfectly acceptable outcome, even if the underlying cause is still unknown. Problem management instead investigates the underlying root cause of one or more incidents, aiming to prevent recurrence altogether or provide a documented, permanent fix; it operates on a longer timescale precisely because service has usually already been restored by incident management. Treating root-cause analysis as something that must happen before service can be restored is one of the most common wrong turns in real-world practice, not just in exam questions.

**Change management** (change enablement) sits alongside these: it assesses, approves and schedules modifications to the live environment so that risk is managed deliberately, distinguishing standard (pre-approved, low-risk, repeatable), normal (assessed case by case) and emergency (expedited for urgent fixes) changes.

The **service catalogue** is the customer-facing subset of the broader service portfolio: it lists the services currently available to users and customers, described in terms they can understand and act on, rather than the full technical detail of every service at every lifecycle stage.

**SLAs, OLAs and underpinning contracts** are often confused because all three set expectations, but they operate at different boundaries: a Service Level Agreement (SLA) is agreed between the service provider and the customer; an Operational Level Agreement (OLA) is an internal agreement between the teams inside the provider organisation who must jointly deliver that SLA; an underpinning contract (UC) is the equivalent agreement with an external supplier whose service feeds into what the provider offers. Finally, the **CMDB (configuration management database)** stores configuration items (CIs) — servers, applications, contracts, and so on — together with their relationships, so that other processes (incident, problem, change) can quickly see what depends on what and assess impact before acting.

## 4. Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery

Business continuity planning is built around two recovery metrics that are very easy to swap under time pressure: **RTO (Recovery Time Objective) vs. RPO (Recovery Point Objective)**. RTO is the maximum acceptable length of time a service can remain down before the disruption becomes unacceptable to the business — it drives how fast you must be able to restore operations. RPO is the maximum acceptable amount of data loss, measured as a point in time — it drives how frequently you must back up or replicate data, since a four-hour RPO means you can afford to lose at most four hours' worth of data. A system can have an aggressive RTO and a lax RPO, or vice versa, and the two require different investments to achieve.

The **3-2-1 backup rule** is a simple, durable heuristic for backup design: keep at least three copies of your data, on at least two different types of media, with at least one copy stored offsite — so that no single failure (a disk crash, a site-wide fire, a ransomware attack that also encrypts an attached backup drive) can destroy every copy at once.

**Disaster recovery sites** are commonly classified by how ready they are to take over: a **hot site** is a fully equipped, continuously running duplicate environment that can take over in minutes with little to no data loss, at high ongoing cost; a **warm site** has the infrastructure in place but requires some setup — restoring recent backups, powering up systems — before it can serve production traffic, trading some recovery time for lower cost; a **cold site** is essentially an empty facility with power and connectivity but no active systems, offering the lowest cost and the longest recovery time.

Finally, **failover vs. failback** describes the two ends of an incident: failover is the act of switching operations over to a backup system or site when the primary fails; failback is the deliberate, carefully sequenced act of switching back to the primary once it has been restored and verified — a step that is often riskier than the failover itself and should never be rushed. A **Business Impact Analysis (BIA)** is what actually sets the RTO and RPO for each business function in the first place, by quantifying the financial and operational impact of an outage of varying lengths.

## 5. Infrastructure Monitoring, Capacity and Performance Management

Effective infrastructure monitoring rests on a distinction between **proactive and reactive monitoring**. Reactive monitoring detects a problem only once it has already caused visible impact — a server is down, a disk is full — and prompts a response after the fact. Proactive monitoring instead watches trends and leading indicators (rising latency, a disk filling up at a known rate, an unusual traffic pattern) to catch and resolve issues before they escalate into an outage; the ability to prevent incidents rather than merely react to them is what separates a mature monitoring practice from a merely functional one.

**Capacity planning** extends this forward in time: it forecasts future resource needs (compute, storage, network) based on observed growth trends and planned business changes, so infrastructure is scaled ahead of demand. Getting it wrong in either direction is costly — under-provisioning causes performance degradation and outages right when demand peaks, while over-provisioning wastes budget on capacity that sits idle.

Modern monitoring is often organised around the **three pillars of observability**: metrics, logs and traces. Metrics are aggregated, numerical time-series data (CPU utilisation, request rate, error rate) that are cheap to store and well suited to dashboards and trend-based alerting. Logs are discrete, timestamped records of individual events, valuable for understanding exactly what happened at a specific moment, including the surrounding context. Traces follow a single request as it moves across multiple services in a distributed system, showing where time was spent and where it failed — the pillar most specifically suited to diagnosing latency problems that span several components. Each pillar answers a different kind of question, and mature observability combines all three rather than relying on any single one.

Finally, **alerting thresholds** must be set deliberately: thresholds set too tight generate a flood of low-value alerts, producing **alert fatigue** — the state in which genuine, actionable alerts get ignored or delayed because they are buried among noise. The fix is not simply adding more alerts, but making each alert symptom-based, actionable, and tied to a real user or business impact — which in turn depends on having a documented performance baseline of what "normal" looks like, so that a genuine deviation can actually be recognised as one.

## 6. Enterprise Architecture and Standards

Enterprise architecture frameworks give organisations a structured way to plan technology change deliberately rather than by accretion, and **TOGAF's Architecture Development Method (ADM)** is the reference cycle most commonly tested. The ADM is iterative rather than a single straight-through project: it begins with a Preliminary phase and Phase A, Architecture Vision, before moving through the Business, Information Systems, and Technology Architecture phases (each capturing the current, "as-is," state and the target, "to-be," state for that domain), then Opportunities and Solutions, Migration Planning, Implementation Governance, and Architecture Change Management — after which the cycle returns to revisit the vision in light of what was learned. The recurring **as-is vs. to-be** distinction is worth being able to name immediately, since transition planning is defined entirely by the gap between the two.

**Architecture governance** is the ongoing discipline that keeps implementation aligned with the intended architecture: a governance board reviews significant design decisions and change proposals against agreed principles and standards, catching drift or unauthorised divergence before it becomes embedded and costly to reverse — a continuous check, not a one-off approval granted at project kick-off.

A **reference architecture** is a proven, reusable template or pattern for solving a recurring class of problem (say, a standard pattern for a multi-region, highly available web application): it is a design to be adapted during implementation, not a deployed system in itself, and its value lies precisely in letting new projects start from a vetted starting point instead of reinventing a solution from scratch.

Finally, **technical debt** is the implied future cost of choosing an expedient, lower-quality solution now over a better one that would take longer to build — like financial debt, it can be taken on deliberately to meet a deadline, but it accrues in cost and risk the longer it goes unaddressed, and a mature architecture practice tracks it explicitly rather than treating it as invisible.

## 7. Infrastructure Security Fundamentals

Infrastructure security at the host and identity level rests on a small number of disciplines that are frequently tested precisely because they sound similar to broader security concepts but refer to distinct, specific practices. **Patch management** is the systematic process of identifying, testing and deploying updates that fix known vulnerabilities in operating systems and applications; the key discipline is timeliness balanced with testing — patches addressing actively exploited vulnerabilities need to move faster through the pipeline than routine updates, and skipping testing to patch instantly is just as much a failure of the process as leaving known vulnerabilities unpatched for months.

**OS and server hardening** reduces a system's attack surface before it is even exposed to a threat: disabling unnecessary services and ports, removing default accounts and credentials, applying vendor or industry security baselines (such as CIS Benchmarks), and configuring systems to the minimum functionality actually required for their role. Hardening is a baseline exercise applied to the system itself, distinct from patch management (which addresses known vulnerabilities over time) and from network-level controls such as firewall rules (which sit outside the host and belong to a different layer of defence entirely).

**Privileged access management (PAM)** governs the small set of accounts that can make significant changes to systems — administrator and root accounts chief among them. Rather than granting standing, broad administrative rights that sit active and exploitable at all times, mature PAM practice vaults privileged credentials centrally, grants elevated access only just-in-time and for a limited duration, and logs privileged sessions for accountability — the goal is to shrink the window and scope in which elevated access exists, not merely to know who technically holds it.

**Defence in depth** is the principle that no single control should be trusted to prevent compromise on its own: independent layers (hardened hosts, strict identity and access controls, patched software, monitoring and logging) are stacked so that the failure of any one layer does not by itself lead to a full breach. Applied at the host and identity level, this means combining hardening, patching, least-privilege access and monitoring together, rather than treating any single one of them — or any single account, including automated service accounts — as sufficient protection on its own.

## 8. Infrastructure Automation and Configuration Management

Modern infrastructure is increasingly managed as code rather than by hand, and the foundational distinction here is **Infrastructure as Code (IaC) — declarative vs. imperative**. A declarative approach (the model used by tools such as Terraform) has the engineer describe the desired end state of the infrastructure — "three web servers, one load balancer, this network configuration" — and leaves it to the tool to work out the steps needed to reach that state from wherever the infrastructure currently is, including on repeated runs. An imperative approach instead specifies the exact sequence of steps to execute, which means the engineer, not the tool, is responsible for accounting for the current state before deciding what to run next. Declarative IaC has become dominant for provisioning precisely because it removes that burden and makes the codebase itself a reliable, version-controlled record of what the infrastructure should look like.

**Configuration management tools** (such as Ansible, Puppet or Chef) operate one layer up from provisioning: once a server exists, they enforce and maintain its desired configuration — installed packages, running services, file contents, user accounts — bringing it in line with a defined state and keeping it there over time, rather than creating the underlying compute resource in the first place.

**Configuration drift** is what these tools exist to prevent: the gradual divergence of a system's actual configuration from its documented or intended baseline, typically caused by manual, ad hoc changes applied directly to production outside the automated process — the very problem that undermines an organisation's ability to trust its own documentation or reliably reproduce an environment.

Both provisioning and configuration tools should be **idempotent**: running the same automation repeatedly against the same target should converge on, and then simply confirm, the same end state without duplicating resources or causing unintended side effects on repeat runs.

Finally, **golden images vs. continuous configuration management** describes two competing strategies for keeping servers consistent: a golden image bakes a fully configured, tested image once and deploys it repeatedly, favouring immutability — when a change is needed, a new image is built and the old instance is replaced rather than patched in place — while continuous configuration management instead repeatedly applies configuration to long-lived servers that are updated in place. Immutable, image-based approaches trade flexibility for consistency and easier rollback; continuous configuration management trades some of that consistency for the ability to update running infrastructure without full replacement.

For each of the eight areas above, make sure you could explain each paired distinction (Type 1 vs. Type 2 hypervisors, RTO vs. RPO, incident vs. problem management, SLA vs. OLA, hot vs. cold DR sites, IaC vs. manual configuration) out loud, in your own words, in under thirty seconds.

- Treat the "Further Reading" list at the end of this document as a working syllabus, not an optional extra — the official ITIL 4, TOGAF, NIST contingency-planning and Uptime Institute Tier materials cover ground that general IT-infrastructure courses often only skim.

C. Zero Trust means no encryption is used anywhere on the network, since trust relationships have been eliminated entirely.

**73.** What is the key difference between an automated vulnerability scan and a penetration test?

B. A vulnerability scan automatically identifies and catalogues known potential weaknesses (such as missing patches or misconfigurations) across a broad set of systems, typically at speed and low cost; a penetration test goes further, having a skilled tester actively attempt to exploit vulnerabilities — including chaining several together — to demonstrate real-world impact, typically at a narrower scope and higher cost.

**74.** What is the distinction between encryption at rest and encryption in transit, and why might an infrastructure design need both?

A. Encryption at rest protects data stored on disks or backups from being read if the storage media is stolen or accessed without authorisation, while encryption in transit protects data as it moves across a network from being intercepted or read by an eavesdropper; a system exposed to both risks needs both forms of protection, since one does not substitute for the other.
B. Encryption in transit protects stored data on disk, while encryption at rest protects data flowing across the network.
C. Implementing encryption at rest automatically also encrypts all data in transit, making separate transit encryption unnecessary.
D. Encryption at rest and in transit are only relevant to cloud-hosted systems, and unnecessary for on-premises infrastructure.

**75.** What is the primary purpose of a Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) platform in infrastructure security operations?

A. To physically block malicious traffic at the network perimeter, functioning as a firewall replacement.

C. To automatically patch every vulnerability it detects, without any human review.
D. A SIEM's sole function is long-term log archival for compliance, with no analytical or alerting capability.

### Topic 8 — Infrastructure Automation and Configuration Management (Q76–80)

**76.** What is the key architectural difference between a push-based configuration management model (such as Ansible's default mode) and a pull-based model (such as Puppet or Chef's typical agent-based mode)?

A. In a push model, a central controller initiates connections to target nodes and applies configuration on demand, without requiring a persistent agent running on each node; in a pull model, an agent installed on each node periodically checks in with a central server and pulls down and applies its own configuration on a regular schedule.
B. Push and pull models are identical in practice, differing only in the marketing terminology used by each vendor.
C. A pull-based model requires the controller to initiate every configuration change manually, while a push-based model runs entirely autonomously with no configuration source at all.
D. Push-based tools cannot be run on a schedule, while pull-based tools can only ever be triggered manually.

**77.** In a CI/CD pipeline, what is the key distinction between continuous delivery and continuous deployment?

A. Continuous delivery and continuous deployment are exactly the same practice, just using regional spelling differences.
B. Continuous delivery ensures every code change is automatically built, tested and packaged into a release-ready state, but still requires an explicit manual decision to actually release it to production; continuous deployment goes one step further, automatically releasing every change that passes the pipeline straight to production with no manual approval gate.
C. Continuous deployment only applies to database changes, while continuous delivery only applies to application code.
D. Continuous delivery happens after production release, while continuous deployment happens only before code is written.

**78.** What is the main purpose of the state file that a declarative IaC tool such as Terraform maintains?

A. It stores a complete backup copy of the actual running virtual machines, in case they need to be physically restored.

C. The state file is optional and has no functional purpose beyond human-readable documentation.
D. The state file exists purely to encrypt secrets and credentials used during provisioning.

**79.** In infrastructure release strategies, what is the key difference between a blue-green deployment and a canary deployment?

A. A blue-green deployment maintains two complete, identical production environments and switches essentially all traffic from the old ("blue") to the new ("green") version at once, enabling a fast rollback by simply switching back; a canary deployment instead gradually shifts a small, then increasing, percentage of live traffic to the new version, validating it under real conditions before a full rollout.
B. Blue-green and canary deployment are two names for exactly the same release strategy.
C. A canary deployment always requires exactly twice the infrastructure of the production environment, just like blue-green deployment does.
D. Blue-green deployment routes only a small fraction of traffic to the new version, while canary deployment switches all traffic at once.

**80.** What is the key distinction between a container orchestration platform (such as Kubernetes) and a configuration management tool (such as Ansible, Puppet or Chef)?

A. Container orchestration and configuration management are simply two different names for the same category of tool, and are fully interchangeable.
B. A container orchestration platform primarily manages the deployment, scaling, networking and self-healing of containerised workloads across a cluster, while a configuration management tool primarily manages the state of a server or node's own configuration (packages, files, services); the two solve different problems and are frequently used together rather than as substitutes for each other.
C. Configuration management tools can only manage physical servers, and are technically incapable of managing any virtualized or containerised resource.
D. Kubernetes is a configuration management tool built specifically to replace Ansible, Puppet and Chef.

### Topic 1 — Compute, Storage and Virtualization Fundamentals

**1. A**

A Type 1 (bare-metal) hypervisor runs directly on hardware with no host OS underneath, giving lower overhead and a smaller attack surface, which is why it is the standard choice for production workloads; a Type 2 (hosted) hypervisor runs as an application on a host OS instead. Option B simply reverses the two definitions — a classic "reversed pair" distractor.

**2. B**

A VM virtualizes the hardware and packages a complete guest OS and kernel; a container instead virtualizes the OS layer and shares the host's kernel with every other container on it, which is exactly why it is lighter and faster to start, but also why a host kernel failure can affect every container running on it.

**3. C**

RAID 10 stripes data across mirrored pairs, so it inherits the fault tolerance of mirroring and the performance of striping, at the cost of usable capacity being cut in half.

**4. D**

A SAN provides block-level storage over a dedicated network so hosts see it as local disk, while a NAS provides file-level storage over a standard IP network via protocols such as NFS or SMB. Option A reverses the two definitions.

**5. B**

Automated tiering moves data between faster/costlier and slower/cheaper media purely based on access frequency, optimising cost against performance without requiring anyone to move it by hand.

### Topic 2 — Data Centre Design and Facilities Management

**6. B**

Tier IV's defining feature over Tier III is fault tolerance: the site survives an unplanned failure of any single component, not just planned maintenance, which is all a Tier III (concurrently maintainable) facility guarantees.

**7. C**

2N fully duplicates the system into two independent, complete capacity paths; N+1 only adds a single spare unit on top of the capacity actually needed. Option B reverses which notation does which.

**8. D**

PUE equals total facility energy divided by IT-equipment energy, so 1.4 means the facility as a whole consumes 1.4 times what the IT equipment alone uses; because 1.0 is the theoretical ideal, the objective is always to drive PUE down, not up — the opposite of what option C claims.

**9. A**

Containment physically separates a rack's cool intake air from its hot exhaust air so the two do not mix, letting cooling systems work against a predictable, concentrated airflow rather than a mixed-temperature room.

**10. C**

The UPS bridges the short gap between a utility failure and the generator coming online, after which the generator sustains supply for as long as needed; the two serve complementary roles on different timescales, not interchangeable ones. Option D reverses which one does what.

### Topic 3 — IT Service Management (ITIL 4)

**11. C**

Incident management's job is to restore service as fast as possible, even via a workaround with the cause still unknown; problem management then investigates the underlying root cause to prevent recurrence, on its own, typically longer, timescale. Option A reverses the two.

**12. D**

A standard change is pre-authorised, low-risk and repeatable, so it can be executed without a fresh case-by-case approval each time — unlike a normal change (assessed individually) or an emergency change (expedited for an urgent fix, which option B actually describes).

**13. A**

The service catalogue is the customer-facing view: current, available services described in terms customers and users can act on, as distinct from the full service portfolio spanning every lifecycle stage.

**14. B**

Because this is an internal agreement between two teams within the same provider organisation, jointly supporting a commitment already made to an external customer, it is an OLA by definition; an SLA (option A) would instead sit directly between the provider and that customer.

**15. D**

The CMDB's role is to record configuration items and, crucially, their relationships, so that other ITSM processes can assess what depends on what and gauge the impact of an incident or change before acting.

### Topic 4 — Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery

**16. D**

RPO measures the maximum tolerable data loss (here, two hours' worth), and RTO measures the maximum tolerable downtime (here, eight hours) — swapping them, as option A does, is a classic reversed-pair trap.

**17. A**

The rule specifies three copies of the data, on two different media types, with one copy offsite, precisely so that no single failure mode can wipe out every copy simultaneously.

**18. B**

A warm site has infrastructure ready but needs some setup — restoring backups, powering systems up — before it can take over, trading some recovery time against a hot site's near-instant failover for a lower ongoing cost. Option D actually describes a cold site.

**19. C**

Switching back to the primary site once it has been restored and verified is failback, and it is generally considered riskier than the original failover precisely because it commits live production traffic back to a system that must first be carefully validated.

**20. A**

A BIA quantifies the financial and operational impact of an outage over different durations for each business function, which is exactly what determines the RTO and RPO that function should be assigned.

### Topic 5 — Infrastructure Monitoring, Capacity and Performance Management

**21. A**

Acting on a leading indicator (a disk filling at a known rate) to fix a problem before it causes an outage is the definition of proactive monitoring; reactive monitoring (option B) would instead only respond once the disk had already filled up.

**22. B**

Both extremes carry a cost: over-provisioning wastes budget on idle capacity, while under-provisioning causes exactly the performance problems and outages that show up when demand is highest; sound capacity planning sits between the two.

**23. C**

Metrics are aggregated numerical time series (good for dashboards and trend-based alerting), logs are discrete timestamped event records with context, and traces follow one request's journey across a distributed system's services. Option A swaps the definitions of metrics and logs.

**24. D**

Overly sensitive thresholds bury genuine, actionable alerts under low-value noise until they are ignored or delayed; the fix is to make alerts symptom-based and tied to real impact, not to keep adding more of them.

**25. C**

A documented baseline of normal behaviour is what lets a team tell an abnormal deviation apart from ordinary variation; without one, "abnormal" has no defined reference point.

### Topic 6 — Enterprise Architecture and Standards

**26. A**

The ADM is a cycle, not a one-off linear plan: it runs through Architecture Vision, the Business/Information Systems/Technology Architecture phases, Opportunities and Solutions, Migration Planning and Implementation Governance, then loops back to revisit the vision.

**27. B**

Governance is a continuous review discipline — checking design decisions and change proposals against agreed principles on an ongoing basis — not a single sign-off granted once at a project's outset.

**28. C**

A reference architecture is a reusable, vetted pattern meant to be adapted to a new context, not an already-deployed system in its own right — that distinction is what makes it valuable as a starting point for multiple projects.

**29. D**

Technical debt is the deferred cost of choosing a quicker, lower-quality solution now: like financial debt, it accrues in extra maintenance burden and risk the longer it is left unaddressed.

**30. A**

The as-is architecture captures the current, documented state of a domain, and the to-be architecture captures the intended target state; the entire point of transition planning is to define and close the gap between the two.

### Topic 7 — Infrastructure Security Fundamentals

**31. B**

A vulnerability under active exploitation needs to move through testing and deployment faster than routine updates, since the risk of leaving it open now outweighs the normal patch cadence — though skipping testing altogether, as much as ignoring the patch, would itself be a process failure.

**32. C**

Hardening reduces the attack surface of the host itself: disabling unneeded services and default accounts and applying a recognised baseline such as a CIS Benchmark. Firewall rules (option B) are a network-level control, not a host-hardening measure, which is exactly the kind of category error this topic tests.

**33. D**

Mature PAM vaults privileged credentials and grants elevated access only just-in-time, for a limited window, with sessions logged — minimising both how long and how broadly elevated access exists, rather than simply tracking who holds standing rights.

**34. A**

Defence in depth stacks independent layers (hardening, least privilege, patching, monitoring) so that no single layer's failure is enough, on its own, to cause a full compromise.

**35. B**

Granting a service account full administrative rights "just in case" is a direct violation of least privilege: if that account or its credentials are ever compromised, the attacker inherits everything the account can do, far beyond what the application actually needs.

### Topic 8 — Infrastructure Automation and Configuration Management

**36. C**

A declarative tool such as Terraform is told the desired end state and works out the steps to reach it, including on repeated runs; an imperative approach instead requires the engineer to specify and sequence every step, tracking the current state themselves. Option A reverses the two.

**37. D**

Once a server exists, a configuration management tool enforces and maintains its desired state — packages, services, files, accounts — over time; provisioning the underlying compute resource in the first place (option A) is a separate, earlier concern.

**38. A**

Configuration drift is exactly this unplanned divergence: a system's real configuration slowly diverges from its documented baseline, almost always because of manual changes applied outside the automated pipeline.

**39. B**

Idempotency means repeated runs against the same target converge on, and then simply confirm, the same end state, which is what makes automation safe to re-run after a failure or interruption.

**40. D**

A golden-image strategy bakes and tests a complete image once, then replaces instances wholesale when something needs to change, favouring consistency and simple rollback; continuous configuration management instead keeps long-lived servers updated in place, trading some of that consistency for not having to replace running infrastructure every time.

### Topic 1 — Compute, Storage and Virtualization Fundamentals

**41. B**

Thin provisioning defers physical allocation until data is actually written, allowing storage capacity to be over-committed across many virtual disks; thick provisioning reserves the full amount upfront, trading flexibility for the certainty that the space is guaranteed to be there. Option A reverses the two definitions.

**42. A**

Synchronous replication only acknowledges a write once it is confirmed at both sites, giving a near-zero RPO but making latency sensitive to the distance between sites; asynchronous replication acknowledges locally first, which scales across long distances but leaves a small window of unreplicated data exposed if the primary fails. This is exactly why synchronous replication is typically only practical over limited distances, contrary to what option D suggests.

**43. C**

HCI collapses compute, storage, and networking — and typically virtualization — into a single software-defined, centrally managed platform built from standardized, scalable nodes, replacing the traditional model of separately purchased and independently scaled SAN, network, and server tiers.

**44. A**

Live migration moves a running VM's active state between hosts while it continues to run, so the workload experiences little to no downtime — the standard mechanism for performing host maintenance or rebalancing load without an outage.

**45. B**

NVMe drives connect directly to the PCIe bus and use a command protocol built for flash memory, with many parallel command queues, avoiding the legacy AHCI/SCSI command stack and shallow queue depth that SATA and SAS inherited from spinning-disk-era design — this is the underlying reason for their throughput and latency advantage, not merely a different connector.

### Topic 2 — Data Centre Design and Facilities Management

**46. A**

Economizers use naturally cool outside air or water, when ambient conditions allow, to meet some or all of the facility's cooling load without running energy-intensive compressors, directly reducing the facility's energy overhead and therefore improving PUE.

**47. B**

A clean-agent system suppresses fire by displacing oxygen or interrupting combustion chemically, leaving no conductive residue and without discharging liquid onto live racks, whereas a water-based sprinkler discharge in a server room would likely destroy exactly the equipment the suppression system was meant to save.

**48. B**

Blanking panels close otherwise-open rack unit slots so that cold supply air cannot bypass through the empty space and mix directly with hot exhaust air on the other side of the rack — precisely the mixing that aisle containment is designed to prevent.

**49. A**

A DCIM platform gives a single view over a data centre's physical layer — power, cooling, floor space, rack and asset capacity — bridging facilities management and IT operations, distinct from a CMDB, which tracks IT service configuration items and their relationships rather than physical facility infrastructure.

**50. B**

Dual, independently-fed power supplies mean that the failure of an entire upstream path — a PDU, breaker, or UPS on one side — still leaves the server powered by its other, independent feed, which is the whole point of the A/B feed design; this requirement does not disappear at Tier IV, contrary to what option D suggests.

### Topic 3 — IT Service Management (ITIL 4)

**51. B**

The principle calls for breaking work into smaller increments, delivering them, and using the feedback from each cycle to steer the next one, rather than trying to specify and deliver everything in one large release before learning anything from real use.

**52. A**

The Service Value Chain is the operating model at the centre of the SVS: six activities that organisations combine into value streams appropriate to a given scenario, converting demand into value for customers and stakeholders.

**53. B**

A major incident triggers its own escalated procedure — typically a dedicated response team, more frequent stakeholder communication and a faster escalation path — precisely because its impact and urgency far exceed a routine incident, even though it still starts within the incident management process rather than skipping it.

**54. A**

A known error is a problem for which the root cause and a workaround have already been identified and documented, typically in a KEDB, so that the next incident traced back to the same problem can be resolved quickly by reusing the known workaround rather than re-investigating from scratch.

**55. C**

A request for something new and routine — like a licence — is a service request rather than an incident, since nothing is broken; it is normally handled via a standardised, pre-defined fulfilment process rather than the incident-management workflow.

### Topic 4 — Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery

**56. B**

A BCP covers how the whole organisation keeps essential functions running through a disruption — people, processes, facilities, suppliers — while a DRP is the narrower, technology-focused plan for restoring IT systems and data, usually functioning as one supporting component within the broader BCP.

**57. B**

MTD is the hard ceiling beyond which the disruption becomes existentially or financially unacceptable to the business; RTO is the target an organisation actually plans and invests to hit, and sound practice always keeps RTO safely inside MTD rather than pushing right up against or past it.

**58. A**

Incrementals only capture what changed since the last backup of any kind, so each one is quick and small but a full restore must replay the whole chain in order; differentials capture everything since the last full backup, growing larger over time but needing only the last full plus the latest differential to restore.

**59. A**

A tabletop exercise is a low-risk, discussion-based walkthrough of roles and plan steps with nothing actually invoked, while a full interruption test genuinely fails services over to the DR site, validating real technical recovery capability at a materially higher operational risk if something does not go as planned.

**60. B**

By replicating changes continuously or at very short intervals rather than waiting for the next scheduled backup window, CDP dramatically shrinks the amount of data that could ever be lost, which is exactly what a tighter RPO means.

### Topic 5 — Infrastructure Monitoring, Capacity and Performance Management

**61. A**

An SLI is what you actually measure (e.g. success rate), an SLO is the target you set for that measurement (e.g. 99.9%), and the error budget is simply the allowed shortfall between the SLO and perfection, which teams can deliberately spend on releasing riskier changes rather than treating every SLO breach as an emergency.

**62. A**

Synthetic monitoring runs scripted checks against a service on a schedule from known locations, so it can surface problems even when real traffic is light or absent; RUM instead observes actual user sessions, capturing genuine real-world variability, but only when and where real users are actually present.

**63. B**

MTTD measures how long an incident goes unnoticed before anyone becomes aware of it, while MTTR measures how long it then takes to actually resolve it once response begins; both shorten overall impact when reduced, but they describe different segments of the incident timeline.

**64. B**

A mean can be pulled down by a large mass of fast requests, hiding a meaningful slower tail; p95/p99 explicitly report what the slowest fraction of requests actually experienced, which is usually what drives real user complaints even when the average looks healthy.

**65. B**

Horizontal scaling spreads load across more nodes, which also tends to improve fault tolerance since no single node's failure removes all capacity; vertical scaling instead grows the resources of one existing server, which is often simpler to set up but eventually hits a hardware ceiling and remains a single point of failure. Option A reverses the two.

### Topic 6 — Enterprise Architecture and Standards

**66. A**

TOGAF recognises Business, Data, Application and Technology as the four architecture domains, together often abbreviated BDAT, covering strategy and process, the organisation's data assets, the individual application systems, and the underlying software/hardware infrastructure respectively.

**67. B**

Enterprise architecture takes the organisation-wide view, aligning strategy, capability and technology across every domain and project, while solution architecture works within that broader framework to design a single system or project, applying the enterprise's standards rather than setting them.

**68. B**

ArchiMate is a modelling notation for visually representing architecture, while TOGAF's ADM defines the process for developing it; they are complementary and commonly paired, with ArchiMate diagrams used to express the deliverables the ADM calls for at each phase.

**69. B**

The Architecture Repository is where reference architectures, standards, patterns and governance artefacts from past and ongoing ADM cycles are stored, so new architecture work can build on what already exists instead of starting from a blank page every time.

**70. B**

The EIF defines interoperability across legal, organisational, semantic and technical layers, running from aligned legislation and coordinated processes, through a shared and precise meaning for exchanged data, down to the technical standards that let systems actually connect — a structure specifically built for cross-border public-sector digital services.

### Topic 7 — Infrastructure Security Fundamentals

**71. B**

Zero Trust requires every access request to be explicitly verified based on identity, device posture and context, regardless of whether it originates inside or outside the traditional network boundary, rather than assuming anything already inside the perimeter is automatically trustworthy the way a castle-and-moat model does.

**72. B**

Segmenting the network into smaller, controlled zones limits how far an attacker who compromises one part of the network can move laterally into others, containing the blast radius of a breach rather than leaving every system reachable from any compromised point on a flat network.

**73. B**

A vulnerability scan automatically surfaces known potential weaknesses across many systems quickly and cheaply, while a penetration test has a skilled human actively try to exploit — and chain together — vulnerabilities to demonstrate real-world impact, typically over a narrower scope and at higher cost and depth.

**74. A**

Encryption at rest protects data sitting on disk or in backups if the media is stolen or improperly accessed, while encryption in transit protects data as it crosses a network from interception; a system exposed to both risks genuinely needs both, since securing one does not automatically secure the other.

**75. B**

A SIEM's core value is correlating log and event data from many systems in near real time and applying detection rules and analytics to surface suspicious patterns that would be very hard to notice by reviewing each system's logs individually, going well beyond passive long-term archival.

### Topic 8 — Infrastructure Automation and Configuration Management

**76. A**

In a push model, a central controller connects out to targets and applies configuration on demand with no persistent agent required; in a pull model, an agent running on each node checks in with a central server on its own schedule and pulls down whatever configuration applies to it.

**77. B**
