# 5. Succeeding in the EUFTE (Written Test)

The EUFTE — the *free-text essay on EU matters* — is the last test in the EPSO/AD/429/26 (4) selection procedure that is actually scored before your application file is checked for admissibility. By the time you sit it, you have already passed the reasoning tests and cleared the field-related multiple-choice test for your area (ICT Infrastructure, ICT Project Management, Clouds & Networks, or Data Science). EUFTE is deliberately different in kind from everything that came before it: there is no bank of correct/incorrect statements to train against, no single right answer to memorise, and no partial credit for knowing more EU facts than the next candidate. It is a 40-minute writing exercise, and it is graded on how well you write, not on what you already know.

This chapter explains what the test actually measures, how to use your 40 minutes, the mistakes that cost candidates the most points, walks through one fully worked example from briefing to finished essay, and gives you a set of practice prompts modelled on the kind of briefing-plus-task format EPSO uses — each with a model outline (not a full sample essay) and a self-assessment checklist you can apply to your own drafts.

## What EUFTE Actually Measures

This is the single most important thing to understand before you write a word, because it is also the point candidates most often get wrong.

**EUFTE does not test your knowledge of EU affairs.** It does not reward candidates who can cite institutions, treaties, policy names, or recent EU news beyond what's in the material handed to you at the exam. **EUFTE does not test your field-specific technical knowledge either** — you already proved you know your ICT domain in the field-related MCQ that preceded this test. Writing a technically sophisticated answer that goes beyond the source material earns you nothing here, and the time you spend on it is time you didn't spend on the five things that *are* graded.

What EUFTE tests is **written communication skill**: can you take a short piece of reference material, understand what it says, and produce a piece of writing — for a stated audience and a stated purpose — that is logically structured, concise, clear, correctly pitched, and grounded in that material? That's it. Nothing else is being marked.

This is the same discipline that verbal reasoning demands of you, just inverted. In verbal reasoning, you had to resist the urge to answer using outside knowledge instead of what the passage actually said. In EUFTE, you have to resist the urge to write about what *you* know instead of what the **materials provided by EPSO** actually say. A polished, well-organised essay that is only loosely anchored in the source material will score worse than a plainer essay that clearly and economically uses what was given to you. Personal opinions on the topic, general EU trivia, and technical elaboration beyond what the task asks for are not just unrewarded — they cost you the minutes and words you needed for the parts that *are* being assessed.

## Format at a Glance

| | |
|---|---|
| Duration | 40 minutes |
| Task | One free-text writing task on an EU-related topic |
| Reference material | Provided as part of the test itself — you must use it |
| Score | 0–10 |
| Pass mark | 5/10 |
| Language | Language 2 |
| Position in the procedure | After the reasoning tests and the field-related MCQ; the last scored test before the admissibility check |

Because reference material is supplied at the exam, EUFTE is open-book in the narrowest possible sense: you are given exactly the facts you're allowed to use, and no more. There is nothing to "study" for the content of the essay itself — your preparation should go into the skill of writing well, quickly, and in a disciplined way from a short source document, which is exactly what the practice section below is for.

## The Five Evaluation Anchors

EPSO's own description of the written test sets out five things it evaluates. These are not vague ideals — they are the actual grading dimensions a corrector will be looking for in your script, so it is worth internalising each one individually.

> - Structuring communication to reflect logical idea progression
> - Writing concisely, without unnecessary words or phrases
> - Presenting content clearly and comprehensibly
> - Adapting writing to the intended audience and purpose
> - Using the provided materials effectively

**Structuring communication to reflect logical idea progression.** Your reader should never have to guess why one paragraph follows another. State your main point early, then support it — don't make the reader wait for the conclusion. Each paragraph should carry one idea, and the order of paragraphs should itself tell a story: context, then substance, then implication or recommendation.

*Example — weak:* "The pilot ran for six months and tested three tools; feedback was mixed, and before discussing next steps it is worth noting that budget was limited throughout." (buries the point under background) *Strong:* "The pilot shows one of the three tools clearly outperforms the others and should be rolled out first." (states the point; the background can follow, briefly, in support of it)

**Writing concisely, without unnecessary words or phrases.** Every sentence should earn its place. If you can delete a sentence and the paragraph loses no meaning, delete it. Concision is not about writing short sentences for their own sake — it's about not making the reader read words that carry no information.

*Example — weak:* "It should be noted that, due to the fact that response times increased, this is something that will need to be addressed at some point in the near future." *Strong:* "Response times increased and need addressing soon."

**Presenting content clearly and comprehensibly.** Plain, direct language beats impressive-sounding language. Avoid jargon unless the material itself uses it and the audience would recognise it. A reader on their first and only read-through should understand you without having to re-read a sentence.

*Example — weak:* "Stakeholder engagement was operationalised via a multi-channel outreach modality to optimise uptake." *Strong:* "We contacted stakeholders by email, phone, and in person to increase participation."

**Adapting writing to the intended audience and purpose.** A note to a Director asking for a decision reads differently from a plain-language explainer for colleagues, which reads differently again from talking points for a spokesperson. The task will always tell you who you are writing for and why — that instruction is not decoration, it is part of what's graded.

*Example — weak (to a Director who needs a decision):* "There are many interesting angles to this pilot, and reasonable people could disagree about how to interpret the data, though of course further study might be warranted." *Strong:* "I recommend proceeding with Option B; here is why."

**Using the provided materials effectively.** Every factual claim in your answer should be traceable to something in the briefing. This includes using the material's own facts and figures accurately (not distorting or exaggerating them) and using enough of what's relevant — not just the first fact you noticed, but the material that actually supports the task you were asked to do.

*Example — weak:* "Studies generally show that training programmes improve outcomes." (a generic claim, not tied to the briefing) *Strong:* "This programme's own survey found 65% of participants reported improved outcomes — well above the 50% baseline set at launch." (grounded in the specific figures you were given)

## Budgeting the 40 Minutes

EPSO does not publish an official minute-by-minute breakdown, but the following split is a reasonable strategy worth rehearsing until it becomes automatic:

- **~5 minutes — read and plan.** Read the material once for content, then re-read the task instruction carefully: who is the audience, what is the purpose, what does a complete answer need to cover? Jot a skeleton — opening point, 2–4 supporting points in a logical order, a closing line — before you write a single sentence of prose.
- **~30 minutes — write.** Follow your skeleton. Don't stop to polish sentence by sentence; get the structure and content down first.
- **~5 minutes — review.** Re-read once, specifically hunting for filler words to cut, sentences that repeat a point already made, and any claim that isn't actually supported by the material. Check the opening line still states your main point.

The exact split matters less than the discipline of *having* a planning phase and a review phase at all — both are easy to skip under time pressure, and both are exactly what candidates who run out of time or hand in a disorganised answer tend to have skipped.

**If you're running behind at around the 20-minute mark**, cut scope, not quality: drop your weakest supporting point rather than rushing every point equally to fit them all in. A shorter answer that stays well-structured and still reaches a clear recommendation scores better than a longer one that trails off unfinished or gets rushed and sloppy in its closing lines.

## Common Mistakes

- **Ignoring the provided materials and writing generically.** Falling back on what you already know about "digital transformation" or EU policy in the abstract, instead of the specific facts in front of you, is the single most common way candidates lose marks on "using the provided materials effectively."
- **No clear thesis, weak paragraph structure.** Starting to write before deciding what the main point is, then discovering the point somewhere in the middle of the answer. The reader — and the corrector — should get your main point in the first sentence or two.
- **Padding with filler instead of being concise.** Phrases like "it is important to note that," "in order to," "at this point in time," or "needless to say" add length without adding information, and directly work against the concision anchor.
- **Wrong tone or register for the stated audience.** Writing a casual, chatty note when the task calls for a formal briefing to a Director, or writing an over-formal, jargon-heavy note when the task calls for a plain-language explanation to non-specialist colleagues.
- **Running out of time because there was no planning phase.** Diving straight into writing, realising halfway through that the structure doesn't work, and either rushing an unstructured ending or leaving the answer incomplete.
- **Restating the briefing instead of synthesising it.** Rephrasing each sentence of the source material in order, without drawing out the connections between facts (cause and effect, trade-offs, what matters most for the stated task), reads as a summary rather than an analysis — and using the materials "effectively" means doing something with them, not just reporting them back.

## A Fully Worked Example

The practice prompts below deliberately give you an outline, not a full essay, so that you build the skill of turning your own outline into finished prose under time pressure — copying someone else's finished answer teaches you nothing about writing your own. Before you start on them, though, it helps to see one complete worked example: a full briefing, a full ~250-word answer written to it, and a walkthrough of why that answer scores well against each of the five anchors. Read the answer once for the writing itself, then reread it alongside the breakdown that follows.

> **Service Review Note — IT Service Desk, First-Contact Resolution**
> *IT Operations Unit*
>
> Over the past quarter, the IT Service Desk received 4,200 tickets, of which 58% were resolved on first contact, against an internal target of 75%. A breakdown by category shows that password-reset and access-request tickets — 45% of total volume — have a first-contact resolution rate of 91%, while software-installation and configuration tickets — 30% of total volume — have a first-contact resolution rate of only 22%, with most of these requiring escalation to a second-line specialist.
>
> An internal review found that first-line agents have access to a self-service password-reset tool integrated into their ticketing system, but no equivalent guided tool for common software-installation requests; agents currently rely on a shared document of ad-hoc notes for these cases.
>
> Two options are proposed for next quarter: building a guided self-service tool for the ten most common software-installation requests, estimated to take six weeks of developer time, or running a two-day refresher training session for all first-line agents on existing installation procedures, which could be delivered within two weeks. The Head of IT Operations must choose which option to pursue before next quarter's resourcing is finalised.

**Task:** Draft a briefing note (around 250 words) for the Head of IT Operations, summarising the review's findings and recommending which option to pursue.

**Full model answer**

Overall first-contact resolution stands at 58% this quarter, well below our 75% target — but the shortfall is concentrated almost entirely in one ticket category, not spread evenly across the desk.

Password-reset and access-request tickets, 45% of volume, already resolve on first contact 91% of the time, thanks to the self-service tool agents have for these cases. Software-installation tickets, 30% of volume, tell the opposite story: only 22% resolve on first contact, and most escalate to a second-line specialist. The review traces this gap to tooling, not skill — agents have no equivalent guided tool for installation requests and currently rely on an ad-hoc shared document.

Two options are on the table for next quarter. A guided self-service tool for the ten most common installation requests would take six weeks to build, but would close the same tooling gap that already makes the password-reset category succeed. A two-day refresher training session could be delivered in two weeks, but would not close that gap — agents already know the procedures; what they lack is the guided support that works for password resets.

Since the review identifies a tooling gap, not a knowledge gap, as the root cause, I recommend committing developer time to the self-service tool now, even though it takes longer to deliver, over the faster but narrower training option. Please confirm before next quarter's resourcing is finalised.

**Why this scores well**

- *Structure:* The first sentence states the headline (a specific shortfall, not a general problem) before any supporting detail. Each paragraph then does one job — the two categories, the two options, the recommendation — in the order a reader needs them.
- *Concision:* No sentence restates a figure already given. "58%... against our 75% target" is stated once, not repeated as "significantly below target" a few lines later.
- *Clarity:* Plain language throughout ("tell the opposite story," "closes the same gap") rather than jargon like "cross-functional tooling remediation."
- *Audience and purpose:* Written as a decision request to a named decision-maker, ends with an explicit ask ("please confirm"), and skips any background the Head of IT Operations doesn't need (there's no explanation of what a ticketing system is).
- *Using the materials:* Every figure (58%, 75%, 45%/91%, 30%/22%, six weeks, two weeks) comes directly from the briefing, and the recommendation is justified by the review's own stated cause (a tooling gap) rather than an outside opinion about IT support best practice.

Notice what the answer does *not* do: it doesn't add background on service-desk management in general, doesn't hedge the recommendation across multiple sentences, and doesn't include a sentence that isn't pulling its weight toward the task. That discipline — say only what the task needs, in the order the reader needs it — is what these five anchors are actually asking for, and it's exactly what the practice prompts below ask you to rehearse on your own.

## Practice Prompts

Every briefing below is invented for practice purposes: the bodies, programmes, figures and named units are fictional, written in a plausible EU-institutional register, and cover safe, non-controversial subject matter — the same principle the verbal reasoning texts follow. Topics vary deliberately (digital policy, environment, public administration reform, data protection, EU funding, ICT governance, staff mobility, public procurement, multilingualism, recruitment, research funding, occupational health, external relations, digital accessibility) so you get used to adapting quickly to unfamiliar subject matter, which is exactly what the real test will ask of you.

For each prompt: read the briefing once, read the task, plan for a few minutes, then write a full timed draft (40 minutes is the realistic target once you're comfortable with the format; when starting out, it's fine to allow yourself longer and tighten the time as you improve — see the closing section). The word counts given are suggested targets for practice only; EPSO does not publish a fixed word limit, and writing an appropriately complete answer within the available time matters more than hitting an exact number.

Each model outline is a skeleton, not a sample essay — it shows you *how* a strong answer would be built, not what to copy.

### Practice Prompt 1 — Digital Policy: Cross-Border File Exchange

> **Internal Note — Cross-Border Case File Exchange Pilot**
> *Interinstitutional Digital Cooperation Unit*
>
> Between January and June, three national administrations and one EU agency piloted the electronic exchange of case files that had previously been sent by post or as unstructured email attachments. In the pilot's first phase, 40% of files required manual reformatting on arrival because sending and receiving systems used incompatible formats, adding an average of 6 working days to case handling time.
>
> In the pilot's second phase, participants adopted a shared data standard, the "Common Exchange Format v2," for all new files. Manual reformatting fell to under 5% of files, and the added handling time fell to an average of 1 working day. Participant feedback was strongly positive, though two participants noted that adopting the standard required a one-off investment in updating their local systems.
>
> The pilot team's closing report recommends extending use of the Common Exchange Format v2 to all participating bodies, coordinated through a new working group, but flags that a wider rollout would require a budget line and formal sign-off, as some administrations will need technical support to make the switch.

**Task:** You are a policy officer in the Interinstitutional Digital Cooperation Unit. Draft a briefing note (around 250 words) for your Director, who has not read the full pilot report, summarising the findings and recommending whether and how to proceed.

**Model answer — outline only**
- Opening: state the headline finding first — the pilot shows a shared data standard cuts handling delays sharply — before any background detail.
- Point 1: the problem the pilot addressed (incompatible formats, 40% manual reformatting, 6-day average delay).
- Point 2: what worked (Common Exchange Format v2 adopted in phase two; reformatting under 5%; delay down to 1 day).
- Point 3: the catch (wider rollout needs budget sign-off and technical support for some administrations — don't omit this, a Director needs the full picture to decide).
- Closing: a clear recommendation (proceed to phased rollout via the proposed working group) and what decision is being requested of the Director.

**Self-assessment checklist**
- Does my opening sentence give the Director the headline finding, rather than making them read to the end to find it?
- Did I cut every phrase that restates something I'd already said (e.g., saying the pilot "worked well" twice in different words)?
- Would a Director who skims this once understand the recommendation without needing to re-read a sentence?
- Is the tone appropriately concise and decision-oriented for a Director, rather than reading like a technical report for engineers?
- Does every number and claim in my draft come from the briefing, with nothing added from what I personally know about IT interoperability projects?

### Practice Prompt 2 — Environment: Sustainability Progress Review

> **Note for the Record — Business Travel and Office Resources, First-Half Review**
> *Corporate Sustainability Team*
>
> Business flights booked by staff fell by 18% compared with the same period last year, a reduction the team attributes mainly to the "hybrid-meeting-by-default" policy introduced in January, which asks staff to justify in-person travel for meetings that could be held remotely. Estimated associated CO2 savings from this reduction are significant and are detailed in Annex 1 (not reproduced here).
>
> Paper printing volumes, by contrast, fell by only 4%, against a first-half target of 15%. A survey of heavy-printing units found the main reasons cited were the continued habit of printing documents for in-person meetings and a lack of awareness of the digital annotation tools now available on staff laptops.
>
> Two options are under consideration for the second half of the year: (1) expanding the video-conferencing equipment budget to further support the travel-reduction trend, or (2) launching a targeted "digital annotation" training and awareness campaign aimed specifically at the heaviest-printing units. Both would require budget approval at the next Corporate Services planning meeting; the team does not have the budget to do both at full scale this year.

**Task:** Draft a briefing note (around 250 words) for the Director of Corporate Services, summarising the first-half data and recommending which of the two options should be prioritised for the second half of the year.

**Model answer — outline only**
- Opening: one-line summary of the overall picture — good progress on travel, printing lagging well behind target.
- Point 1: the travel result and its likely cause (hybrid-meeting policy), framed as a success worth reinforcing.
- Point 2: the printing shortfall against target and the survey's explanation for it (habit, lack of awareness of digital tools).
- Point 3: the two options, stated briefly and neutrally, with the resource constraint (can't fund both at full scale) made explicit.
- Closing: a reasoned recommendation between the two options based on what the briefing actually shows (e.g., printing is furthest from target and its cause — lack of awareness — is directly addressed by the training option), plus the next step (budget approval at the planning meeting).

**Self-assessment checklist**
- Does my note lead with the overall picture before going into the two separate data points?
- Have I stated the recommendation once, clearly, rather than hedging across several sentences?
- Is each of the two options described in a way a non-specialist reader would immediately understand?
- Have I matched the tone to a briefing note for a Director (concise, decision-focused) rather than a general sustainability essay?
- Is my recommendation justified only by what the note tells me (the target shortfall and its stated cause), not by my own views on environmental policy?

### Practice Prompt 3 — Public Administration Reform: Hybrid-Work Pilot

> **Summary of Survey Results — Hybrid-Working Pilot, Four Directorates**
> *Staff Wellbeing and Organisation Unit*
>
> The six-month hybrid-working pilot, which set two fixed "core days" per week for in-person attendance, has concluded. A survey of pilot participants (612 responses, 74% response rate) found that 72% of respondents reported improved work-life balance, and 65% reported no negative effect on their perceived productivity.
>
> However, 35% of team leads reported difficulty scheduling collaborative sessions, citing conflicting core days across teams that work together. Actual attendance on core days averaged 61%, against an 80% target set at the start of the pilot, with several team leads noting that "core day" is often treated as a guideline rather than a fixed commitment.
>
> Two options have been proposed for the next phase: making core-day attendance a mandatory, tracked commitment, or keeping the current flexible approach but investing in shared scheduling tools so teams can align their core days with each other. The Unit needs a recommendation to bring to the Directors' meeting next week.

**Task:** Write a memo (around 250 words) to your Head of Unit, explaining the pilot's results and recommending whether core-day attendance should be made mandatory.

**Model answer — outline only**
- Opening: the pilot's overall verdict in one line — well received by staff, but the core-day mechanism itself isn't working as designed.
- Point 1: the positive results (work-life balance, no productivity loss) — establishes the pilot is broadly worth continuing.
- Point 2: the specific problem (low core-day attendance, scheduling conflicts between teams) — this is the actual decision point.
- Point 3: what the data suggests about which fix addresses the real cause (a scheduling/coordination problem, not necessarily a compliance problem) — sets up the recommendation.
- Closing: a clear recommendation (which of the two options, or a combination) and the immediate next step (input needed before the Directors' meeting).

**Self-assessment checklist**
- Does my memo separate "is the pilot worth keeping" from "does the core-day mechanism need fixing," in a clear logical order?
- Did I avoid restating the same statistic in two different sentences?
- Is my recommendation understandable to someone who has not seen the survey, without needing background explanation?
- Have I written this as a memo to a Head of Unit ahead of a decision, not as a general reflection on remote work?
- Does my recommendation follow from the attendance/scheduling data given, rather than from a general opinion about mandatory attendance policies?

### Practice Prompt 4 — Data Protection: Retention Policy Update

> **Records Management Note — Updated Retention Schedule, Case-Management System**
> *Data Protection and Records Unit*
>
> Following a legal basis review, the retention period for personal data held in closed case files on the case-management system will change from 10 years to 5 years from the date a case is closed. Audit log entries linked to each file will be retained separately for 3 years. The review concluded that the shorter periods are sufficient to meet the applicable legal and administrative requirements while better respecting data minimisation principles.
>
> Deletion of data past the new retention periods will be carried out automatically by the system starting from the first of next month; no manual action is needed for records already in the system. However, staff who have kept local copies of case documents (on shared drives, in email, or printed) must delete those local copies once the corresponding case file is deleted from the system, as local copies are not covered by the automated deletion.
>
> Questions about specific cases can be directed to the Records Management help desk.

**Task:** You work in the unit that manages the case-management system. Write a short, plain-language memo (around 200 words) to colleagues across the department — who are not data-protection specialists — explaining what is changing and what they personally need to do.

**Model answer — outline only**
- Opening: one plain sentence stating what's changing (retention period for closed case files is getting shorter) and when.
- Point 1: the new periods in plain terms (5 years for case files, 3 years for audit logs) — no need to restate the legal-review reasoning in detail, just enough context.
- Point 2: what happens automatically (system deletion) vs. what colleagues must do themselves (delete local copies) — this is the actionable part and should be the most prominent.
- Closing: where to go with questions (help desk), stated briefly.

**Self-assessment checklist**
- Does my memo tell colleagues what they need to *do*, not just what the policy *is*?
- Have I avoided legal/technical jargon that a non-specialist reader wouldn't need or understand?
- Is the memo short enough that a busy colleague would actually read it in full?
- Is the tone appropriately plain and direct for an internal all-colleagues memo, rather than formal legal-note style?
- Have I kept the retention periods and the deletion mechanics exactly as stated in the note, without adding assumptions about what "local copies" might mean?

### Practice Prompt 5 — EU Funding Programme: Mid-Term Results

> **Mid-Term Evaluation Summary — Digital Skills Facility (Years 1–3 of 5)**
> *Programme Evaluation Team*
>
> The Digital Skills Facility, a grant programme co-funded with participating countries to improve digital literacy, has trained 54,000 people by the end of Year 3, ahead of the original target of 50,000 for this stage. Overall budget absorption stands at 61%.
>
> Results are uneven across the programme's two strands. The "public-sector digital literacy" strand has used 84% of its allocated budget and exceeded its training targets. The "small business digital skills" strand, by contrast, has used only 30% of its allocated budget; grant applications from small businesses have been consistently low, and an applicant survey found the main barrier cited was the length and complexity of the application form.
>
> The evaluation team's draft recommendations are to simplify the small-business application form for Year 4 and to consider reallocating a portion of unused small-business funding to the public-sector strand, where demand continues to exceed the original allocation, pending a final decision by the Programme Board.

**Task:** Draft a set of talking points/briefing note (around 250 words) for the Head of Communications, who will present the mid-term results at an internal stakeholders' meeting (not a public/press event). The note should highlight what's working, flag the concern clearly, and note the recommended fix, in a tone suitable for an informed internal audience.

**Model answer — outline only**
- Opening: overall headline (ahead of target on training numbers, but absorption uneven across strands) — gives the audience the full picture in one line before the detail.
- Point 1: the success story (public-sector strand, budget use and targets) — stated with the actual figures.
- Point 2: the concern (small-business strand's low absorption and the stated reason — form complexity, not lack of interest).
- Point 3: the recommended fix (simplify the form; possible reallocation), framed as pending a Programme Board decision, not a done deal.
- Closing: a short line anchoring the overall message the Head of Communications should leave the room with.

**Self-assessment checklist**
- Does the note give the full headline (good and less-good news together) up front, rather than only positive framing?
- Have I cut any sentence that repeats the same figure or point in different words?
- Would an internal stakeholder unfamiliar with this programme follow the note without needing extra explanation?
- Is the tone calibrated for an internal stakeholders' meeting (candid, evaluative) rather than external public-facing messaging?
- Have I kept the recommendation framed exactly as "pending a Programme Board decision," as stated in the source, rather than presenting it as already agreed?

### Practice Prompt 6 — ICT Governance: Security Incident Reporting

> **Annual Summary — Security Incident Reports, Three Participating Bodies**
> *Interinstitutional IT Security Board Secretariat*
>
> Reported phishing attempts across the three participating bodies rose by 22% compared with the previous reporting year. Despite this, the number of successful compromises (cases where a user's credentials or device were actually affected) fell by 40% over the same period. The Board's assessment attributes the drop in successful compromises mainly to the mandatory security-awareness training rolled out mid-year.
>
> However, completion of the mandatory training stood at 68% of staff by year end, against a Board target of 90%. Completion rates varied significantly between bodies, with one participating body reaching only 52% completion. The Secretariat's analysis suggests the gap is concentrated among staff who joined after the initial training rollout and were not automatically re-enrolled.
>
> The Secretariat is preparing recommendations for the Board's next meeting and has asked each member to come with a proposed action to close the completion gap before the next reporting year.

**Task:** As secretary to the Board, write a briefing note (around 250 words) for Board members summarising the year's figures and proposing one concrete action to close the training completion gap.

**Model answer — outline only**
- Opening: the headline result — awareness training is working (compromises down), but completion is well short of target, which is a risk to sustaining that result.
- Point 1: the positive trend (phishing attempts up, but successful compromises down 40%, credited to training).
- Point 2: the completion shortfall (68% vs. 90% target, with one body notably lower) and its likely cause (new joiners not re-enrolled).
- Point 3: the proposed concrete action, directly targeted at the stated cause (e.g., automatic enrolment of new joiners into the training as part of onboarding).
- Closing: what's being asked of Board members (bring their own proposed action to the next meeting, per the Secretariat's request).

**Self-assessment checklist**
- Does my note connect the completion gap to the risk it creates (sustaining the drop in compromises), rather than presenting the two figures as unrelated?
- Have I removed any sentence that only restates that training "is important" without adding new information?
- Is the note clear enough that a Board member from a different technical background than mine would follow it without difficulty?
- Is the tone and level of detail appropriate for a Board-level briefing rather than a technical incident report?
- Is my proposed action grounded in the specific cause the note identifies (new joiners not re-enrolled), rather than a generic security-awareness suggestion?

### Practice Prompt 7 — Staff Mobility: Interinstitutional Secondment Scheme

> **Note on the Interinstitutional Mobility Scheme — First Round Results**
> *Human Resources Development Unit*
>
> The new Interinstitutional Mobility Scheme, allowing staff to spend three months on secondment in another participating EU body, closed its first application round with 120 applications for 40 available places. A survey of participants at the end of their secondment found that 89% would recommend the scheme to a colleague, citing exposure to new working methods and professional networks as the main benefits.
>
> The most common piece of feedback, raised by over half of respondents, was that the application timeline and required paperwork were unclear at the outset, causing some applicants to submit incomplete applications in the first round. In response, the Unit has simplified the application form from six pages to two and published a fixed timeline for the second round, opening next month; the shorter form and the timeline are both now posted on the Scheme's page on the interinstitutional mobility intranet portal.
>
> The Unit would like to encourage broad participation in the second round, particularly from staff who did not apply in the first round due to the paperwork concerns now addressed.

**Task:** Write a short intranet announcement (around 200 words) for all staff, explaining what the scheme is, what has changed based on first-round feedback, and how to apply, in an inviting, plain-language tone.

**Model answer — outline only**
- Opening: what the scheme offers, in one inviting sentence (three-month secondment to another EU body) plus a strong signal of quality (participant satisfaction figure).
- Point 1: brief context — first round's popularity (120 applications for 40 places) framed positively.
- Point 2: what's changed and why it matters to a prospective applicant (shorter form, fixed timeline) — directly addresses the barrier raised by past applicants.
- Closing: a direct call to action — when the second round opens and where to find the application (the Scheme's page on the mobility intranet portal).

**Self-assessment checklist**
- Does my announcement open with what the scheme offers the reader, rather than with administrative background first?
- Have I trimmed any sentence that a busy colleague skimming the intranet wouldn't need?
- Is the announcement written so that someone who has never heard of the scheme understands it fully in one read?
- Is the tone genuinely inviting and plain, matching an all-staff announcement rather than a formal HR policy note?
- Have I described the changes to the process (form length, timeline) exactly as stated, without adding detail the note doesn't give?

### Practice Prompt 8 — Public Procurement: Framework Contract Renewal

## Editing for Concision, and What Matters Most: Timed Practice

A few self-editing habits are worth drilling until they become automatic, since you will only have a few minutes to apply them at the exam:

- **Cut filler.** Phrases like "it should be noted that," "in order to," "due to the fact that," "at this point in time," and "needless to say" can almost always be deleted or replaced with a single word ("because," "now") without losing meaning.
- **Combine short, choppy sentences that share a subject.** Two sentences that both describe the same fact from different angles can usually become one tighter sentence.
- **Lead with the point.** Especially in a note to a Director or any busy reader, put the conclusion or recommendation first and the justification after — not the other way around.
- **Test every sentence with one question: does removing it lose any information the task needs?** If not, it's a candidate for the cutting-room floor.

Beyond technique, one thing matters more for EUFTE than any further content study: **actually writing under the 40-minute clock.** Reading about structure and concision is necessary but not sufficient — the skill only becomes reliable under time pressure once you've rehearsed it under time pressure. Work through the practice prompts above (and, once you're comfortable, briefings you write for yourself from any short EU-related text) as full timed exercises — by hand, or in the platform's essay editor once available — rather than reading them and mentally sketching an answer. As noted in the selection procedure overview, EUFTE has the lowest pass mark of any scored test in this competition, but it is also the phase candidates tend to underestimate — the format, not the subject matter, is what decides the outcome.
