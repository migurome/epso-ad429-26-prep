# 6. Preparing for Test Day

Every test in this competition — reasoning, field-related MCQ, and EUFTE — is taken **remotely, from your own computer, on a date and time fixed in advance**. There is no test centre to travel to. This sounds convenient, but it shifts a real share of the risk onto your own setup: a webcam that won't cooperate, a browser that isn't supported, or a housemate walking into frame can cost you as much as a hard question can. This chapter collects what EPSO documents about the remote testing conditions, plus the practical habits candidates and prep sites recommend for the parts EPSO doesn't spell out in detail.

Treat the specifics below (exact RAM figures, provider names, minute-by-minute timing) as **the general shape of the requirement, not a substitute for your own invitation email**. EPSO sends detailed, sitting-specific instructions once you're admitted to test, and those instructions override anything here if they differ.

## The Format: Remote Proctored Testing

Since 2023, EPSO runs all computer-based tests as **remotely proctored exams**: you sit the test on your own device, at home (or wherever you choose), while a remote proctor monitors you by webcam and screen-share to confirm you're following the rules. There are no physical test centres to book. EPSO currently works with two testing platforms/providers — **TAO** (Open Assessment Technologies), the newer and now primary platform, and **Prometric**, used as a secondary option — so the exact interface you see can vary slightly by competition; the mechanics described below apply to both.

One thing worth knowing going in: in November 2024 the European Data Protection Supervisor formally reprimanded EPSO over how a previous version of this remote-proctoring system (used in 2023) handled candidates' biometric data and consent. EPSO had already discontinued that specific setup by the time of the ruling, and as part of the fallout it committed to two candidate-facing improvements: the right to **redo a test** if a technical or platform failure affected you, and **automatic disclosure of your answers and the correct answers** after testing closes. Neither of these is something you need to request specially — they're standing commitments EPSO made in response to the ruling — but it's useful to know they exist if your test session goes wrong through no fault of your own.

## Technical Requirements

Run EPSO's official system/compatibility check **at least 48 hours before your test**, not the night before — it's the only way to catch a driver, permissions, or firewall problem while you still have time to fix it. As a rule of thumb, the platform expects:

| | |
|---|---|
| Device | Laptop or desktop computer. Tablets and mobile phones are not supported, whatever their specs. |
| Corporate/work computers | Usually won't work unless you have full administrator rights — corporate firewalls and security policies routinely block the proctoring software even when the automated check passes. Use a personal machine if you can. |
| Operating system | Windows 10 or later, macOS 10.15 or later, or Ubuntu 18.04 or later. |
| Browser | A current version of Chrome or Edge. |
| Memory / disk | A modest amount of free RAM and disk space (on the order of a few GB) — the automated check will tell you if you're short. |
| Screen | **One screen only.** Dual-monitor setups (including a laptop with an external monitor both active) are explicitly not permitted — if you use a laptop with an external monitor, you must connect the external screen and close the laptop lid, which also disables the laptop's built-in camera and keyboard. Recommended resolution 1920×1080, minimum 1024×768, text scaling at 100%. |
| Power | Plug your computer directly into a power socket — not through a docking station, which some platforms flag as a prohibited device. |
| Webcam | A **movable** webcam (built-in on a laptop is fine, as long as you can tilt/rotate it or physically lift the laptop) — you'll be asked to pan it around the room for the environment check. |
| Microphone & speakers | Both required and working. Wired headphones/headset are typically allowed; Bluetooth or wireless earbuds and headsets are not. |
| Internet | A stable connection — wired Ethernet is more reliable than Wi-Fi. If you must use Wi-Fi, sit close to the router and make sure nobody else in the household is streaming video, gaming, or on a video call during your slot. Rough guide: at least 5 Mbps download / 1 Mbps upload. |
| Security software | You'll likely be asked to temporarily disable antivirus/firewall software and any active VPN for the proctoring app to run. |

## Your Testing Environment

- A **quiet, private room with a door you can close**. Tell anyone you live with which exact window you'll be testing in, so nobody walks in or makes noise.
- You must be **alone** in the room for the whole test — no other people, and typically no pets either.
- Reasonable, even lighting on your face — the proctor needs to see you clearly for the entire session, and a backlit or very dim setup can trigger a flag even when you've done nothing wrong.
- A clear desk: nothing on it beyond what's explicitly allowed (next section) — clutter in the camera's view is one of the more common reasons candidates get an avoidable question from their proctor.

## What You Can (and Cannot) Have With You

**Generally allowed:**
- Your laptop or desktop, with its charger
- A single external monitor (if used, per the screen rule above)
- Mouse, keyboard, mousepad
- Your valid photo ID (passport or national ID card — required, not optional)
- Tissues
- A drink in an unlabelled container (a labelled bottle can look like a place to hide notes, so plain/unlabelled is the safe choice)

**Not allowed on your desk or in the room:**
- Mobile phones (even switched off, even in a drawer within reach — put it in another room)
- A physical calculator, pen, paper, notebooks, or any writing materials
- Books, printed notes, or sticky notes
- Docking stations
- Headphones/earbuds that aren't wired (Bluetooth and wireless are typically rejected)
- Any other person or pet in the room

The two items that trip people up most: **you cannot use a physical calculator or paper**, even for the numerical reasoning test — see below for what you get instead — and **your phone has to leave the room entirely**, not just go face-down on the desk.

## Check-In: ID and Room Scan

Plan to log in **15–20 minutes before** your scheduled start, not exactly on time — the check-in itself takes time, and arriving late for it can cost you test time or, in the worst case, the slot itself.

The check-in typically involves:
1. **Identity verification** — you show your ID to the camera; the name and photo must be on the same side of the document and clearly legible.
2. **A 360° scan of the room** — you'll be asked to pick up your webcam (or rotate your laptop) and slowly show all four walls, the ceiling, the floor, your desk from above and underneath, and the chair.
3. **A scan of yourself** — standing up, turning around, and sometimes showing your sleeves, pockets, and glasses (to confirm you're not hiding a device or notes on your person).

None of this is meant to be intimidating — proctors run through the same checklist hundreds of times a day — but it goes faster, and feels much less stressful, if you've already tidied the room and know what's coming.

## The On-Screen Tools (Not Your Own)

Because physical calculators and scratch paper aren't allowed, the test platform provides its own on-screen equivalents for the numerical reasoning test:

- **An on-screen calculator** — basic arithmetic functions, operated with mouse clicks or your keyboard's number pad. It does the arithmetic; it does *not* do the reasoning. Deciding which numbers to plug in, and in what order, is still entirely on you.
- **An on-screen scratchpad/notepad** — a text box where you can jot intermediate results or short notes, since you can't write on paper.
- **Navigation tools** common across the reasoning and MCQ tests: a progress panel showing answered/unanswered questions, the ability to jump directly to a specific question, and a "flag for review" marker for questions you want to revisit before time runs out.

The single most repeated piece of advice about these tools, across official material and prep sites alike, is the same: **practise with them before test day**, not during it. If the first time you click the on-screen calculator or try to flag a question is in the real exam, you'll burn seconds — sometimes 5–10 per question — just figuring out the interface, on tests where every question is timed individually. Use the official sample tests (linked in Resources) specifically to get comfortable with the toolbar, not just the question content.

## Rules of Conduct During the Test

- Stay inside the camera frame for the entire test — stepping out, even briefly, is treated as a rule violation, not a bathroom-break allowance.
- Don't speak or read questions aloud.
- Don't use any device other than the one you're tested on — no second screen, no phone, no smartwatch.
- Don't leave your seat unless the platform's specific break policy (stated in your invitation) allows it.

## If Something Goes Wrong

Technical failures happen — a dropped connection, a proctor who never joins, a tool on the platform that doesn't work. If a genuine technical or platform problem affects your session, EPSO's post-2024 commitment is that you should be offered the possibility to **redo the test**, and after testing closes you're entitled to receive **your own answers together with the correct answers**. Document the problem as it happens if you can (note the time, what happened, and, if the platform lets you, a screenshot) and raise it with the helpdesk contact in your invitation immediately — not weeks later.

## Practical Tips From Candidates and Prep Sites

None of these are official EPSO rules — they're the recurring, low-effort habits that candidates and EPSO-prep sites (YSE, EU Training, and others) mention often enough that they're worth adopting:

- **Do the compatibility/system check 48 hours ahead**, not the night before, so a driver or permissions issue doesn't become a crisis on test morning.
- **Prefer a wired Ethernet connection** over Wi-Fi if you have the option — it's the single most common cause of avoidable connection drops.
- **Log in 15–20 minutes early.** Check-in (ID + room scan) takes real time, and rushing it raises your stress right before the part of the test that actually counts.
- **Rehearse with the real on-screen tools**, not just practice questions on paper — the calculator, the scratchpad, and the flag/navigate functions all cost time to learn under pressure.
- **Do at least one or two full timed mock sessions** in the days before, at the same time of day as your real slot if possible, so your first experience of the real time pressure isn't on the day that counts.
- **The calculator doesn't do the reasoning for you.** It's easy to lean on it and stop thinking — several prep sources flag this explicitly for numerical reasoning: work out *what* to calculate before you start clicking buttons.
- **Answer every question.** There is no negative marking on the reasoning and MCQ tests, so an educated guess always beats a blank.
- **Use the flag/review marker sparingly** — flag a question only when you have a specific, recoverable doubt, not reflexively every time you feel unsure; flagging everything defeats the purpose of the tool and leaves you with an unmanageable review list at the end.
- **Eliminate obviously wrong options first**, then decide between what's left, rather than searching for "the right answer" cold.
- **Warn the people you live with** about your exact test window, and put a sign on the door — an interruption you could have prevented is the most avoidable way to lose points.
- **Nerves are a bigger risk than the material.** A few slow breaths before you start, and a plan for what to do if you get stuck on one question (skip, flag, move on), matter as much as knowing the content.
